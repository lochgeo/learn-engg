## The lanthanoids: filling the 4f subshell

The lanthanoids are the fourteen elements cerium (Ce) through lutetium (Lu) that follow lanthanum. They are f-block metals in which the $4f$ subshell is progressively filled. Although they are often called "rare earths", most are not particularly rare; the name survives from the difficulty of separating them, because their chemistry is almost identical. That near-identity is itself the central puzzle of this module.

## 1. Position and electronic configuration

The lanthanoids occupy the first row of the f-block, between barium and hafnium in the periodic table. Their general configuration is

$$
[\mathrm{Xe}]\ 4f^{1-14}\ 5d^{0-1}\ 6s^2.
$$

Lanthanum itself is $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,5d^1\,6s^2$ (no 4f electron) and lutetium is $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\,5d^1\,6s^2$. The fourteen lanthanoids proper run Ce ($4f^1$) to Lu ($4f^{14}$). The $4f$ orbitals are buried beneath the $5s$ and $5p$ shells, so they participate little in bonding; chemically the lanthanoids behave as though only the $6s^2$ (and sometimes $5d^1$) electrons are available.

## 2. Oxidation states

The most common and stable oxidation state is **+3**, formed by losing the two $6s$ and one $5d$ (or $4f$) electron. Many lanthanoids also show **+2** and **+4**, but only when the resulting configuration is specially stable:

- **+4** when it gives $4f^0$ (Ce⁴⁺) or $4f^7$ (Tb⁴⁺).
- **+2** when it gives $4f^7$ (Eu²⁺) or $4f^{14}$ (Yb²⁺).

Outside these cases the +3 state dominates so completely that separating the elements by chemical means is hard.

## 3. The lanthanoid contraction

This is the single most important idea in f-block chemistry. As the $4f$ subshell fills from Ce to Lu, the fourteen protons added to the nucleus are very poorly shielded by the $4f$ electrons. The effective nuclear charge therefore rises steadily, and the atomic (and ionic) radius shrinks by roughly $15\ \mathrm{pm}$ across the series:

| Ion | $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ radius (pm) |
| --- | --- |
| La³⁺ | 103 |
| Ce³⁺ | 102 |
| Nd³⁺ | 98 |
| Sm³⁺ | 96 |
| Gd³⁺ | 94 |
| Dy³⁺ | 91 |
| Yb³⁺ | 87 |
| Lu³⁺ | 86 |

Consequences:

1. The 4d and 5d elements of the same group have almost identical radii (Zr ≈ Hf, Nb ≈ Ta), so they occur together and are extremely difficult to separate.
2. The basicity of the $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ aqua ions decreases from La to Lu: the smaller, more highly charged ions hold their hydration shell more tightly and are less basic.
3. The lattice energies of lanthanoid compounds increase across the series.

## 4. Magnetic and spectroscopic properties

Most lanthanoid ions are coloured and paramagnetic. Because the $4f$ electrons are well shielded, the magnetic moment is close to the free-ion value and is not quenched by bonding; the spin-only formula is not adequate and the full $J$-value formula is used. The colour comes from $f$–$f$ transitions, which are sharp because the $4f$ orbitals are inner and barely affected by the ligand field.

## 5. General physical properties

- All are silvery, reactive metals that tarnish in air.
- They react with water (slowly when cold, faster when hot) to give $\mathrm{Ln(OH)_3}$ and hydrogen.
- They form oxides $\mathrm{Ln_2O_3}$ (except Ce, which gives $\mathrm{CeO_2}$) and halides $\mathrm{LnX_3}$.
- Melting points are high; the metals are soft.

## 6. Uses

Lanthanoids are not curiosities; they are industrial workhorses:

- **Misch metal** (an alloy of Ce, La and other lanthanoids) is used in lighter flints and in deoxidising steel.
- **Cerium(IV) oxide** is a glass-polishing abrasive and a catalyst support.
- **Neodymium** makes the strongest permanent magnets (Nd₂Fe₁₄B) used in motors and headphones.
- **Europium** and **terbium** are phosphors in colour television and LED lamps.
- **Lanthanum** is used in camera lenses and in the fluid-cracking catalysts of oil refineries.

## 7. Worked example: identifying a lanthanoid from its radius

**Example.** An unknown $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ ion has radius $91\ \mathrm{pm}$ and is paramagnetic with a magnetic moment close to that of a free ion with 5 unpaired f-electrons. Identify it.

From the table, $91\ \mathrm{pm}$ corresponds to dysprosium ($\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$). Its configuration is $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^9$, which has 5 unpaired electrons in the $4f$ subshell (Hund's rule), consistent with the magnetic data. The radius and the unpaired count agree, so the ion is $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$.

Sanity check: $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$ is indeed one of the coloured, paramagnetic lanthanoid ions used in magnets and phosphors, which matches the physical description.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Lanthanoid mistakes">

- **Calling all lanthanoids +3 only.** Ce, Pr, Tb can be +4 and Eu, Yb can be +2 under the right conditions.
- **Attributing the lanthanoid contraction to poor shielding by d electrons.** It is the $4f$ electrons that shield poorly.
- **Using the spin-only formula for lanthanoid magnetic moments.** The moments are governed by the total angular momentum $J$, not by spin alone.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Which lanthanoid ion is most strongly reducing: $\mathrm{Ce^{3+}}$ or $\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}$? Explain.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}$ is a strong oxidising agent (it wants to become $\mathrm{Ce^{3+}}$ with the stable $4f^1$... actually $4f^0$? Wait: Ce³⁺ is $4f^1$, Ce⁴⁺ is $4f^0$. Ce⁴⁺ gaining an electron reaches the empty $4f^0$? Let's be careful: Ce⁴⁺ ($4f^0$) is stable; Ce³⁺ ($4f^1$) is the common state. Ce⁴⁺ is a strong oxidiser because it readily accepts an electron to become Ce³⁺. So $\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}$ is oxidising, not reducing. The strongly reducing ion among the lanthanoids is $\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}$ (and $\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}$), which readily loses an electron to reach the stable +3 state.

</details>

**B.** Why are Zr and Hf found together in nature and so hard to separate?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The lanthanoid contraction makes Hf (5d) almost the same size as Zr (4d), so their compounds have nearly identical solubilities and crystallise together. Separation requires ion-exchange or solvent-extraction methods.

</details>

**C.** Arrange $\mathrm{La^{3+}}$, $\mathrm{Gd^{3+}}$ and $\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}$ in order of increasing basicity.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Basicity decreases as radius decreases across the series: $\mathrm{Lu^{3+}} < \mathrm{Gd^{3+}} < \mathrm{La^{3+}}$. The largest ion, $\mathrm{La^{3+}}$, is the most basic.

</details>

## 8. Separation of lanthanoids

Because the lanthanoids have nearly identical chemistry, classical precipitation does not separate them. Modern separation uses **ion-exchange chromatography** or **solvent extraction**, exploiting the small differences in stability constants that arise from the lanthanoid contraction. The contraction is small, but it is large enough to give slightly different complex stabilities, which ion-exchange resins amplify into clean separation.

## Try it yourself (extra)

**D.** Why is promethium the only radioactive lanthanoid, and why is it not found in nature in appreciable amounts?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Promethium has no stable isotope; its longest-lived isotope has a half-life of about 17.7 years, so any primordial promethium has long since decayed. It is produced artificially in nuclear reactors.

</details>

## 9. Spectra and colour

Lanthanoid ions give sharp absorption bands because the $4f$ orbitals are inner and only weakly perturbed by the ligand field. The colours are characteristic: $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ is lilac, $\mathrm{Pr^{3+}}$ green, $\mathrm{Er^{3+}}$ pink. This sharpness is exploited in lasers (Nd:YAG) and in phosphors.

## Try it yourself (extra)

**E.** Which lanthanoid is used in the most common solid-state laser, and in what host crystal?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Neodymium in a yttrium aluminium garnet host: Nd:YAG, emitting at 1064 nm.

</details>

## Remember

- Lanthanoids = Ce–Lu; general configuration $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{1-14}\,5d^{0-1}\,6s^2$.
- The dominant oxidation state is +3; +2 and +4 occur only at special configurations ($4f^0$, $4f^7$, $4f^{14}$).
- The lanthanoid contraction (~15 pm) arises from poor 4f shielding and shrinks radii across the series.
- Contraction makes 4d and 5d elements (Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta) almost identical in size.
- Basicity of $\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}$ decreases from La to Lu.
- Lanthanoids are coloured, paramagnetic, and industrially vital (magnets, phosphors, catalysts).
