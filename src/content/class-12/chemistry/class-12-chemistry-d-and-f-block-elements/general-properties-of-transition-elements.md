## The d-block is a family of metals with a twist

Transition metals are metals, but not "ordinary" ones. Their atoms differ from main-group metals in one crucial way: the $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ subshells lie close in energy, so electrons can be removed from either. That produces a distinctive cluster of properties — several oxidation states, coloured ions, magnetism, catalytic activity — that no other block of the periodic table shows in the same combination. This module surveys those properties and the physical reasons behind them.

## 1. Metallic character and physical constants

All d-block elements are metals. They have high electrical and thermal conductivity, metallic lustre and malleability. Their melting and boiling points are generally high because the metallic bond is strengthened by the involvement of both $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ electrons in bonding. The 4d and 5d metals have the highest melting points in the periodic table (W, 3422 °C; Re, 3186 °C).

Two important exceptions break the trend:

- **Zinc, cadmium and mercury** have much lower melting points (Hg is liquid at room temperature) because their $d^{10}$ subshell does not participate in bonding; only the two $s$ electrons bond, giving weaker metallic bonding.
- The **first (3d) series** has lower melting points than the 4d/5d series because the 3d orbitals are more contracted and overlap less effectively.

Densities are high and rise across a series, peaking near the middle (osmium and iridium are among the densest elements known).

## 2. Atomic and ionic radii

Across a transition series the atomic radius changes only slightly — it decreases slowly and then levels off. The reason is that added electrons enter the inner $(n-1)d$ subshell, which shields the outer $ns$ electrons poorly, but the added nuclear charge is also partly screened. The net effect is a nearly constant radius.

The important consequence is the **lanthanoid contraction's preview**: the 4d and 5d elements have very similar radii because the poor shielding by 4f electrons (in the lanthanoids that precede the 5d series) raises the effective nuclear charge on the 5d elements. Zr and Hf, for example, are almost identical in size.

Ionic radii follow the same pattern: $\mathrm{M^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{M^{3+}}$ radii decrease slowly across a series.

## 3. Ionisation enthalpy

First ionisation enthalpies of transition metals are higher than those of the s-block metals of the same period, but the increase across a series is irregular. Removing an electron from a $d^5$ or $d^{10}$ configuration costs extra energy (Mn and Zn show local maxima), and the second and third ionisation enthalpies are comparatively low once the $s$ electron is gone, which is why +2 and +3 states are common.

## 4. Oxidation states (overview)

Transition metals show a wide range of oxidation states, from +1 to +7 in manganese. The maximum oxidation state generally equals the number of $ns + (n-1)d$ electrons available, and it peaks in the middle of a series (Mn +7, Cr +6). Higher states are more common in the lighter (3d) metals; the heavier metals prefer lower states because the $d$ electrons become more tightly bound. This is treated in detail in module 3.

## 5. Magnetic properties

A transition-metal ion is **paramagnetic** if it has unpaired d electrons and **diamagnetic** if all are paired. The spin-only magnetic moment is

$$
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \mathrm{BM},
$$

where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons and BM is the Bohr magneton. Orbital contributions can raise the value, but the spin-only formula is the standard first estimate for 3d ions.

**Worked example.** How many unpaired electrons are in $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$, and what are their spin-only moments?

- $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6$ → 4 unpaired → $\mu = \sqrt{4(6)} = \sqrt{24} = 4.90\ \mathrm{BM}$.
- $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5$ → 5 unpaired → $\mu = \sqrt{5(7)} = \sqrt{35} = 5.92\ \mathrm{BM}$.

Sanity check: $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ has more unpaired electrons than $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and therefore a larger moment, which matches experiment.

## 6. Colour

Most transition-metal compounds are coloured. The colour arises from **d–d transitions**: in a ligand field the five d orbitals split into groups of different energy, and an electron can be promoted between them by absorbing visible light. The observed colour is the complement of the absorbed wavelength. Ions with $d^0$ or $d^{10}$ configurations (Sc³⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu⁺) are usually colourless because no d–d transition is possible.

## 7. Catalytic activity

Transition metals and their compounds are excellent catalysts. The reasons are:

- variable oxidation states let the metal accept and donate electrons during a reaction;
- the ability to adsorb reactants onto the surface (heterogeneous catalysis);
- the ability to form temporary complexes with reactants (homogeneous catalysis).

Examples: Fe in the Haber process, V₂O₅ in the contact process, Ni in hydrogenation, Pt in catalytic converters.

## 8. Complex formation

Transition metals form a vast number of coordination complexes. The reasons are the small size and high charge of the ions, the availability of empty d orbitals to accept electron pairs from ligands, and the ability to show multiple oxidation states. This is the subject of the next chapter (Coordination Compounds) and is only flagged here.

## 9. Alloy formation and interstitial compounds

Because transition metals have similar atomic radii, they form alloys readily (brass, bronze, steel). They also form **interstitial compounds** when small atoms (H, B, C, N) occupy the holes in the metal lattice; these are very hard, have high melting points and are chemically inert (steel, tungsten carbide).

## 10. Non-stoichiometric compounds

Transition metals form oxides and sulphides whose compositions are not fixed (Fe₀.₉₅O, Cu₂₋ₓS). The variable oxidation state of the metal makes this possible; main-group compounds are usually stoichiometric.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Property mistakes">

- **Assuming every transition metal has a high melting point.** Zn, Cd and Hg are the clear exceptions.
- **Using the spin-only formula for 4d/5d or lanthanoid ions** and expecting agreement. Orbital contributions dominate there; the formula is a 3d first estimate.
- **Confusing colour with the metal's own colour.** The colour belongs to the ion in a particular ligand environment, not to the element.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ has configuration $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5$. Compute its spin-only magnetic moment.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$n = 5$, so $\mu = \sqrt{5(5+2)} = \sqrt{35} = 5.92\ \mathrm{BM}$.

</details>

**B.** Which ion is colourless: $\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}$ or $\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}$? Why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}$ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^0$ — no d electrons, so no d–d transition; it is colourless. $\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}$ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^1$ and shows a d–d transition, so it is coloured (purple).

</details>

**C.** Explain why Zr and Hf have almost identical atomic radii despite being in different periods.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The lanthanoids (Ce–Lu) precede Hf and fill the 4f subshell. The 4f electrons shield the 5d electrons poorly, so the effective nuclear charge on Hf is much larger than a simple period trend would suggest, shrinking its radius to match Zr's. This is the lanthanoid contraction.

</details>

## 11. Enthalpy of atomisation

The enthalpy of atomisation of a transition metal is high and peaks near the middle of a series. The reason is that both the $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ electrons contribute to metallic bonding, and the number of unpaired electrons available for bonding is greatest near the centre. This is why W and Re have the highest melting points in the periodic table, while Zn, Cd and Hg (with no unpaired d electrons available) have the lowest.

## 12. Standard electrode potentials

The standard reduction potentials $E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}/M})$ are not smooth across a series. Manganese has a very negative $E^\circ$ (−1.18 V) because $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ has the stable half-filled $3d^5$ configuration, making Mn hard to oxidise further and its metal a strong reducing agent. Zinc has a negative $E^\circ$ (−0.76 V) despite being $d^{10}$, because its ion is particularly stable in solution.

## Try it yourself (extra)

**D.** Which metal in the 3d series has the highest enthalpy of atomisation, and why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Tungsten is 5d, not 3d; within the 3d series the maximum occurs near chromium/vanadium where the most unpaired electrons are available for metallic bonding. (Any well-reasoned answer naming the mid-series peak is acceptable.)

</details>

## Remember

- Transition metals are hard, dense, high-melting metals — except Zn, Cd, Hg.
- Radii change little across a series; the lanthanoid contraction makes 4d and 5d elements similar in size.
- Variable oxidation states peak in the middle of a series.
- Paramagnetism follows $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \mathrm{BM}$ for 3d ions.
- Colour comes from d–d transitions; $d^0$ and $d^{10}$ ions are colourless.
- Catalysis, complex formation, alloying and interstitial compounds all stem from the partially filled d subshell.
