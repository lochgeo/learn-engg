## A partially filled d shell changes the whole periodic table

The d- and f-block elements are the "middle" of the periodic table. Their chemistry is governed not by the outermost s or p electrons alone, but by an **inner subshell** that is only partly filled. That single feature — a partially filled d or f subshell — produces the properties we associate with transition metals: variable oxidation states, coloured ions, magnetism, and catalytic activity. This module fixes where these elements sit and how their electrons are arranged; every later module builds on that arrangement.

## 1. Where the blocks sit

The periodic table is built by filling subshells in order of increasing energy. The s-block (groups 1–2) and p-block (groups 13–18) form the main body. Between them sit the **d-block elements**, groups 3 to 12, in which the $(n-1)d$ subshell is filled. Below the main table sit the **f-block elements**, the lanthanoids and actinoids, in which the $(n-2)f$ subshell is filled.

- **d-block**: 3d series Sc–Zn, 4d series Y–Cd, 5d series La–Hg (with Lu/Hf complications), and the 6d series beginning with Ac.
- **f-block**: lanthanoids La–Lu (or Ce–Lu, depending on convention) and actinoids Ac–Lr.

An element is a **transition element** only if it has an **incompletely filled d subshell in its ground state or in one of its common oxidation states**. Zinc, cadmium and mercury have a filled $d^{10}$ configuration in both the atom and their common +2 ions, so they are technically not transition elements, though they are often grouped with the d-block.

## 2. The general electronic configuration

For a d-block element of period $n$,

$$
\text{configuration} = (\text{noble gas})\ (n-1)d^{1-10}\ ns^{0-2}.
$$

The two subshells $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ are close in energy, so electrons can be promoted between them. This is why configurations are not always the simple "fill s, then d" picture.

The first transition series (3d) is the one most often quoted:

| Element | Z | Ground-state configuration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sc | 21 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^1\,4s^2$ |
| Ti | 22 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^2\,4s^2$ |
| V | 23 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^3\,4s^2$ |
| Cr | 24 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^5\,4s^1$ |
| Mn | 25 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^5\,4s^2$ |
| Fe | 26 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^6\,4s^2$ |
| Co | 27 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^7\,4s^2$ |
| Ni | 28 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^8\,4s^2$ |
| Cu | 29 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^{10}\,4s^1$ |
| Zn | 30 | $[\mathrm{Ar}]\ 3d^{10}\,4s^2$ |

Two exceptions stand out. **Chromium** takes $3d^5\,4s^1$ instead of $3d^4\,4s^2$ because a half-filled d subshell gains extra stability from exchange energy. **Copper** takes $3d^{10}\,4s^1$ instead of $3d^9\,4s^2$ because a completely filled d subshell is similarly stabilised. The 4d and 5d series follow the same pattern with analogous exceptions (Mo, Ag).

## 3. The f-block configurations

For the lanthanoids, the general configuration is

$$
[\mathrm{Xe}]\ 4f^{1-14}\ 5d^{0-1}\ 6s^2,
$$

and for the actinoids

$$
[\mathrm{Rn}]\ 5f^{1-14}\ 6d^{0-1}\ 7s^2.
$$

In both series the $f$ subshell is being filled, but the outer $s$ and sometimes one $d$ electron are also present. The lanthanoids are usually taken as La (5d¹ 6s², with no 4f electron) through Lu (4f¹⁴ 5d¹ 6s²); the actinoids run Ac (6d¹ 7s²) through Lr (5f¹⁴ 6d¹ 7s²). The $f$ orbitals are buried inside the atom, so f-electrons shield poorly and their chemical consequences (contraction, magnetism) are indirect.

## 4. Why the configuration matters

Three consequences follow directly from a partially filled d or f subshell:

1. **Variable oxidation states** — the $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ electrons have similar energies, so different numbers of them can be removed. This is developed in module 3.
2. **Paramagnetism** — unpaired d or f electrons give a magnetic moment $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \mathrm{BM}$, where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons.
3. **Colour** — d–d transitions in partially filled subshells absorb visible light. Ions with $d^0$ or $d^{10}$ configurations are usually colourless.

## 5. Worked example: assigning configurations

**Example.** Write the ground-state configurations of (a) $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$, (b) $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$, (c) gadolinium (Gd, Z = 64).

- (a) Cr is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^1$. Removing three electrons takes the 4s electron first, then two 3d electrons: $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^3$.
- (b) Cu is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}\,4s^1$. Removing two electrons gives $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^9$.
- (c) Gd is a lanthanoid: $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7\,5d^1\,6s^2$. The $4f^7$ half-filled shell is particularly stable.

Sanity check: in every ion the 4s/6s electrons are removed before the $(n-1)d$/$4f$ electrons, because the s subshell lies higher in energy once the atom is ionised. This ordering rule is the one most often forgotten.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Configuration errors">

- **Removing d electrons before s electrons when forming cations.** The ns electrons are always lost first.
- **Treating Zn, Cd and Hg as transition elements.** Their d subshells are full in both atom and common ion.
- **Forgetting the Cr and Cu exceptions** and writing $3d^4\,4s^2$ or $3d^9\,4s^2$.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Write the ground-state configuration of $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and count its unpaired electrons.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Fe is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6\,4s^2$. Removing two 4s electrons gives $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6$, with **4 unpaired electrons** (high-spin octahedral; the free ion has 4 unpaired).

</details>

**B.** Predict whether $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$ is paramagnetic or diamagnetic, and explain.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{Zn^{2+}} = [\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}$, a filled subshell with no unpaired electrons, so it is **diamagnetic**.

</details>

**C.** Why is the 4f subshell filled *after* 6s but *before* 5d in the lanthanoids?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The 6s orbital is lowest in energy initially and fills first. Once 6s is occupied, the 4f level drops below 5d in energy for the lanthanoids, so 4f fills next, with an occasional 5d electron when that lowers the total energy (La, Gd, Lu).

</details>

### Example 2: an ion in a high oxidation state

$\mathrm{Mn^{4+}}$: Mn is $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5\,4s^2$. Removing four electrons (two 4s, then two 3d) gives $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^3$, with 3 unpaired electrons. This is the state found in $\mathrm{MnO_2}$.

## Remember

- d-block = groups 3–12, filling $(n-1)d$; f-block = lanthanoids and actinoids, filling $(n-2)f$.
- General d-block configuration: $(n-1)d^{1-10}\,ns^{0-2}$.
- Cr and Cu are the two 3d exceptions: half-filled and fully filled d subshells.
- A transition element needs an incompletely filled d subshell in the atom or a common ion.
- Remove ns electrons before $(n-1)d$ electrons when forming cations.
