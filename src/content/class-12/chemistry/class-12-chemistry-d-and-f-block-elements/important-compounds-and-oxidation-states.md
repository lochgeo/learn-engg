## Oxidation states are the bookkeeping of d-block chemistry

Because the $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ electrons of a transition metal have similar energies, a transition metal can lose different numbers of electrons and exist in several oxidation states. The state it actually adopts depends on the ligand, the pH, and the oxidising or reducing conditions. This module fixes the oxidation-state patterns for the key metals (Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu) and then studies the two compounds that every board and JEE paper expects: potassium dichromate and potassium permanganate.

## 1. Oxidation-state patterns

The maximum oxidation state a metal can reach equals the number of electrons in its $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ subshells combined. It rises to a maximum near the middle of a series and then falls.

| Metal | Common oxidation states | Maximum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sc | +3 | +3 |
| Ti | +2, +3, +4 | +4 |
| V | +2, +3, +4, +5 | +5 |
| Cr | +2, +3, +6 | +6 |
| Mn | +2, +3, +4, +6, +7 | +7 |
| Fe | +2, +3 | +6 (rare) |
| Co | +2, +3 | +3 |
| Ni | +2 | +4 (rare) |
| Cu | +1, +2 | +2 |
| Zn | +2 | +2 |

Three trends are worth remembering:

1. **The +2 state is common** across the series because the two $ns$ electrons are the easiest to remove.
2. **Higher states are more stable for the lighter metals** of a group; for the heavier metals the $d$ electrons are more tightly bound, so lower states dominate (e.g., Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ are common; Ru and Os prefer +4 to +8).
3. **The highest states need strongly oxidising conditions** and are usually found in oxoanions (CrO₄²⁻, MnO₄⁻), not in simple salts.

## 2. Calculating an oxidation state

The oxidation state is found by enforcing charge balance on the formula, treating O as −2 and H (in acids) as +1.

**Example 1.** Chromium in $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$:

$$
2(+1) + 2x + 7(-2) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 2 + 2x - 14 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = +6.
$$

**Example 2.** Manganese in $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$:

$$
1(+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 + x - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = +7.
$$

**Example 3.** Iron in $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$:

$$
3x + 4(-2) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = +\frac{8}{3},
$$

which is not an integer because $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$ contains both Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ in a 1:2 ratio. A fractional oxidation state is a signal of mixed valence.

## 3. Potassium dichromate, $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$

**Preparation.** Chromite ore ($\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}$) is fused with sodium carbonate and air, giving sodium chromate; acidification converts chromate to dichromate; treatment with KCl precipitates $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$.

$$
\mathrm{2CrO_4^{2-} + 2H^+ \rightleftharpoons Cr_2O_7^{2-} + H_2O}
$$

Chromate (yellow) and dichromate (orange) interconvert with pH: dichromate dominates in acid, chromate in base.

**Properties.** Orange crystals, soluble in water, strong oxidising agent in acidic medium. The dichromate ion is a powerful oxidiser:

$$
\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-} + 14H^+ + 6e^- \rightarrow 2Cr^{3+} + 7H_2O}, \quad E^\circ = +1.33\ \mathrm{V}.
$$

**Uses.** Leather tanning, dyeing, preparation of chromic acid, volumetric analysis.

## 4. Potassium permanganate, $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$

**Preparation.** Pyrolusite ($\mathrm{MnO_2}$) is fused with KOH and an oxidiser (KNO₃ or air) to give potassium manganate ($\mathrm{K_2MnO_4}$); disproportionation in acid or neutral solution gives permanganate.

$$
\mathrm{3MnO_4^{2-} + 4H^+ \rightarrow 2MnO_4^- + MnO_2 + 2H_2O}
$$

**Properties.** Deep purple crystals; in solution the $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ ion is an intense purple. It is a very strong oxidiser; the reduction product depends on pH:

- **Acidic medium:** $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O}$, $E^\circ = +1.51\ \mathrm{V}$ (colourless $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$).
- **Neutral/weakly acidic:** $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 2H_2O + 3e^- \rightarrow MnO_2 + 4OH^-}$ (brown precipitate).
- **Strongly alkaline:** $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + e^- \rightarrow MnO_4^{2-}}$ (green manganate).

**Uses.** Volumetric analysis (self-indicating), disinfectant, oxidising agent in organic synthesis, water treatment.

## 5. A redox equivalence: dichromate versus permanganate

Both are used as titrants. The number of electrons gained per formula unit is 6 for dichromate and 5 for permanganate in acid. The equivalent mass is therefore $M/6$ for $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$ and $M/5$ for $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$. This is the standard numerical pattern tested in titration problems.

**Worked example.** A sample of $\mathrm{FeSO_4}$ is titrated with $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ in acid. The reaction is

$$
\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 5Fe^{2+} + 8H^+ \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 5Fe^{3+} + 4H_2O}.
$$

If $20.0\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of $0.0200\ \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ is used, how many moles of $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ are present?

$$
n(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.0200 = 4.00\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol}.
$$

From the stoichiometry, $n(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}) = 5 \times 4.00\times10^{-4} = 2.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}$.

Sanity check: permanganate is the stronger oxidiser on paper ($E^\circ$ 1.51 V vs 1.33 V), but dichromate is preferred when the solution must stay clear (no $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ precipitate) and when a primary standard is needed, because $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$ is more stable and easier to dry.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Compound and oxidation-state mistakes">

- **Using the alkaline reduction product of permanganate in an acidic titration.** In acid, $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ becomes $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ (5 electrons); in neutral/alkaline it becomes $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ (3 electrons). The stoichiometry changes.
- **Forgetting that chromate and dichromate are pH-dependent.** Acid favours orange dichromate; base favours yellow chromate.
- **Treating a mixed-valence oxide such as $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$ as having a single oxidation state.** The fractional value signals mixed valence.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** What is the oxidation state of vanadium in $\mathrm{V_2O_5}$?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$2x + 5(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = +5$. Vanadium reaches its maximum oxidation state here.

</details>

**B.** In a titration, $25.0\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of $0.100\ \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$ is used. How many moles of $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ does it oxidise in acid?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$n(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}) = 0.100 \times 0.0250 = 2.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}$. Each dichromate takes 6 electrons and each $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ gives 1, so $n(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}) = 6 \times 2.50\times10^{-3} = 1.50\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{mol}$.

</details>

**C.** Why does $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ act as its own indicator in a titration?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ is intensely purple while the product $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ is colourless. The first drop of excess permanganate after the equivalence point gives a permanent pale pink colour, so no separate indicator is needed.

</details>

## 6. Other compounds worth knowing

- **Potassium chromate, $\mathrm{K_2CrO_4}$** — yellow, the form stable in alkaline solution; used as an indicator in precipitation titrations (Mohr's method).
- **Chromium(III) oxide, $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$** — green pigment, amphoteric oxide.
- **Manganese(IV) oxide, $\mathrm{MnO_2}$** — black solid, used in dry cells and as an oxidising agent in neutral medium.
- **Iron oxides** — $\mathrm{FeO}$ (non-stoichiometric), $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$ (rust), $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$ (magnetite, mixed valence).

## Try it yourself (extra)

**D.** A $0.200\ \mathrm{g}$ sample containing $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ is titrated with $0.0200\ \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$; $18.0\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ is required. Find the percentage of $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ in the sample (molar mass of Fe = $55.8\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$).

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$n(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.0180 = 3.60\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol}$. $n(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}) = 5 \times 3.60\times10^{-4} = 1.80\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}$. Mass of Fe = $1.80\times10^{-3} \times 55.8 = 0.100\ \mathrm{g}$. Percentage = $(0.100/0.200)\times100 = 50.0\%$.

</details>

## Remember

- Oxidation states are found by charge balance; O is −2, H is +1.
- The maximum state equals $ns + (n-1)d$ electrons and peaks mid-series.
- Chromate (yellow) ⇌ dichromate (orange) is pH-dependent.
- In acid: $\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}$ → $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ (6 e⁻); $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ → $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ (5 e⁻).
- The reduction product of permanganate depends on pH; get this wrong and the stoichiometry is wrong.
- Fractional oxidation states signal mixed valence.
