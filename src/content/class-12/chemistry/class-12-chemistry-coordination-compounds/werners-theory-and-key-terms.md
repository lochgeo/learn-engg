## Why coordination compounds needed a new theory

Before 1893, compounds such as $\mathrm{CoCl_3\cdot 6NH_3}$ were treated as simple double salts. Alfred Werner showed that the ammonia molecules are not merely mixed in; they are bound directly to the cobalt ion in a fixed, directional arrangement. That insight created coordination chemistry. The central idea is that a metal ion can hold ligands in a definite geometry, and that some of the attached groups can be removed by a solvent while others cannot.

## 1. Werner’s primary and secondary valences

Werner proposed that a metal exhibits two kinds of valence:

- **Primary valence** = the oxidation state of the metal. It is satisfied by anions and is usually ionisable; these ions can be precipitated by reagents such as $\mathrm{AgNO_3}$.
- **Secondary valence** = the coordination number. It is satisfied by neutral molecules or anions (ligands) that are directly attached to the metal and are not ionisable under ordinary conditions.

The secondary valence is directional and determines the geometry of the complex. Consider the cobalt(III) ammine chlorides:

| Formula written as hydrate | Structural formula | Ionisable chloride |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CoCl_3\cdot 6NH_3}$ | $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_6}]\mathrm{Cl_3}$ | 3 |
| $\mathrm{CoCl_3\cdot 5NH_3}$ | $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_5Cl}]\mathrm{Cl_2}$ | 2 |
| $\mathrm{CoCl_3\cdot 4NH_3}$ | $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2}]\mathrm{Cl}$ | 1 |
| $\mathrm{CoCl_3\cdot 3NH_3}$ | $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_3Cl_3}]$ | 0 |

The number of ionisable chlorides falls as more chlorides move into the coordination sphere. This single table is the experimental backbone of Werner’s theory: the metal keeps a fixed coordination number (6 for Co(III)) and the geometry is octahedral.

## 2. The coordination entity and its parts

A **coordination entity** (or complex) is the central metal atom or ion together with its attached ligands, enclosed in square brackets. Everything inside the brackets is the **coordination sphere**; species outside are **counter ions**.

- **Central metal atom/ion**: the atom or ion that accepts electron pairs. It is usually a transition metal, but not always (e.g. $\mathrm{[AlF_6]^{3-}}$).
- **Ligand**: a molecule or ion that donates a lone pair to the metal. Ligands are classified by how many donor atoms they use:
  - *unidentate*: one donor atom ($\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{CN^-}$);
  - *bidentate*: two donor atoms (ethane-1,2-diamine, $\mathrm{en}$; oxalate, $\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}$);
  - *polydentate*: more than two (EDTA uses six).
- **Chelating ligand**: a polydentate ligand that forms a ring with the metal. Chelates are unusually stable (the chelate effect).
- **Ambidentate ligand**: can bind through either of two donor atoms ($\mathrm{NO_2^-}$ through N or O; $\mathrm{SCN^-}$ through S or N).

## 3. Coordination number and oxidation number

The **coordination number (CN)** is the number of sigma bonds between the metal and ligands. For unidentate ligands it equals the number of ligands; for bidentate ligands it is twice the number of ligands. Common values are 2, 4 and 6, giving linear, tetrahedral/square-planar and octahedral geometries respectively.

The **oxidation number** of the central metal is found by charge balance: the sum of the metal’s oxidation number and the charges of all ligands equals the charge of the complex.

Worked example: in $[\mathrm{Fe(CN)_6}]^{3-}$,

$$
x + 6(-1) = -3 \;\Rightarrow\; x = +3.
$$

So iron is in the +3 oxidation state. Note that CN = 6 here, while the oxidation number is +3; these are different quantities and are often confused.

## 4. Homoleptic and heteroleptic complexes

- **Homoleptic**: all ligands are identical, e.g. $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_6}]^{3+}$, $[\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}]$.
- **Heteroleptic**: more than one kind of ligand, e.g. $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2}]^+$.

Heteroleptic complexes are where isomerism becomes important (module 3).

## 5. Effective atomic number (EAN)

Sidgwick’s EAN rule suggests that a stable complex often has the metal’s effective atomic number equal to that of the next noble gas. The EAN is

$$
\mathrm{EAN} = Z - (\text{oxidation number}) + (\text{electrons donated by ligands}),
$$

where $Z$ is the atomic number of the metal.

Worked example: in $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_6}]^{3+}$, cobalt has $Z=27$ and oxidation number +3, so the metal contributes $27-3=24$ electrons. Six $\mathrm{NH_3}$ ligands donate $6\times 2 = 12$ electrons. Hence $\mathrm{EAN}=24+12=36$, the atomic number of krypton. The rule is a useful guide, not a law: many stable complexes do not satisfy it, and it says nothing about geometry or colour.

## 6. A reliable method for a new complex

1. Identify the central metal and its oxidation number by charge balance.
2. Count the coordination number from the ligands, remembering denticity.
3. Decide whether the complex is homoleptic or heteroleptic.
4. Use the CN to guess the geometry (2 linear, 4 tetrahedral/square planar, 6 octahedral).
5. If asked, compute the EAN and compare with the nearest noble gas.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Werner-theory errors">
- **Counting ligands instead of donor atoms.** A bidentate ligand such as en contributes 2 to the coordination number, not 1.
- **Confusing primary and secondary valences.** The oxidation state and the coordination number are different; $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_6}]^{3+}$ has CN 6 but Co is +3.
- **Treating the EAN rule as a law.** It predicts stability for many complexes but fails for others and says nothing about geometry or colour.
</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Find the oxidation number of platinum in $[\mathrm{PtCl_6}]^{2-}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Let $x$ be the oxidation number. Then $x + 6(-1) = -2$, so $x = +4$. Platinum is Pt(IV).

</details>

**B.** Compute the EAN of iron in $[\mathrm{Fe(CN)_6}]^{4-}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Iron has $Z=26$. The charge balance gives $x + 6(-1) = -4$, so $x = +2$. The metal contributes $26-2=24$ electrons; six cyanide ligands donate $6\times 2 = 12$. Thus $\mathrm{EAN}=24+12=36$, again krypton.

</details>

**C.** A complex has formula $[\mathrm{Co(en)_2Cl_2}]^+$. What is its coordination number?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Each ethane-1,2-diamine (en) is bidentate, contributing 2 donor atoms, so two en ligands give 4. The two chloride ligands are unidentate, giving 2 more. Hence $\mathrm{CN}=6$ and the geometry is octahedral.

</details>

## Check your understanding

Without calculating a number: why does moving a chloride from outside the brackets into the coordination sphere reduce the number of ionisable chlorides in Werner’s series?

<details><summary>Reveal</summary>

A chloride inside the brackets is covalently coordinated to the metal and is no longer a free ion in solution. Only the chlorides outside the brackets are counter ions and can be precipitated. Thus each chloride that enters the sphere removes one ionisable chloride.

</details>

## Remember

- Primary valence = oxidation state (ionisable); secondary valence = coordination number (directional, non-ionisable).
- The coordination sphere is everything inside the square brackets; counter ions lie outside it.
- Coordination number counts sigma bonds, not ligands, and is not the oxidation number; chelating and ambidentate ligands create the subtlest cases.
- The EAN rule is a guide to stability, not a predictor of geometry or colour.
