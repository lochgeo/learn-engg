## Carbon monoxide as a ligand

Carbon monoxide is a neutral, strong-field ligand that binds to metals through both sigma donation and pi back-donation. The resulting **metal carbonyls** are the simplest organometallic compounds and are central to industrial catalysis. This module also surveys the wider uses of coordination compounds in biology, medicine, analysis and metallurgy.

## 1. Metal carbonyls

A metal carbonyl is a compound in which CO is bound to a metal. They may be:

- **Homoleptic**: only CO ligands, e.g. $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$, $\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}$, $\mathrm{Cr(CO)_6}$.
- **Heteroleptic**: CO plus other ligands, e.g. $\mathrm{HMn(CO)_5}$, $\mathrm{Co(CO)_3NO}$.

Preparation:

- Direct combination: $\mathrm{Ni + 4CO \rightarrow Ni(CO)_4}$ (Mond process conditions).
- Reductive carbonylation: a metal salt is reduced in the presence of CO, e.g. $\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}$ from iron(II) salts with CO and a reducing agent.

### Bonding in metal carbonyls: synergic effect

CO donates electron density from its carbon lone pair into an empty metal orbital (sigma donation), while the metal donates electron density from a filled d orbital into the empty $\pi^*$ antibonding orbital of CO (pi back-donation). The two processes reinforce each other:

- Back-donation weakens the C–O bond (lower stretching frequency in IR) and strengthens the M–C bond.
- This is why CO binds more strongly to metals in low oxidation states with electron-rich d orbitals.

The C–O stretching frequency in the IR spectrum is a direct probe of back-donation: more back-donation → lower wavenumber.

### The 18-electron rule

Stable metal carbonyls usually obey the **18-electron rule**: the metal’s valence electrons plus the electrons donated by ligands (2 per CO) total 18, matching the next noble-gas configuration.

| Carbonyl | Metal valence e⁻ | CO donors | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$ | 10 | $4\times 2=8$ | 18 |
| $\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}$ | 8 | $5\times 2=10$ | 18 |
| $\mathrm{Cr(CO)_6}$ | 6 | $6\times 2=12$ | 18 |
| $\mathrm{Mn_2(CO)_{10}}$ | $2\times 7=14$ | $10\times 2=20$ | $14+20+2=36$, so 18 per Mn (the Mn–Mn bond supplies one electron to each metal) |

Binuclear carbonyls often share an electron through a metal–metal bond to reach 18.

## 2. Applications of coordination compounds

### Biology

- **Haemoglobin**: $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ in a porphyrin ring binds $\mathrm{O_2}$ reversibly; CO competes and causes poisoning.
- **Chlorophyll**: $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ in a porphyrin-like ring captures light.
- **Vitamin B₁₂**: cobalt in a corrin ring.
- **Carboxypeptidase**: zinc enzyme for protein digestion.

### Medicine

- **Cisplatin**, cis-$[\mathrm{Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2}]$, is an anticancer drug that cross-links DNA.
- **Sodium nitroprusside**, $\mathrm{Na_2[Fe(CN)_5NO]}$, is a vasodilator.
- **EDTA** chelation therapy removes heavy metals such as lead from the body.

### Analysis

- **EDTA titrations** determine water hardness and metal-ion concentrations.
- **Qualitative analysis** uses coordination reactions: $\mathrm{[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}}$ (deep blue) confirms copper; $\mathrm{[Fe(SCN)]^{2+}}$ (blood red) confirms iron(III); $\mathrm{[Ag(CN)_2]^-}$ chemistry is used in silver estimation.
- **Masking agents** hide interfering ions during titrations.

### Industry and catalysis

- **Hydroformylation** (oxo process) uses cobalt or rhodium carbonyl catalysts to convert alkenes to aldehydes.
- **Monsanto acetic acid process** uses a rhodium carbonyl/iodide catalyst.
- **Wilkinson’s catalyst**, $\mathrm{RhCl(PPh_3)_3}$, hydrogenates alkenes.
- **Ziegler–Natta catalysts** polymerise alkenes.

### Metallurgy

- **Mond process**: impure nickel is converted to $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$, which decomposes to pure nickel at higher temperature.
- **Cyanide process**: gold and silver are leached as $[\mathrm{Au(CN)_2}]^-$ and $[\mathrm{Ag(CN)_2}]^-$ and then recovered by zinc reduction.

## 3. A reliable problem method

1. For a carbonyl, count valence electrons and check the 18-electron rule; if short, look for a metal–metal bond.
2. For applications, identify the metal, its oxidation state and the ligand’s role (chelation, colour, catalysis).
3. Remember that CO is a **strong-field** ligand, not a weak one, and that back-donation lowers the C–O stretching frequency.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Carbonyl and application errors">
- **Treating CO as a weak-field ligand.** It is one of the strongest field ligands and sits at the strong end of the spectrochemical series.
- **Counting only metal valence electrons for the 18-electron rule.** Each CO donates 2 electrons; the ligand contribution must be included.
- **Ignoring back-donation.** Without it, the M–C bond would be weak and the C–O frequency would not shift; the synergic effect explains both.
- **Confusing cisplatin (cis) with its trans isomer.** Only the cis isomer is an effective anticancer drug.
</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Verify the 18-electron rule for $\mathrm{Cr(CO)_6}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Cr(0) contributes 6 valence electrons; six CO ligands contribute $6\times 2 = 12$. Total = 18, so the complex is stable and diamagnetic.

</details>

**B.** Why does free CO have a higher C–O stretching frequency than CO in $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

In the complex, metal-to-CO back-donation populates the $\pi^*$ antibonding orbital of CO, weakening and lengthening the C–O bond. A weaker bond vibrates at a lower frequency.

</details>

**C.** Which coordination compound is used to refine nickel industrially?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Nickel tetracarbonyl, $\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}$, in the Mond process.

</details>

## Check your understanding

Why does CO poisoning of haemoglobin occur even though $\mathrm{O_2}$ and CO both bind to $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$?

<details><summary>Reveal</summary>

CO binds to $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ in haemoglobin with much greater affinity than $\mathrm{O_2}$ (roughly 200–250 times). Even small amounts of CO displace oxygen and prevent transport, because the binding is competitive and essentially irreversible under physiological conditions.

</details>

## Remember

- Metal carbonyls obey the 18-electron rule; CO donates 2 electrons per ligand.
- Bonding involves sigma donation plus pi back-donation (synergic effect).
- CO is a strong-field ligand; back-donation lowers the C–O IR stretching frequency.
- Coordination compounds are used in biology (haem, chlorophyll, B₁₂), medicine (cisplatin), analysis (EDTA, colour tests), catalysis and metallurgy.
- The Mond process and cyanide process are the classic metallurgical applications.
