## Reactions named after what they reveal

The reactions in this module are named, but they are not arbitrary. Each one depends on a specific structural feature: an α-hydrogen, the absence of an α-hydrogen, a methyl group next to the carbonyl, or the difference between an aldehyde and a ketone. That is why they are also the best laboratory tests in the chapter. A positive result tells you not just that a carbonyl is present, but which kind.

The key is the **α-hydrogen**, the hydrogen on the carbon immediately next to the carbonyl. That hydrogen is weakly acidic because the resulting carbanion is stabilised by delocalisation onto the carbonyl oxygen. The conjugate base is an **enolate**:

$$
\mathrm{RCH_2CHO + OH^- \rightleftharpoons [RCHCHO]^- + H_2O}.
$$

The enolate is the nucleophile in the aldol and haloform reactions. A carbonyl with no α-hydrogen cannot form an enolate, so it takes a different path.

## 1. Aldol condensation

Two molecules of an aldehyde or ketone with α-hydrogens react in dilute base. One enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of the second molecule, and the product is a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound:

$$
\mathrm{2CH_3CHO \xrightarrow{dil.\ NaOH} CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CHO}.
$$

The product is 3-hydroxybutanal, an **aldol**. On heating, it loses water to give an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CHO \xrightarrow{\Delta} CH_3CH{=}CHCHO + H_2O}.
$$

The product is but-2-enal. The conjugated $\mathrm{C{=}C{-}C{=}O}$ system is more stable than the isolated double bond, which is why dehydration is favourable.

If only one of the two carbonyl compounds has α-hydrogens, a **crossed aldol** is possible. Benzaldehyde has no α-hydrogen, so it can act only as the electrophile; with ethanal and base it gives cinnamaldehyde after dehydration:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5CHO + CH_3CHO \xrightarrow{dil.\ NaOH} C_6H_5CH{=}CHCHO + H_2O}.
$$

## 2. Cannizzaro reaction

An aldehyde with **no α-hydrogen** cannot undergo aldol condensation. In concentrated base it disproportionates instead: one molecule is reduced to an alcohol and another is oxidised to a carboxylate. This is the Cannizzaro reaction.

$$
\mathrm{2C_6H_5CHO + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5CH_2OH + C_6H_5COONa}.
$$

Benzaldehyde gives benzyl alcohol and sodium benzoate. Methanal behaves the same way, giving methanol and sodium methanoate. In a **crossed Cannizzaro** with excess methanal, the formaldehyde is oxidised to formate and the other aldehyde is reduced to its alcohol.

The mechanism begins with addition of $\mathrm{OH^-}$ to the carbonyl carbon. The resulting alkoxide transfers a hydride to a second aldehyde molecule, which is why one carbon is reduced while the other is oxidised.

## 3. Haloform reaction

A methyl ketone, or ethanal, reacts with iodine in sodium hydroxide to give a yellow precipitate of triiodomethane, $\mathrm{CHI_3}$, also called iodoform:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 + 3I_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow CHI_3 + CH_3COONa + 3NaI + 3H_2O}.
$$

Ethanal reacts similarly:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CHO + 3I_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow CHI_3 + HCOONa + 3NaI + 3H_2O}.
$$

The methyl group is fully iodinated to $\mathrm{CI_3}$; hydroxide then attacks the carbonyl carbon and $\mathrm{CI_3^-}$ leaves, giving the carboxylate and iodoform.

The test is positive for ethanal, for methyl ketones such as propanone and butan-2-one, and for secondary alcohols that can be oxidised to a methyl ketone under the reaction conditions, such as propan-2-ol. It is negative for methanol, propanal and benzaldehyde.

## 4. Tests that separate aldehydes from ketones

Aldehydes are easily oxidised; ketones are not. The classical tests exploit that difference.

**Tollens' reagent** is ammoniacal silver nitrate, containing the complex ion $\mathrm{[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+}$. An aldehyde reduces it to metallic silver, which forms a mirror on a clean glass surface:

$$
\mathrm{RCHO + 2[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+ + 3OH^- \rightarrow RCOO^- + 2Ag + 4NH_3 + 2H_2O}.
$$

The test is positive for aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes, and negative for ketones. Methanoic acid also gives a positive result because it contains a formyl hydrogen.

**Fehling's solution** contains copper(II) ions stabilised by tartrate. An aldehyde reduces the blue copper(II) complex to a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide:

$$
\mathrm{RCHO + 2Cu^{2+} + 5OH^- \rightarrow RCOO^- + Cu_2O + 3H_2O}.
$$

Fehling's is positive for aliphatic aldehydes but **negative for aromatic aldehydes** such as benzaldehyde, which are not oxidised easily enough by this reagent. It is also negative for ketones.

**Schiff's reagent** is a decolourised solution of rosaniline. Aldehydes restore a magenta colour; ketones do not react under the usual conditions.

**2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (Brady's reagent)** gives a yellow, orange or red precipitate with most aldehydes and ketones. It confirms a carbonyl group but does not distinguish the two classes. The melting point of the derivative helps identify the specific compound.

| Test | Aldehyde | Ketone | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tollens' | silver mirror | no reaction | aromatic aldehydes also positive |
| Fehling's | red $\mathrm{Cu_2O}$ | no reaction | aromatic aldehydes negative |
| Schiff's | magenta colour | no reaction | sensitive but less specific |
| 2,4-DNP | yellow-orange solid | yellow-orange solid | confirms carbonyl, not class |
| Iodoform | positive for ethanal | positive for methyl ketones | yellow $\mathrm{CHI_3}$ |

## 5. Reducing a carbonyl all the way to an alkane

Two named methods remove the carbonyl oxygen completely.

**Clemmensen reduction** uses zinc amalgam and concentrated hydrochloric acid:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 \xrightarrow{Zn{-}Hg/HCl} CH_3CH_2CH_3}.
$$

**Wolff–Kishner reduction** uses hydrazine, then potassium hydroxide at high temperature in a glycol solvent:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 \xrightarrow[KOH,\ \Delta]{NH_2NH_2} CH_3CH_2CH_3}.
$$

Both convert propanone to propane. Clemmensen is preferred for acid-stable compounds, Wolff–Kishner for base-stable compounds. The Wolff–Kishner route goes through a hydrazone intermediate.

## 6. Worked example: iodoform yield

Propanone ($5.8\ \text{g}$, molar mass $58\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$) is treated with excess iodine and sodium hydroxide. The dried precipitate of iodoform has mass $35.0\ \text{g}$. Find the theoretical yield and the percentage yield.

$$
n(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3})=\frac{5.8}{58}=0.100\ \text{mol}.
$$

One mole of propanone gives one mole of iodoform, so the theoretical amount is $0.100\ \text{mol}$. The molar mass of $\mathrm{CHI_3}$ is

$$
12+1+3(127)=394\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

Thus

$$
m_{\text{theoretical}}=0.100\times394=39.4\ \text{g}.
$$

The percentage yield is

$$
\frac{35.0}{39.4}\times100\%=88.8\%.
$$

**Sanity check.** Iodoform is a heavy molecule because it contains three iodine atoms, so a small molar amount corresponds to a large mass. A yield of $35.0\ \text{g}$ from $5.8\ \text{g}$ of propanone is therefore plausible; the mass of the product is not expected to be close to the mass of the starting material.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Named-reaction traps">

- **Using Fehling's test for benzaldehyde.** Aromatic aldehydes do not reduce Fehling's solution, even though they do reduce Tollens' reagent.
- **Assuming iodoform is specific to methyl ketones.** Ethanal and methyl carbinols such as propan-2-ol also give a positive test.
- **Trying an aldol condensation on benzaldehyde.** It has no α-hydrogen, so it cannot form an enolate; under strong base it undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction instead.
- **Confusing the two complete reductions.** Clemmensen is acidic (Zn–Hg/HCl); Wolff–Kishner is basic (NH₂NH₂/KOH). Choose according to which functional groups survive.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Which of ethanal, propanone and benzaldehyde can undergo an aldol condensation? Explain.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Ethanal and propanone both have α-hydrogens, so they can form enolates and undergo aldol addition. Benzaldehyde has no α-hydrogen: the carbon next to the carbonyl is part of the aromatic ring and already has no hydrogen available for enolisation. Under strong base benzaldehyde undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction instead.

</details>

**2.** A sample of propanone is treated with iodine and sodium hydroxide. Write the equation and calculate the mass of iodoform expected from $0.050\ \text{mol}$ of propanone.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 + 3I_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow CHI_3 + CH_3COONa + 3NaI + 3H_2O}.
$$

The stoichiometry is $1:1$, so $0.050\ \text{mol}$ of propanone gives $0.050\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{CHI_3}$. With molar mass $394\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$,

$$
m=0.050\times394=19.7\ \text{g}.
$$

</details>

**3.** How would you distinguish ethanal from propanone using one simple test?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Use Tollens' reagent or Fehling's solution. Ethanal is an aldehyde and gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent, or a red precipitate of $\mathrm{Cu_2O}$ with Fehling's solution. Propanone is a ketone and gives no reaction with either. The iodoform test would not distinguish them, because both ethanal and propanone give a yellow precipitate.

</details>

## Remember

- α-Hydrogens are acidic because the resulting enolate is resonance-stabilised; a carbonyl without α-hydrogens cannot undergo aldol addition.
- Aldol condensation gives a β-hydroxy carbonyl, which dehydrates to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl.
- The Cannizzaro reaction disproportionates aldehydes that lack α-hydrogens.
- Iodoform is positive for ethanal, methyl ketones and methyl carbinols; Tollens' and Fehling's separate aldehydes from ketones, with Fehling's failing for aromatic aldehydes.
- Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner both remove the carbonyl oxygen, but under acidic and basic conditions respectively.
