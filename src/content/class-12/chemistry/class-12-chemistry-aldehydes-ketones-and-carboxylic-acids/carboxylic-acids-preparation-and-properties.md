## The same carbonyl, now with an –OH beside it

A carboxylic acid contains a carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group on the same carbon:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}COOH}.
$$

That arrangement looks like an aldehyde with an –OH added, but the two groups interact strongly. The oxygen of the –OH donates a lone pair into the carbonyl, and the negative charge left after losing a proton is shared equally by the two oxygens. The result is a functional group that is acidic, forms hydrogen-bonded dimers, and reacts by substitution at the carbonyl carbon rather than by simple addition.

The acid is weak, but it is much stronger than an alcohol or a phenol. Ethanoic acid has $\mathrm{p}K_a=4.76$; ethanol has about $16$; phenol about $10$. The difference comes from the carboxylate ion, not from the –OH itself.

## 1. Structure and the carboxylate ion

The carboxyl carbon is $sp^2$ and the group is planar. In the undissociated acid, the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ bond is about $121\ \text{pm}$ and the $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bond about $143\ \text{pm}$. When the proton is lost, the two $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bonds become equivalent at about $127\ \text{pm}$. The negative charge is delocalised over both oxygens:

$$
\mathrm{RCOOH \rightleftharpoons RCOO^- + H^+}.
$$

This delocalisation is the main reason carboxylic acids are acidic. An alkoxide has the charge localised on one oxygen; a carboxylate spreads it over two equivalent oxygens, which lowers the energy of the ion.

## 2. Nomenclature

The suffix for a carboxylic acid is **-oic acid**, and the carbonyl carbon is C1.

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{HCOOH}$ | methanoic acid | formic acid |
| $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ | ethanoic acid | acetic acid |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COOH}$ | propanoic acid | propionic acid |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2COOH}$ | butanoic acid | butyric acid |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5COOH}$ | benzoic acid | benzoic acid |
| $\mathrm{HOOC{-}COOH}$ | ethanedioic acid | oxalic acid |
| $\mathrm{HOOC{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$ | propanedioic acid | malonic acid |
| $\mathrm{HOOC{-}(CH_2)_4{-}COOH}$ | hexanedioic acid | adipic acid |

When a carboxylic acid is present with another group, the acid has the highest priority and the other group becomes a prefix: $\mathrm{HOCH_2COOH}$ is 2-hydroxyethanoic acid (glycolic acid), and $\mathrm{CH_3COCOOH}$ is 2-oxopropanoic acid (pyruvic acid).

Salts are named by replacing **-ic acid** with **-ate**: sodium ethanoate, sodium benzoate, potassium methanoate.

## 3. Physical properties

Carboxylic acids form **cyclic dimers** through two hydrogen bonds:

$$
\mathrm{2RCOOH \rightleftharpoons (RCOOH)_2}.
$$

The dimer has twice the apparent molar mass and much stronger intermolecular forces than a simple alcohol. That is why ethanoic acid boils at about $118^\circ\text{C}$, well above ethanol ($78^\circ\text{C}$) and far above an alkane of similar mass.

The first four members are miscible with water because the carboxyl group both donates and accepts hydrogen bonds. Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain grows; benzoic acid is only slightly soluble in cold water.

## 4. Acidity and substituent effects

The acid dissociation constant is

$$
K_a=\frac{[\mathrm{RCOO^-}][\mathrm{H^+}]}{[\mathrm{RCOOH}]},
\qquad
\mathrm{p}K_a=-\log_{10}K_a.
$$

A smaller $\mathrm{p}K_a$ means a stronger acid. Some useful values:

| Acid | $\mathrm{p}K_a$ | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| methanoic acid | $3.75$ | no electron-donating alkyl group |
| ethanoic acid | $4.76$ | methyl group donates electron density |
| propanoic acid | $4.87$ | longer alkyl group, slightly weaker |
| benzoic acid | $4.20$ | ring withdraws electron density inductively |
| chloroethanoic acid | $2.87$ | chlorine withdraws electron density |
| dichloroethanoic acid | $1.29$ | two withdrawing groups |
| trichloroethanoic acid | $0.65$ | three withdrawing groups |
| ethanedioic acid | $1.23$ (first) | second carboxyl group withdraws |

Two rules cover most questions.

- **Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity.** They stabilise the carboxylate ion by pulling electron density away from the negative charge. Halogens, $\mathrm{-NO_2}$ and $\mathrm{-CN}$ are common examples.
- **Electron-donating groups decrease acidity.** Alkyl groups push electron density towards the carboxylate and destabilise it. This is why methanoic acid is stronger than ethanoic acid.

The inductive effect weakens with distance. A chlorine on the α-carbon has a large effect; the same chlorine on the β-carbon has much less. That distance dependence is a standard exam comparison.

## 5. Preparation

**Oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes.** Acidified potassium permanganate or dichromate takes a primary alcohol through the aldehyde to the acid:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow{[O]} CH_3CHO \xrightarrow{[O]} CH_3COOH}.
$$

**Oxidation of alkylbenzenes.** Toluene is oxidised to benzoic acid with hot alkaline permanganate, and the side chain is cut back to the ring:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_3 \xrightarrow{KMnO_4,\ \Delta} C_6H_5COOH}.
$$

**Hydrolysis of nitriles.** A nitrile is hydrolysed in aqueous acid or base:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CN + 2H_2O + H^+ \rightarrow CH_3COOH + NH_4^+}.
$$

**Grignard reagent and carbon dioxide.** The Grignard reagent attacks $\mathrm{CO_2}$ to give a carboxylate salt, which is protonated on work-up:

$$
\mathrm{RMgX + CO_2 \rightarrow RCOOMgX \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} RCOOH}.
$$

This route adds one carbon, so it is useful for ascending a homologue.

**Hydrolysis of esters.** Acidic or basic hydrolysis of an ester gives the acid or its salt. Basic hydrolysis is irreversible because the carboxylate is formed directly.

## 6. Reactions of carboxylic acids

**Acid–base reactions.** Carboxylic acids liberate carbon dioxide from sodium hydrogen carbonate, which distinguishes them from phenols:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + NaHCO_3 \rightarrow CH_3COONa + CO_2 + H_2O}.
$$

They also react with reactive metals and with sodium hydroxide:

$$
\mathrm{2CH_3COOH + 2Na \rightarrow 2CH_3COONa + H_2}.
$$

**Esterification.** With an alcohol and an acid catalyst, the acid forms an ester. The reaction is reversible and is driven forward by removing water:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}.
$$

**Reduction.** Lithium aluminium hydride reduces a carboxylic acid to a primary alcohol:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH \xrightarrow{LiAlH_4} CH_3CH_2OH}.
$$

Sodium borohydride is not strong enough for this reduction.

**Decarboxylation.** Sodium ethanoate heated with soda lime loses carbon dioxide and gives methane:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO,\ \Delta} CH_4 + Na_2CO_3}.
$$

β-Keto acids decarboxylate much more easily because the enol intermediate is stabilised.

**α-Halogenation (Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky).** Red phosphorus and chlorine or bromine replace an α-hydrogen:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{P} CH_2ClCOOH + HCl}.
$$

**Formic acid is special.** It has a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon, so it behaves partly like an aldehyde: it reduces Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution, and it can be oxidised to carbon dioxide and water.

## 7. Worked example: pH of a weak acid

Find the pH and the percentage ionisation of a $0.10\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ solution of ethanoic acid at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Take $K_a=1.8\times10^{-5}$.

Let $x$ be the concentration of $\mathrm{H^+}$ and of ethanoate at equilibrium. Then

$$
K_a=\frac{x^2}{0.10-x}\approx\frac{x^2}{0.10}.
$$

The approximation is justified if $x$ is small compared with $0.10$. Solving,

$$
x^2=(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.10)=1.8\times10^{-6},
$$

$$
x=\sqrt{1.8\times10^{-6}}=1.34\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

The pH is

$$
\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}(1.34\times10^{-3})=2.87.
$$

The percentage ionisation is

$$
\frac{1.34\times10^{-3}}{0.10}\times100\%=1.34\%.
$$

**Sanity check.** The ionisation is only about $1.3\%$, so the approximation $0.10-x\approx0.10$ is valid. The pH lies between that of a strong acid of the same concentration ($\mathrm{pH}=1$) and pure water ($\mathrm{pH}=7$), which is what a weak acid should give.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Carboxylic-acid traps">

- **Treating a carboxylic acid as a strong acid.** It is weak and only partly ionised; calculate with $K_a$ rather than assuming complete dissociation.
- **Using sodium bicarbonate to distinguish a carboxylic acid from a phenol.** Both dissolve in NaOH, but only the carboxylic acid gives carbon dioxide with $\mathrm{NaHCO_3}$.
- **Forgetting that formic acid reduces Tollens' reagent.** Its carbonyl carbon still carries a hydrogen, so it behaves like an aldehyde towards mild oxidants.
- **Ignoring the dimer when explaining boiling points.** Carboxylic acids boil higher than alcohols of similar mass because of the cyclic hydrogen-bonded dimer.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Explain why trichloroethanoic acid is a much stronger acid than ethanoic acid.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Each chlorine withdraws electron density inductively from the carboxylate ion. Three chlorines make the carboxylate substantially more stable by dispersing the negative charge, so the equilibrium for proton loss lies further to the right. The $\mathrm{p}K_a$ falls from $4.76$ for ethanoic acid to about $0.65$ for trichloroethanoic acid.

</details>

**2.** Arrange benzoic acid, 4-nitrobenzoic acid and 4-methylbenzoic acid in order of increasing acidity.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The nitro group is strongly electron-withdrawing and stabilises the carboxylate, so 4-nitrobenzoic acid is the strongest. The methyl group is electron-donating and destabilises the carboxylate, so 4-methylbenzoic acid is the weakest. The order is 4-methylbenzoic acid < benzoic acid < 4-nitrobenzoic acid.

</details>

**3.** Excess sodium hydrogen carbonate is added to $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of ethanoic acid. Calculate the mass of sodium ethanoate formed.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The reaction is

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + NaHCO_3 \rightarrow CH_3COONa + CO_2 + H_2O}.
$$

The stoichiometry is $1:1$, so $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of acid gives $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of sodium ethanoate. Its molar mass is $82\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so

$$
m=0.10\times82=8.2\ \text{g}.
$$

</details>

## Remember

- The carboxyl group is planar and the carboxylate ion delocalises its negative charge over two equivalent oxygens, which is why carboxylic acids are much stronger acids than alcohols.
- Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity; electron-donating alkyl groups decrease it, and the effect weakens with distance.
- Carboxylic acids form hydrogen-bonded dimers, so they boil higher than alcohols of similar mass.
- They liberate $\mathrm{CO_2}$ from $\mathrm{NaHCO_3}$, form esters with alcohols, and are reduced to primary alcohols by LiAlH₄.
- Formic acid is the exception that reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents because it retains a formyl hydrogen.
