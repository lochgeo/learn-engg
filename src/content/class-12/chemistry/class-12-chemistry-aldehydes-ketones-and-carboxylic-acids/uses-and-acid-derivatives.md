## From one acid, a family of compounds

A carboxylic acid is not only an acid. Its carbonyl carbon can be converted into a group that carries a different leaving group: chloride, carboxylate, alkoxide or amide. These are the **acid derivatives**, and they share a common structure:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CO{-}L},
$$

where $\mathrm{L}$ is the leaving group. The chemistry of the whole family is one reaction, **nucleophilic acyl substitution**, repeated with different leaving groups. The differences between the members are differences of reactivity, and reactivity is what makes them useful in synthesis.

## 1. The derivative family

| Derivative | General formula | Leaving group $\mathrm{L}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| acyl chloride | $\mathrm{RCOCl}$ | $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ |
| acid anhydride | $\mathrm{(RCO)_2O}$ | $\mathrm{RCOO^-}$ |
| ester | $\mathrm{RCOOR'}$ | $\mathrm{R'O^-}$ |
| amide | $\mathrm{RCONH_2}$ | $\mathrm{NH_2^-}$ |

A nitrile, $\mathrm{RCN}$, is sometimes included because it hydrolyses to a carboxylic acid, but it does not contain a carbonyl group and is better treated as a separate functional group.

## 2. Why the reactivity order is fixed

The order is

$$
\text{acyl chloride} > \text{anhydride} > \text{ester} > \text{amide}.
$$

Two factors explain it.

**Leaving-group ability.** A good leaving group is a weak base. Chloride is a very weak base, so it leaves easily. A carboxylate is a weaker base than an alkoxide, and an amide nitrogen, if it left as $\mathrm{NH_2^-}$, would be a very strong base indeed. The better the leaving group, the faster the substitution.

**Resonance donation into the carbonyl.** The heteroatom attached to the carbonyl donates a lone pair into the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ π system. That donation reduces the partial positive charge on the carbonyl carbon and makes attack by a nucleophile slower. Nitrogen donates much more strongly than oxygen, so an amide is the least electrophilic member. Chlorine donates poorly, so an acyl chloride is the most electrophilic.

This is the same logic as the reactivity of aldehydes and ketones, extended to a carbon that carries a leaving group.

## 3. The substitution mechanism

Nucleophilic acyl substitution is an **addition–elimination** sequence:

1. The nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, and the π bond opens to give a tetrahedral intermediate.
2. The tetrahedral intermediate collapses. The carbonyl reforms and the leaving group departs.

$$
\mathrm{RCO{-}L + Nu^- \rightarrow [R{-}C(O^-)(Nu){-}L] \rightarrow RCO{-}Nu + L^-}.
$$

Unlike nucleophilic addition to an aldehyde or ketone, the carbonyl is restored at the end. That is why acid derivatives undergo substitution rather than simply adding a nucleophile.

## 4. Making the derivatives

**Acyl chlorides** are made from the acid with thionyl chloride, phosphorus pentachloride or phosphorus trichloride:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow CH_3COCl + SO_2 + HCl}.
$$

Thionyl chloride is convenient because the by-products are gases, so the acyl chloride is left relatively pure. Acyl chlorides fume in moist air and must be kept dry.

**Anhydrides** can be made by dehydrating the acid with phosphorus pentoxide, or by treating an acyl chloride with a carboxylate salt:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCl + CH_3COONa \rightarrow (CH_3CO)_2O + NaCl}.
$$

**Esters** are made by Fischer esterification, or more rapidly by treating an acyl chloride or anhydride with an alcohol:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCl + C_2H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3COOC_2H_5 + HCl}.
$$

**Amides** are made by treating an acyl chloride or anhydride with ammonia. Two equivalents of ammonia are needed because one neutralises the hydrogen chloride:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCl + 2NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + NH_4Cl}.
$$

## 5. Reactions of the derivatives

**Hydrolysis.** Acyl chlorides hydrolyse vigorously with water; anhydrides hydrolyse steadily; esters hydrolyse slowly and need acid or base; amides are the most resistant and need prolonged heating with acid or alkali.

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COCl + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3COOH + HCl},
$$

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5 + NaOH \rightarrow CH_3COONa + C_2H_5OH}.
$$

The basic hydrolysis of an ester is **saponification** when the ester is a fat or oil. It is irreversible because the carboxylate is formed directly.

**Alcoholysis and ammonolysis.** Esters react with alcohols to give a different ester (transesterification), and with ammonia to give an amide:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5 + NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + C_2H_5OH}.
$$

Transesterification is the basis of biodiesel production: a triglyceride reacts with methanol to give methyl esters and glycerol.

**Reduction.** Esters and amides are reduced by lithium aluminium hydride. An ester gives two alcohols; an amide gives an amine.

**Dehydration of amides.** Phosphorus pentoxide removes water from an amide to give a nitrile:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CONH_2 \xrightarrow{P_2O_5} CH_3CN + H_2O}.
$$

**Hofmann bromamide reaction.** A primary amide treated with bromine and sodium hydroxide loses the carbonyl carbon and gives an amine with one carbon fewer:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow CH_3NH_2 + Na_2CO_3 + 2NaBr + 2H_2O}.
$$

This is a useful way to descend a homologue.

## 6. Uses of carboxylic acids and their derivatives

| Compound | Main uses |
| --- | --- |
| methanoic acid | leather tanning, rubber coagulation, preservative |
| ethanoic acid | vinegar (about $5$–$8\%$), solvent, cellulose acetate, aspirin synthesis |
| benzoic acid and benzoates | food preservatives |
| oxalic acid | rust removal, metal cleaning, bleaching |
| citric acid | food and beverage acidulant |
| long-chain fatty acids | soaps, detergents, cosmetics |
| ethyl ethanoate | solvent, pear-drop flavour |
| methyl butanoate | pineapple flavour |
| acid chlorides | acylating agents in pharmaceuticals and polymers |
| amides | nylon, urea, proteins |

Aspirin is made by acetylating salicylic acid with ethanoic anhydride:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_4(OH)COOH + (CH_3CO)_2O \rightarrow C_6H_4(OCOCH_3)COOH + CH_3COOH}.
$$

Nylon-6,6 is a polyamide made from hexanedioic acid (adipic acid) and hexane-1,6-diamine. The amide links in the polymer are the same $\mathrm{-CO{-}NH{-}}$ group you meet in a simple amide.

## 7. Worked example: yield and atom economy

Ethanoic acid ($12.0\ \text{g}$, molar mass $60\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$) is heated with excess ethanol and a little concentrated sulphuric acid. The isolated ethyl ethanoate has mass $12.3\ \text{g}$. Find the percentage yield and the atom economy of the esterification.

$$
n(\mathrm{CH_3COOH})=\frac{12.0}{60}=0.200\ \text{mol}.
$$

The reaction is $1:1$, so the theoretical amount of ester is $0.200\ \text{mol}$. Ethyl ethanoate has molar mass $88\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$:

$$
m_{\text{theoretical}}=0.200\times88=17.6\ \text{g}.
$$

The percentage yield is

$$
\frac{12.3}{17.6}\times100\%=69.9\%\approx70\%.
$$

For the atom economy, the balanced equation is

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}.
$$

The desired product has molar mass $88\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$; the reactants together have molar mass $60+46=106\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. Hence

$$
\text{atom economy}=\frac{88}{106}\times100\%=83.0\%.
$$

**Sanity check.** The yield is below $100\%$, as an equilibrium esterification should be. The atom economy is below $100\%$ because water is a by-product; a reaction that produced no by-product would have an atom economy of $100\%$.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Derivative traps">

- **Treating an amide as a base like an amine.** The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl, so amides are essentially neutral. They do not form salts with dilute acid in the way amines do.
- **Assuming esters hydrolyse as easily as acyl chlorides.** Acyl chlorides react vigorously with water; esters need acid or base and heat.
- **Reversing the reactivity order.** Acyl chlorides are the most reactive and amides the least; the order follows leaving-group ability and resonance donation.
- **Forgetting the second equivalent of ammonia.** The hydrogen chloride produced in the reaction consumes one equivalent, so two are needed to isolate the amide.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Explain why ethanoyl chloride reacts much faster than ethyl ethanoate with water.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Chloride is a much weaker base and therefore a much better leaving group than ethoxide. In addition, the oxygen of the ester donates electron density into the carbonyl far more strongly than chlorine does, which reduces the electrophilicity of the ester carbonyl. Both effects make the acyl chloride much more reactive.

</details>

**2.** Give the products of (a) ethanoic anhydride with ethanol and (b) ethanoic anhydride with ammonia.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

(a) The alcohol attacks one carbonyl, ethanoate leaves, and the products are ethyl ethanoate and ethanoic acid:

$$
\mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O + C_2H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3COOC_2H_5 + CH_3COOH}.
$$

(b) Ammonia attacks, and the products are ethanamide and ammonium ethanoate:

$$
\mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O + 2NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + CH_3COONH_4}.
$$

</details>

**3.** Ethanoic acid ($0.10\ \text{mol}$) is converted to ethyl ethanoate in $75\%$ yield. Calculate the mass of ester obtained.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The theoretical amount is $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of ester. At $75\%$ yield, the actual amount is $0.075\ \text{mol}$. With molar mass $88\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$,

$$
m=0.075\times88=6.6\ \text{g}.
$$

</details>

## Remember

- Acid derivatives share the structure $\mathrm{RCO{-}L}$ and react by nucleophilic acyl substitution: addition to the carbonyl, then elimination of the leaving group.
- Reactivity follows the leaving group and resonance donation: acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide.
- Acyl chlorides are made with $\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ or $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ and react vigorously with water, alcohols and ammonia.
- Esters are hydrolysed in base by saponification and converted into other esters by transesterification, the basis of biodiesel.
- Amides are neutral, form polyamides such as nylon, and can be degraded to amines by the Hofmann bromamide reaction.
