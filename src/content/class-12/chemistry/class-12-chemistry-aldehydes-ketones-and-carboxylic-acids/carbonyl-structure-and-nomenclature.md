## The group that makes carbon electrophilic

Most organic reactions you have met so far begin with an electron-rich species attacking something electron-poor. The carbonyl group, $\mathrm{C{=}O}$, supplies that electron-poor centre in a remarkably clean way. Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the shared pair is pulled towards oxygen. The carbon is left with a partial positive charge, and a nucleophile can attack it directly.

That single polarised bond explains a large part of this chapter: nucleophilic addition to aldehydes and ketones, the acidity of the hydrogens next to a carbonyl, the ease of oxidation of aldehydes, and the reactivity of acid derivatives. Before any of that, you need to see the shape, name the compounds correctly and count what the formula allows.

## 1. Shape and polarity of the carbonyl group

The carbonyl carbon is bonded to three atoms and has no lone pair of its own, so it is $sp^2$ hybridised. Its three σ bonds lie in a plane at about $120^\circ$, and the remaining $p$ orbital forms a π bond with oxygen above and below that plane.

| Feature | Typical value or consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Hybridisation at carbon | $sp^2$, trigonal planar |
| Bond angle | about $120^\circ$ |
| $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ bond length | about $121\ \text{pm}$ |
| $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ single bond length | about $143\ \text{pm}$ |
| Dipole moment | $2.3$–$2.9\ \text{D}$, pointing towards oxygen |

The dipole is the key. We write the polarisation as $\mathrm{C^{\delta+}{=}O^{\delta-}}$. The carbon is not a full positive ion, but it is electrophilic enough to be attacked by cyanide, hydride, Grignard reagents, ammonia derivatives and alcohols. The oxygen, meanwhile, can accept a proton or a Lewis acid, which is why many carbonyl reactions are catalysed by acid.

The π bond is also polarised, so the carbonyl oxygen is a weak Lewis base and a good hydrogen-bond acceptor. That makes low-molecular-mass aldehydes and ketones soluble in water, even though they cannot donate a hydrogen bond themselves.

## 2. Aldehydes and ketones

An **aldehyde** has the carbonyl carbon bonded to at least one hydrogen:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CHO}.
$$

A **ketone** has the carbonyl carbon bonded to two carbon groups:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}CO{-}R'}.
$$

The simplest aldehyde is methanal, $\mathrm{HCHO}$, usually called formaldehyde. It is the only aldehyde with no alkyl group at all. The simplest ketone is propanone, $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}$, commonly called acetone.

| Structure | Class | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{HCHO}$ | aldehyde | methanal | formaldehyde |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CHO}$ | aldehyde | ethanal | acetaldehyde |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CHO}$ | aldehyde | propanal | propionaldehyde |
| $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}$ | ketone | propanone | acetone |
| $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_3}$ | ketone | butan-2-one | methyl ethyl ketone |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5CHO}$ | aromatic aldehyde | benzaldehyde | benzaldehyde |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5COCH_3}$ | aromatic ketone | 1-phenylethan-1-one | acetophenone |

A ketone cannot be terminal: a carbonyl at the end of a chain would have a hydrogen on it and would therefore be an aldehyde. This is a useful structural check when you draw a skeleton.

## 3. Nomenclature

For the IUPAC name, find the longest chain that contains the carbonyl carbon, number it so that the carbonyl carbon receives the lowest possible locant, and add the suffix **-al** for an aldehyde or **-one** for a ketone. The carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde is always C1, so its locant is normally omitted.

- $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CHO}$ is butanal, not butan-1-al.
- $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_3}$ is butan-2-one; the carbonyl is at C2, so the locant is needed.
- $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2CHO}$ is 4-oxopentanal. The aldehyde has higher priority, so the ketone is named as an **oxo** prefix.
- $\mathrm{OHC{-}CH_2{-}CHO}$ is propanedial; both ends are aldehydes, so the suffix is **-dial**.

When another group has a higher priority than the carbonyl, the carbonyl becomes a prefix. The main order you need is:

$$
\text{carboxylic acid} > \text{ester} > \text{amide} > \text{aldehyde} > \text{ketone} > \text{alcohol}.
$$

So $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2OH}$ is 4-hydroxybutan-2-one, and $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2COOH}$ is 3-oxobutanoic acid.

Common names survive in the laboratory and in industry: formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, acetone, benzaldehyde and acetophenone are all acceptable in everyday use, but the IUPAC name is the one to use when the structure must be unambiguous.

## 4. Counting unsaturation

A molecular formula alone does not fix a structure. The **degree of unsaturation**, also called the index of hydrogen deficiency, tells you how many rings and π bonds the formula contains. For a saturated, acyclic hydrocarbon with $n$ carbons, the formula is $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}$. Every ring or π bond removes two hydrogen atoms. Halogens occupy hydrogen positions, and each nitrogen adds one hydrogen to the saturated formula. Oxygen and sulphur do not change the count.

For a formula $\mathrm{C_cH_hN_nX_x}$,

$$
\text{DBE}=\frac{2c+2+n-h-x}{2}.
$$

A carbonyl group contributes one degree of unsaturation. A benzene ring contributes four: three π bonds plus one ring. So benzaldehyde, $\mathrm{C_7H_6O}$, has

$$
\text{DBE}=\frac{2(7)+2-6}{2}=5,
$$

which matches one carbonyl plus one aromatic ring.

This count is a fast way to reject impossible structures. A formula with $\text{DBE}=1$ cannot contain both a carbonyl and a $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bond unless it also has no ring and the hydrogens have been miscounted.

## 5. Physical consequences

Aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole attractions, so their boiling points lie above those of alkanes of similar molar mass but below those of alcohols. Butanal (molar mass $72\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$) boils near $75^\circ\text{C}$; pentane, with almost the same molar mass, boils near $36^\circ\text{C}$; butan-1-ol, which can donate a hydrogen bond, boils near $118^\circ\text{C}$.

The first few members are appreciably soluble in water because the carbonyl oxygen accepts hydrogen bonds from water. Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon part grows. Methanal and ethanal are gases or very volatile liquids; the higher members are liquids or solids.

## 6. Worked example: formula from combustion

A $0.144\ \text{g}$ sample of a saturated aldehyde burns completely to give $0.352\ \text{g}$ of carbon dioxide and $0.144\ \text{g}$ of water. Find its molecular formula and identify a possible structure.

Convert the masses to amounts:

$$
n(\mathrm{CO_2})=\frac{0.352}{44}=8.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol},
$$

so $n(\mathrm{C})=8.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$.

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2O})=\frac{0.144}{18}=8.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol},
$$

so $n(\mathrm{H})=2(8.00\times10^{-3})=1.60\times10^{-2}\ \text{mol}$.

The mass of oxygen in the sample is the remainder:

$$
m(\mathrm{O})=0.144-(8.00\times10^{-3})(12)-(1.60\times10^{-2})(1)=0.032\ \text{g},
$$

$$
n(\mathrm{O})=\frac{0.032}{16}=2.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}.
$$

The ratio is

$$
\mathrm{C:H:O}=8.00:16.0:2.00=4:8:1,
$$

so the empirical formula is $\mathrm{C_4H_8O}$. Its empirical formula mass is $72\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, and the sample amount is $0.144/72=2.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$, so the molecular formula is also $\mathrm{C_4H_8O}$.

The degree of unsaturation is

$$
\text{DBE}=\frac{2(4)+2-8}{2}=1,
$$

which is exactly one carbonyl and no ring or $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bond. A saturated aldehyde with four carbons is butanal, $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CHO}$. Its isomer butan-2-one is a ketone, so the aldehyde must be butanal; the question's statement that the compound is an aldehyde settles the choice.

**Sanity check.** Butanal has molar mass $72\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, and $0.002\ \text{mol}$ contains $0.008\ \text{mol}$ of carbon, which is $0.352\ \text{g}$ of $\mathrm{CO_2}$. The numbers agree.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Naming and structure traps">

- **Putting the carbonyl at the end of a ketone.** A terminal carbonyl is an aldehyde. Ketones need a carbon group on each side.
- **Numbering from the wrong end.** Choose the direction that gives the carbonyl the lower locant, then number substituents.
- **Forgetting the priority order.** A ketone in a molecule that also contains an aldehyde or a carboxylic acid is named as an *oxo* prefix, not as *-one*.
- **Treating oxygen as contributing to the degree of unsaturation.** Oxygen is ignored in the DBE formula; only carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and halogens enter.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Give the IUPAC name of $\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2CHO}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The aldehyde has higher priority than the ketone, so the chain is numbered from the aldehyde carbon: C1 is $\mathrm{CHO}$, C2 is $\mathrm{CH_2}$, C3 is $\mathrm{CH_2}$, C4 is the carbonyl carbon and C5 is the methyl carbon. The ketone is therefore at C4 and is written as an *oxo* prefix. The name is **4-oxopentanal**.

</details>

**2.** A compound has molecular formula $\mathrm{C_6H_8O}$. What is its degree of unsaturation, and what does that allow?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$$
\text{DBE}=\frac{2(6)+2-8}{2}=3.
$$

The formula has three degrees of unsaturation. They could be, for example, one carbonyl plus two $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bonds, or one carbonyl plus one ring and one $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bond. The formula alone does not distinguish these; it only rules out a fully saturated acyclic carbonyl, which would need $\text{DBE}=1$.

</details>

**3.** Butanal and pentane have almost the same molar mass. Which has the higher boiling point, and why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Butanal boils higher. Its polar $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ group gives dipole–dipole attractions between molecules, while pentane relies only on weaker dispersion forces. Butan-1-ol would boil higher still because it can donate a hydrogen bond, which butanal cannot.

</details>

## Remember

- The carbonyl carbon is $sp^2$, planar and electrophilic because the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ bond is polarised towards oxygen.
- Aldehydes have a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon; ketones have two carbon groups and therefore cannot be terminal.
- Name aldehydes with **-al** and ketones with **-one**, numbering to give the carbonyl the lowest locant and using *oxo* when a higher-priority group is present.
- The degree of unsaturation is $\text{DBE}=(2c+2+n-h-x)/2$; a carbonyl counts as one.
- Dipole–dipole forces raise boiling points above alkanes, but without an $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ bond aldehydes and ketones boil below alcohols of similar mass.
