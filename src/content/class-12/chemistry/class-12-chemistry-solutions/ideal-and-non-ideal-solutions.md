## When mixing is “perfect”

An **ideal solution** is one in which every intermolecular interaction is the same strength: A–A, B–B and A–B interactions are all equal. In such a solution the molecules mix without any heat effect and without any volume change, and Raoult’s law holds for both components over the entire composition range. Real solutions are ideal only in the limit of very similar molecules (benzene + toluene, hexane + heptane, or a component in very dilute solution).

This module classifies solutions as ideal or non-ideal, explains the two kinds of deviation, and introduces azeotropes — the mixtures that refuse to be separated by ordinary distillation.

## 1. Conditions for an ideal solution

For a binary mixture of A and B to be ideal, three conditions should hold:

1. $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}=0$ (no heat is absorbed or released on mixing).
2. $\Delta V_{\text{mix}}=0$ (the volume of the mixture equals the sum of the pure volumes).
3. Raoult’s law holds for both components for all compositions: $p_A=x_A\,p_A^\circ$ and $p_B=x_B\,p_B^\circ$.

These conditions are satisfied when A and B have similar size, shape and intermolecular force type. Benzene and toluene are the textbook example; n-hexane and n-heptane are another.

## 2. Non-ideal solutions: deviations from Raoult’s law

Most real mixtures deviate because A–B interactions differ from A–A and B–B. The deviation is measured by comparing the observed vapour pressure with Raoult’s-law prediction.

### Positive deviation

If A–B interactions are **weaker** than the average of A–A and B–B, molecules escape more easily, so the vapour pressure is **higher** than Raoult’s law predicts. $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}>0$ (endothermic) and $\Delta V_{\text{mix}}>0$ (expansion).

Examples: ethanol + water, acetone + carbon disulphide, acetone + ethanol. In ethanol + water, hydrogen bonding between like molecules is partially broken on mixing, so the mixture is “looser”.

### Negative deviation

If A–B interactions are **stronger** (for example, hydrogen bonding or acid–base complexing), molecules are held more tightly, so the vapour pressure is **lower** than predicted. $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}<0$ (exothermic) and $\Delta V_{\text{mix}}<0$ (contraction).

Examples: phenol + aniline, chloroform + acetone, nitric acid + water. In chloroform + acetone, a hydrogen bond forms between the chloroform H and the acetone O, stabilising the mixture.

## 3. Azeotropes

When a deviation is large enough, the vapour-pressure curve develops a maximum or a minimum, and the liquid and vapour compositions become equal at one point. That mixture is an **azeotrope**: it boils at a constant temperature and composition, so ordinary distillation cannot separate it further.

- **Minimum-boiling azeotrope** (positive deviation): boils at a temperature *below* both pure components. Example: ethanol + water at 95.6 % ethanol by mass, boiling at 351.1 K (pure ethanol boils at 351.5 K, water at 373 K).
- **Maximum-boiling azeotrope** (negative deviation): boils at a temperature *above* both pure components. Example: nitric acid + water at 68 % HNO₃, boiling at 393.5 K.

Azeotropes are not compounds; they are mixtures with a fixed boiling composition. Breaking them needs azeotropic or extractive distillation, or a chemical step.

## 4. Raoult’s law in dilute solutions: Henry’s law as a special case

In a very dilute solution of B in A, the solvent A obeys Raoult’s law ($p_A=x_Ap_A^\circ$), while the solute B obeys **Henry’s law** ($p_B=K_H\,x_B$). Henry’s law is therefore the limiting form of Raoult’s law for the solute at infinite dilution. This is why gas solubility (Henry’s law) and vapour-pressure lowering (Raoult’s law) are two faces of the same idea.

## 5. Worked example: classifying a mixture

Consider acetone + chloroform. Pure acetone boils at 329 K and pure chloroform at 334 K. The mixture shows a maximum boiling point and a minimum vapour pressure at an intermediate composition.

- The vapour pressure is **lower** than Raoult’s law predicts → **negative deviation**.
- Cause: hydrogen bonding between chloroform’s hydrogen and acetone’s oxygen, which is stronger than the dipole–dipole forces in either pure liquid.
- Result: a **maximum-boiling azeotrope**.

Classification check: negative deviation ↔ stronger A–B interaction ↔ exothermic mixing ↔ volume contraction ↔ maximum-boiling azeotrope. All five statements are consistent.

## 6. Method for deviation problems

1. Compare the observed vapour pressure with the Raoult’s-law value.
2. If observed > predicted: positive deviation; A–B weaker; endothermic; minimum-boiling azeotrope possible.
3. If observed < predicted: negative deviation; A–B stronger; exothermic; maximum-boiling azeotrope possible.
4. Name the specific interaction (hydrogen bond, dipole, London) that explains the sign.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Confusing deviation sign with boiling-point direction">
A positive deviation means *higher* vapour pressure, which means *lower* boiling point — a minimum-boiling azeotrope. A negative deviation means *lower* vapour pressure and a *higher* boiling point. Students often pair “positive” with “higher boiling point”; the correct pairing is positive deviation ↔ minimum boiling point. Always trace the chain: deviation sign → vapour pressure → boiling point.
</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** A mixture of two liquids shows $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}<0$ and $\Delta V_{\text{mix}}<0$. What kind of deviation does it show, and what happens to its vapour pressure?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Negative deviation: A–B interactions are stronger than like–like interactions, so molecules are held more tightly and the vapour pressure is lower than Raoult’s law predicts.

</details>

**B.** Which of the following pairs is most likely to form an ideal solution: (i) ethanol + water, (ii) benzene + toluene, (iii) acetone + chloroform?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Benzene + toluene: both are non-polar, similar size and shape, with only London forces, so A–A, B–B and A–B interactions are nearly equal. The other two show strong hydrogen bonding on mixing and deviate markedly.

</details>

**C.** Explain why an ethanol–water azeotrope cannot be separated into pure ethanol by simple distillation.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

At the azeotropic composition the liquid and vapour have the same composition, so boiling produces vapour of the same composition as the liquid. No enrichment occurs, and the composition stays fixed until one component is exhausted in a way that leaves the azeotrope.

</details>

## Check your understanding

A student claims that every mixture with a negative heat of mixing must be non-ideal. Is this correct? Explain.

<details><summary>Reveal</summary>

No. An ideal solution has $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}=0$ exactly. Any non-zero heat of mixing, including negative, signals a non-ideal solution. The claim is true in its conclusion (negative heat of mixing ⇒ non-ideal) but the reasoning should be that ideal solutions have zero heat of mixing by definition, not that non-ideality is a matter of degree.

</details>

## Take away

- Ideal solutions have equal A–A, B–B and A–B interactions: $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}=\Delta V_{\text{mix}}=0$ and Raoult’s law holds everywhere.
- Positive deviation: weaker A–B, higher vapour pressure, lower boiling point, possible minimum-boiling azeotrope.
- Negative deviation: stronger A–B, lower vapour pressure, higher boiling point, possible maximum-boiling azeotrope.
- Henry’s law is Raoult’s law for the solute at infinite dilution.
