## The lone pair that defines a family

Ammonia is a weak base because its nitrogen atom carries a lone pair that can accept a proton. Replace one or more of ammonia's hydrogens by alkyl or aryl groups and you obtain an **amine**. The nitrogen remains the functional centre: it is roughly tetrahedral, with three bonds and one lone pair pointing into the fourth corner.

Almost everything in this chapter follows from that lone pair. It makes amines basic, it makes them nucleophiles, and it lets them form salts with acids. The attached groups decide how available the lone pair really is. Alkyl groups push electron density toward nitrogen; an aromatic ring pulls the lone pair into its π system. Learning to read that competition is the main skill of the chapter.

## 1. Geometry at nitrogen

In ammonia the H–N–H angle is about $107.8^\circ$, smaller than the tetrahedral $109.5^\circ$ because the lone pair occupies more space than a bond pair. Simple amines keep angles close to this value. The C–N bond is about $147\ \text{pm}$ long, between a C–C single bond ($154\ \text{pm}$) and a C–O bond ($143\ \text{pm}$).

The nitrogen is therefore **pyramidal**, not flat. This matters for two reasons:

- The lone pair is exposed above the three substituents, so it can approach a proton or an electrophile from a defined direction.
- A nitrogen with three different substituents is chiral, just as a carbon with four different groups is chiral.

### Nitrogen inversion

Unlike a chiral carbon, a chiral amine usually cannot be resolved into stable enantiomers. The nitrogen flips rapidly: the lone pair sweeps through the plane of the three substituents and reappears on the other side. The barrier is only about $25\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, small enough for thermal motion at room temperature. The two enantiomers interconvert many times per second, so a bulk sample is racemic.

Two situations restore configurational stability:

- **Quaternary ammonium salts**, $\mathrm{R_4N^+}$, have four groups and no lone pair. They cannot invert and can be resolved.
- **Constrained rings** such as aziridines slow the flip enough for enantiomers to be separated at low temperature.

<learning-block type="concept" title="Degree is about nitrogen">

The label primary, secondary or tertiary describes how many carbon groups are bonded **to nitrogen**, not how branched the carbon skeleton is. 2-methylpropan-2-amine is a primary amine even though its carbon skeleton is highly branched: its nitrogen carries one carbon group and two hydrogens.

</learning-block>

## 2. Classification

| Class | General formula | Example | Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary ($1^\circ$) | $\mathrm{RNH_2}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2NH_2}$ | ethanamine |
| Secondary ($2^\circ$) | $\mathrm{R_2NH}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3NHCH_2CH_3}$ | N-methylethanamine |
| Tertiary ($3^\circ$) | $\mathrm{R_3N}$ | $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}$ | N,N-dimethylmethanamine |
| Quaternary ($4^\circ$) | $\mathrm{R_4N^+X^-}$ | $\mathrm{(CH_3)_4N^+Cl^-}$ | tetramethylammonium chloride |

A second classification cuts across the first:

- **Aliphatic amines** have only alkyl groups on nitrogen, for example ethylamine.
- **Aromatic amines** have the nitrogen bonded directly to an aromatic ring, for example aniline, $\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}$.
- **Mixed amines** contain both, for example N-methylaniline.
- **Heterocyclic amines** have nitrogen inside a ring. Pyridine's lone pair lies in an $sp^2$ orbital in the ring plane and is available for protonation; pyrrole's lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet and is essentially non-basic. That contrast is a useful reminder that the lone pair's environment, not the word "amine", controls basicity.

## 3. Nomenclature

For IUPAC names, take the longest carbon chain attached to nitrogen, drop the final *-e* of the alkane name, and add **-amine**. Number the chain so that the carbon bearing nitrogen gets the lowest locant.

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ | methanamine | methylamine |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2NH_2}$ | ethanamine | ethylamine |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2NH_2}$ | propan-1-amine | n-propylamine |
| $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHNH_2}$ | propan-2-amine | isopropylamine |
| $\mathrm{CH_3NHCH_3}$ | N-methylmethanamine | dimethylamine |
| $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2NCH_2CH_3}$ | N,N-dimethylethanamine | — |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}$ | benzenamine | aniline |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5NHCH_3}$ | N-methylbenzenamine | N-methylaniline |

Substituents on nitrogen are listed with the locant **N**, and repeated substituents take the prefix di- or tri-: N,N-dimethylethanamine. When the amine is not the senior group, it appears as the prefix **amino**. Thus $\mathrm{H_2NCH_2COOH}$ is 2-aminoethanoic acid (glycine), because the carboxylic acid outranks the amine.

Aromatic amines with a methyl group on the ring are called toluidines; the position is shown as o-, m- or p- in common names, or by a number in IUPAC names.

## 4. Formula and degree of unsaturation

For a formula containing carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and halogen, the degree of unsaturation is

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{2C+2+N-H-X}{2},
$$

where $C$, $N$, $H$ and $X$ are the numbers of carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen and halogen atoms. Oxygen and sulphur are ignored because they are divalent and do not change the hydrogen count needed for a saturated skeleton.

For a saturated acyclic amine, $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+3}N}$, substitution gives

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{2n+2+1-(2n+3)}{2}=0.
$$

So every extra ring or π bond raises the count by one, exactly as for hydrocarbons. Aniline, $\mathrm{C_6H_7N}$, has

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{12+2+1-7}{2}=4,
$$

which matches one benzene ring (three π bonds plus one ring).

## 5. Worked example: isomers of $\mathrm{C_4H_{11}N}$

**Step 1 — check the formula.** With $C=4$, $N=1$, $H=11$ and no halogen,

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{8+2+1-11}{2}=0.
$$

There are no rings and no double bonds, so every isomer is an acyclic saturated amine.

**Step 2 — count by class.**

- Primary amines, $\mathrm{C_4H_9NH_2}$: butan-1-amine, butan-2-amine, 2-methylpropan-1-amine and 2-methylpropan-2-amine. That is **4**.
- Secondary amines, $\mathrm{R_2NH}$ with the two alkyl groups adding to four carbons: N-methylpropan-1-amine, N-methylpropan-2-amine and N-ethylethanamine. That is **3**.
- Tertiary amines: N,N-dimethylethanamine only. That is **1**.

**Step 3 — total.** $4+3+1=8$ structural isomers. Every one has one nitrogen, no ring and no π bond, so all eight satisfy the same formula. This is a useful check: if a proposed structure has a double bond or a ring, its formula would have two fewer hydrogens.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Counting isomers by carbon skeleton instead of by nitrogen">

A common error is to list only the carbon skeletons — butan-1-amine, butan-2-amine and their branched versions — and stop at four. That counts primary amines only. For $\mathrm{C_4H_{11}N}$ you must also move carbon groups onto nitrogen: three secondary amines and one tertiary amine complete the set of eight. The class of an amine is fixed by the number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen, so always count by class ($1^\circ$, $2^\circ$, $3^\circ$) after finding the degree of unsaturation.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Classify and name $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHNHCH_3}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The nitrogen is bonded to two carbon groups: an isopropyl group and a methyl group. It is therefore a **secondary** amine. The longest chain containing the nitrogen-bearing carbon is propane, and the methyl group on nitrogen is indicated with an N locant. The name is **N-methylpropan-2-amine**.

</details>

**B.** A compound has molecular formula $\mathrm{C_3H_9N}$. Find its degree of unsaturation and count its structural isomers.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{2(3)+2+1-9}{2}=0.
$$

The three acyclic saturated amines are propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine and N-methylethanamine. No other arrangement of three carbons and one nitrogen avoids a ring or a double bond, so there are **3** isomers.

</details>

**C.** Why is a simple chiral amine such as N-methylethanamine not resolvable into stable enantiomers, while a quaternary ammonium salt with four different groups is?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The secondary amine has a lone pair and undergoes rapid pyramidal inversion, interconverting the two configurations. The quaternary salt has four bonds and no lone pair, so it cannot invert; its configuration is fixed and its enantiomers can be separated.

</details>

## Remember

- Amines are ammonia derivatives; the nitrogen lone pair is the functional centre and the source of basicity and nucleophilicity.
- The degree of an amine counts carbon groups bonded to nitrogen, not branches in the carbon skeleton.
- IUPAC names use the longest chain plus **-amine**, with N locants for substituents on nitrogen.
- The degree-of-unsaturation formula must include nitrogen; a saturated acyclic amine has $\text{DoU}=0$.
- Simple chiral amines racemise by nitrogen inversion, but quaternary ammonium salts do not.
