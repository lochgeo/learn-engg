## Two properties, one lone pair

An amine's boiling point and its basicity look like separate facts, but both come from the same nitrogen. The **N–H bond** is polar enough to form hydrogen bonds, which raises boiling points and helps small amines dissolve in water. The **lone pair** accepts a proton, which makes the amine a base.

The two properties can pull in opposite directions. Adding alkyl groups makes the lone pair more electron-rich, so basicity should rise. But the same groups remove N–H hydrogens, so hydrogen bonding and solvation weaken. The observed order in water is a compromise between these effects, and that compromise is the main source of exam errors.

## 1. Physical state and boiling points

Methanamine, ethanamine and trimethylamine are gases at room temperature. Propan-1-amine and most higher amines are liquids, and aniline is a liquid of boiling point about $184^\circ\text{C}$.

Amines hydrogen-bond through N–H···N contacts. The strength of that network depends on how many N–H bonds remain:

| Amine | Class | Boiling point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| propan-1-amine | $1^\circ$ | $48^\circ\text{C}$ |
| N-methylethanamine | $2^\circ$ | about $37^\circ\text{C}$ |
| trimethylamine | $3^\circ$ | $3^\circ\text{C}$ |

All three have the same formula, $\mathrm{C_3H_9N}$, and similar molar masses. Propan-1-amine has two N–H bonds and forms an extensive network; the secondary amine has one; trimethylamine has none and relies only on dipole–dipole forces. The boiling point falls in the same order.

Amines boil **lower than alcohols of similar molar mass**. Propan-1-amine boils at $48^\circ\text{C}$ while propan-1-ol boils at $97^\circ\text{C}$. The O–H bond is more polar than N–H, so alcohol hydrogen bonds are stronger.

### Solubility

Small amines mix freely with water because their N–H bonds donate hydrogen bonds to water and the lone pair accepts hydrogen bonds from water. Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon part grows: the alkyl group cannot hydrogen-bond and disrupts the water network. Aniline is only sparingly soluble in water, although it dissolves readily in dilute acid because it is converted into the soluble anilinium ion.

## 2. Basicity in aqueous solution

An amine accepts a proton from water:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+H_2O \rightleftharpoons RNH_3^++OH^-}.
$$

The base-dissociation constant is

$$
K_b=\frac{[\mathrm{RNH_3^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{RNH_2}]},
$$

and

$$
pK_b=-\log K_b.
$$

A **larger** $K_b$ means a stronger base; a **smaller** $pK_b$ means a stronger base. For the conjugate acid $\mathrm{RNH_3^+}$,

$$
pK_a+pK_b=14
$$

at $25^\circ\text{C}$ in water. A stronger base therefore has a **larger** $pK_a$ for its conjugate acid. This second route is often more convenient because $pK_a$ values of ammonium ions are widely tabulated.

| Base | $pK_b$ | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2NH}$ | 3.27 | strongest of the methylamines |
| $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ | 3.36 | strong |
| $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}$ | 4.22 | weaker than methylamine |
| $\mathrm{NH_3}$ | 4.75 | weak |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}$ (aniline) | 9.38 | very weak |
| $p$-nitroaniline | about 13.0 | extremely weak |

## 3. Why the methylamine order is not the inductive order

In the gas phase, where solvation is absent, the order is exactly what the inductive effect predicts:

$$
\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N>(CH_3)_2NH>CH_3NH_2>NH_3}.
$$

Each methyl group pushes electron density toward nitrogen and stabilises the positive charge on the conjugate acid.

In water the order changes:

$$
\mathrm{(CH_3)_2NH>CH_3NH_2>(CH_3)_3N>NH_3}.
$$

Three effects compete:

- **Inductive donation** by alkyl groups increases electron density on nitrogen and favours protonation.
- **Solvation** of the conjugate acid requires N–H bonds. As methyl groups replace hydrogen, the cation can form fewer hydrogen bonds with water and is less stabilised.
- **Steric hindrance** makes it harder for a proton and water molecules to approach a heavily substituted nitrogen.

Dimethylamine wins because it has two electron-donating methyl groups and still one N–H bond for solvation. Trimethylamine has the most inductive donation but no N–H bond in its conjugate acid, so it falls below methylamine. The order is a balance, not a monotonic trend.

## 4. Aromatic amines

Aniline is about five orders of magnitude weaker as a base than ammonia. The reason is **resonance**, not the inductive effect of the ring. The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the aromatic π system, placing negative charge on the ortho and para carbons:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 \leftrightarrow \text{resonance structures with } N^+ \text{ and ring } C^-}.
$$

When aniline accepts a proton, the lone pair becomes a bonding pair in the N–H bond and can no longer be delocalised. Protonation therefore destroys the resonance stabilisation of the free base, so the equilibrium lies further to the left.

Cyclohexylamine, $\mathrm{C_6H_{11}NH_2}$, has the same number of carbons but no aromatic ring. Its $pK_b$ is about $3.3$, close to an aliphatic amine. The comparison isolates the effect: it is the ring's π system, not the size of the group, that makes aniline weak.

### Substituent effects on aniline

Electron-donating groups on the ring increase basicity; electron-withdrawing groups decrease it.

- $p$-toluidine, with a methyl group, has $pK_b$ about $8.9$: slightly stronger than aniline.
- $p$-nitroaniline has $pK_b$ about $13.0$: much weaker.
- $m$-nitroaniline has $pK_b$ about $11.4$: weaker than aniline but stronger than the para isomer.

The position matters. A para nitro group can withdraw electron density by both the inductive effect and resonance, and it can delocalise the nitrogen lone pair directly. A meta nitro group can withdraw only inductively, so its effect is smaller.

## 5. Worked example: pH of a methylamine solution

Find the pH of $0.10\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ aqueous methylamine at $25^\circ\text{C}$, given $K_b=4.4\times10^{-4}$.

**Set up the equilibrium.** Let $x$ be the concentration of $\mathrm{OH^-}$ formed.

| Species | Initial | Change | Equilibrium |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}$ | $0.10$ | $-x$ | $0.10-x$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3NH_3^+}$ | $0$ | $+x$ | $x$ |
| $\mathrm{OH^-}$ | $0$ | $+x$ | $x$ |

**Write the equilibrium expression:**

$$
K_b=\frac{x^2}{0.10-x}=4.4\times10^{-4}.
$$

**Solve the quadratic:**

$$
x^2+(4.4\times10^{-4})x-4.4\times10^{-5}=0,
$$

$$
x=\frac{-4.4\times10^{-4}+\sqrt{(4.4\times10^{-4})^2+4(4.4\times10^{-5})}}{2}=6.42\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

**Find the pH:**

$$
pOH=-\log(6.42\times10^{-3})=2.19,
$$

$$
pH=14.00-2.19=11.81.
$$

**Sanity check.** The approximation $x\approx\sqrt{K_bC}$ would give $x=6.63\times10^{-3}$, only about $3\%$ away, but $x/C=6.4\%$ is larger than the usual $5\%$ test, so the quadratic is the safer choice. A pH of $11.8$ is reasonable for a moderately weak base: below the $14$ of a strong base such as sodium hydroxide and well above $7$.

## 6. Comparing base strengths from $pK_a$ values

The conjugate acid of aniline, $\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_3^+}$, has $pK_a=4.6$. The conjugate acid of methylamine, $\mathrm{CH_3NH_3^+}$, has $pK_a=10.6$. Since the stronger base has the stronger conjugate acid,

$$
\frac{K_b(\mathrm{CH_3NH_2})}{K_b(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2})}=10^{10.6-4.6}=10^6.
$$

Methylamine is about a million times stronger as a base. The calculation uses only the difference in $pK_a$ values, so it is a quick way to compare any two amines whose conjugate-acid $pK_a$ values are known.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Basicity errors to avoid">

- **Treating a larger $pK_b$ as stronger.** The scale runs the other way: smaller $pK_b$ means stronger base.
- **Assuming more alkyl groups always mean more basic in water.** Trimethylamine is weaker than dimethylamine and methylamine because its conjugate acid is poorly solvated.
- **Forgetting resonance in aniline.** The lone pair is not localised on nitrogen; it is partly in the ring.
- **Confusing solubility with basicity.** Aniline dissolves in acid because it forms a salt, not because it is a strong base.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Arrange propan-1-amine, N-methylethanamine and trimethylamine in order of increasing boiling point.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Trimethylamine (no N–H) < N-methylethanamine (one N–H) < propan-1-amine (two N–H). All have formula $\mathrm{C_3H_9N}$, so the difference is the extent of hydrogen bonding, not molar mass.

</details>

**B.** Which is the stronger base: aniline or cyclohexylamine? Explain in one or two sentences.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Cyclohexylamine is much stronger ($pK_b\approx3.3$ versus $9.4$ for aniline). Its nitrogen is $sp^3$ and not attached to a π system, so the lone pair is localised and available. In aniline the lone pair is delocalised into the aromatic ring, and protonation would destroy that stabilisation.

</details>

**C.** Find the pH of $0.010\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ aniline, given $K_b=3.8\times10^{-10}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The base is so weak that $x$ is tiny compared with $0.010$, so

$$
x\approx\sqrt{K_bC}=\sqrt{(3.8\times10^{-10})(0.010)}=1.95\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

Then $pOH=-\log(1.95\times10^{-6})=5.71$ and $pH=14.00-5.71=8.29$. The approximation is safe because $x/C\approx0.02\%$.

</details>

## Remember

- For isomeric amines, boiling point falls as N–H bonds are removed: $1^\circ>2^\circ>3^\circ$.
- Amines boil below alcohols of similar mass because N–H hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H hydrogen bonds.
- In water, $(CH_3)_2NH>CH_3NH_2>(CH_3)_3N>NH_3$: induction and solvation compete.
- Aniline is a weak base because its lone pair is delocalised into the aromatic ring.
- Smaller $pK_b$, or larger conjugate-acid $pK_a$, means a stronger base.
