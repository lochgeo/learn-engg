## Mastery check

Try each question without looking at the answer first.

### 1. Classify and name

Classify and name $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHNHCH_3}$ and $\mathrm{(CH_3)_4N^+Br^-}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHNHCH_3}$ has two carbon groups on nitrogen, so it is a **secondary** amine. The longest chain containing the nitrogen-bearing carbon is propane, and the methyl group on nitrogen takes an N locant: **N-methylpropan-2-amine**.

$\mathrm{(CH_3)_4N^+Br^-}$ has four methyl groups on nitrogen and no lone pair, so it is a **quaternary ammonium salt**: **tetramethylammonium bromide**.

</details>

### 2. Degree of unsaturation and isomers

For $\mathrm{C_3H_9N}$, find the degree of unsaturation and the number of structural isomers.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{2(3)+2+1-9}{2}=0.
$$

There are no rings or π bonds. The three saturated amines are propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine and N-methylethanamine. **Three isomers.**

</details>

### 3. Order of basicity

Arrange in increasing order of basicity in water: ethylamine, ammonia, aniline, p-nitroaniline.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$$
p\text{-nitroaniline}<\text{aniline}<\text{ammonia}<\text{ethylamine}.
$$

Ethylamine is a typical aliphatic amine and is stronger than ammonia because the ethyl group donates electron density. Aniline is much weaker than ammonia because its lone pair is delocalised into the ring. The para nitro group withdraws electron density further and stabilises the free base relative to the conjugate acid, so p-nitroaniline is the weakest of the four.

</details>

### 4. Choose a preparation

(a) How would you convert 1-bromopropane into butan-1-amine? (b) How would you convert propanamide into ethanamine?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) Treat 1-bromopropane (3 C) with potassium cyanide to make butanenitrile (4 C), then reduce with lithium aluminium hydride to butan-1-amine (4 C). The nitrile route adds exactly one carbon.

(b) Use Hofmann bromamide degradation: bromine and concentrated sodium hydroxide convert propanamide (3 C) into ethanamine (2 C), with the carbonyl carbon lost as carbonate.

</details>

### 5. Hofmann degradation yield

Butanamide, $\mathrm{C_3H_7CONH_2}$, has molar mass $87.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. A $1.74\ \text{g}$ sample is treated with bromine and alkali, and propan-1-amine, $M=59.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, is obtained in $80\%$ yield. Find the mass isolated.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$n(\text{amide})=1.74/87.1=2.00\times10^{-2}\ \text{mol}$. One mole of amide gives one mole of amine, so the theoretical mass is

$$
2.00\times10^{-2}\times59.1=1.18\ \text{g}.
$$

At $80\%$ yield, the isolated mass is $0.80\times1.18=0.945\ \text{g}$, or **$0.94\ \text{g}$** to two significant figures.

Check the carbon count: butanamide has four carbons and propan-1-amine has three, so one carbon has been lost as carbonate.

</details>

### 6. pH of an aniline solution

Find the pH of $0.050\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ aniline at $25^\circ\text{C}$, given $K_b=3.8\times10^{-10}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Aniline is a very weak base, so the approximation $x\approx\sqrt{K_bC}$ is safe:

$$
x=\sqrt{(3.8\times10^{-10})(0.050)}=\sqrt{1.9\times10^{-11}}=4.36\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

Then $pOH=-\log(4.36\times10^{-6})=5.36$ and $pH=14.00-5.36=8.64$. The answer lies between $7$ and $14$, as a weak base should.

</details>

### 7. Predict the products

Give the main organic product for (a) aniline with bromine water, (b) aniline with chloroform and alcoholic potassium hydroxide, (c) aniline with sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid at $0$–$5^\circ\text{C}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) **2,4,6-Tribromoaniline**, a white precipitate. The amino group is strongly activating and ortho/para directing, so all three positions react.

(b) **Phenyl isocyanide** (phenyl carbylamine). The foul smell is the basis of the carbylamine test for primary amines.

(c) **Benzenediazonium chloride**. The cold temperature stabilises the diazonium ion; warming it would give phenol instead.

</details>

### 8. The Hinsberg test

How would you use benzenesulphonyl chloride to distinguish a primary, a secondary and a tertiary amine?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

- The **primary** amine gives an N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide with one acidic N–H remaining, so the product dissolves in aqueous alkali.
- The **secondary** amine gives an N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide with no N–H, so the product is insoluble in alkali.
- The **tertiary** amine has no N–H and does not react at all.

The three outcomes are: soluble, insoluble, and no reaction.

</details>

### 9. Design a synthesis

Design a three-step synthesis of m-bromoaniline from benzene.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

1. Nitrate benzene with concentrated nitric and sulphuric acids to give nitrobenzene.
2. Brominate nitrobenzene with $\mathrm{Br_2/FeBr_3}$. The nitro group is meta directing, so the product is m-bromonitrobenzene.
3. Reduce the nitro group with hydrogen over nickel, or with tin and hydrochloric acid followed by base, to give m-bromoaniline.

The key is that the bromine is introduced while the nitrogen is still a meta-directing nitro group. Reducing first would give an ortho/para-directing amino group and the wrong isomer.

</details>

### 10. Diazonium conditions

Why is diazotisation carried out cold and in excess acid, and what happens if the solution is warmed?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Cold conditions ($273$–$278\ \text{K}$) prevent the diazonium ion from decomposing; above about $5^\circ\text{C}$ it loses nitrogen and is hydrolysed to phenol. Excess acid keeps nitrous acid available, stabilises the diazonium ion, and protonates any unreacted aniline so that it cannot couple with the diazonium salt. Warming the solution deliberately is the standard way to convert the diazonium group into $-\mathrm{OH}$, giving phenol.

</details>
