## A nucleophile with a proton to spare

Every reaction in this module starts with the same question: is the nitrogen acting as a **base** (taking a proton) or as a **nucleophile** (attacking an electron-poor carbon)? Salt formation is pure acid–base chemistry. Acylation and alkylation are nucleophilic substitutions. The tests with nitrous acid and benzenesulphonyl chloride exploit the number of N–H bonds.

Once you can place a reaction in one of those categories, the products follow from ordinary mechanism rather than from memorised lists.

## 1. Salt formation with acids

Amines neutralise acids:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+HCl \rightarrow RNH_3^+Cl^-}.
$$

The salt is ionic and usually soluble in water, even when the free amine is not. This is the basis of a common separation: aniline, which is only sparingly soluble in water, dissolves completely in dilute hydrochloric acid as anilinium chloride. Adding sodium hydroxide reverses the reaction and liberates the free amine:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_3^+Cl^-+NaOH \rightarrow RNH_2+NaCl+H_2O}.
$$

The same trick purifies an amine: dissolve it in acid, extract neutral impurities, then basify the aqueous layer and extract the purified amine.

## 2. Alkylation

The free amine can attack an alkyl halide:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+R'X \rightarrow RR'NH+HX}.
$$

The product is a secondary amine, which can react again. Successive alkylation gives a tertiary amine and finally a quaternary ammonium salt. This is the same over-alkylation problem that limits ammonolysis, and it is why alkylation is not a clean way to make a single amine unless the starting amine is used in large excess or the product is removed.

Quaternary salts are useful in their own right as phase-transfer catalysts and cationic surfactants. They have no lone pair, so they are not basic.

## 3. Acylation

Acid chlorides and acid anhydrides convert amines into amides:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+R'COCl \rightarrow R'CONHR+HCl},
$$

$$
\mathrm{2RNH_2+(R'CO)_2O \rightarrow 2R'CONHR+H_2O}.
$$

The N–H of the amide is only weakly acidic, and the nitrogen lone pair is now delocalised into the carbonyl group. The product is therefore **much less basic and much less nucleophilic** than the starting amine. That change is the point of acylation in synthesis: it protects the amine while other reactions are carried out on the molecule, and it can be reversed by hydrolysis.

Aniline with acetyl chloride gives acetanilide, $\mathrm{C_6H_5NHCOCH_3}$. Acetanilide is a solid and is far less easily oxidised than aniline, which makes it a convenient protected form for ring substitution.

## 4. Reaction with aldehydes and ketones

Primary amines condense with carbonyl compounds to give imines (Schiff bases):

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+R'CHO \rightarrow R'CH{=}NR+H_2O}.
$$

The mechanism is nucleophilic addition of the amine to the carbonyl carbon, followed by proton transfer and loss of water. The C=N bond is the new feature. Imines are hydrolysed back to the amine and carbonyl compound by aqueous acid, so the reaction is reversible and is driven forward by removing water.

Secondary amines cannot form a stable C=N bond because they have no N–H hydrogen to lose after addition. They stop at the addition stage unless a different leaving group is present.

## 5. Reaction with nitrous acid

Sodium nitrite with hydrochloric acid generates nitrous acid, $\mathrm{HNO_2}$, and the nitrosonium ion, $\mathrm{NO^+}$. The product depends on the class of amine and on whether the amine is aliphatic or aromatic.

### Primary aliphatic amines

The initially formed diazonium salt is unstable and decomposes immediately, releasing nitrogen and giving a mixture of alcohols and alkenes:

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2 \xrightarrow{NaNO_2/HCl} RN_2^+ \rightarrow ROH+\text{other products}+N_2}.
$$

Because the mixture is not clean, this reaction is a test for nitrogen evolution rather than a preparative route.

### Primary aromatic amines

Aniline reacts at $273$–$278\ \text{K}$ to give a **stable diazonium salt**:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2+NaNO_2+2HCl \xrightarrow{273-278\ \text{K}} C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^-+NaCl+2H_2O}.
$$

The aromatic ring stabilises the diazonium ion by delocalisation, and the low temperature prevents decomposition. This is the gateway to the next module.

### Secondary amines

Both aliphatic and aromatic secondary amines give N-nitrosamines, usually yellow oily liquids:

$$
\mathrm{R_2NH+HNO_2 \rightarrow R_2N{-}NO+H_2O}.
$$

### Tertiary amines

Aliphatic tertiary amines have no N–H bond and simply form nitrite salts, which are soluble and give no visible change. Aromatic tertiary amines can undergo electrophilic nitrosation at the para position of the ring, giving a green or blue nitroso compound.

This pattern makes nitrous acid a diagnostic reagent: gas from a primary amine, yellow oil from a secondary amine, and little visible change from an aliphatic tertiary amine.

## 6. The carbylamine test

A primary amine heated with chloroform and alcoholic potassium hydroxide gives an isocyanide (carbylamine):

$$
\mathrm{RNH_2+CHCl_3+3KOH \rightarrow RNC+3KCl+3H_2O}.
$$

Isocyanides have a powerful, unpleasant smell even at very low concentration. Secondary and tertiary amines do not react because the test requires two N–H hydrogens to eliminate as water and hydrogen chloride. The carbylamine test therefore distinguishes a primary amine from the other two classes.

## 7. The Hinsberg test

Benzenesulphonyl chloride, $\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2Cl}$, reacts with amines to give sulphonamides:

- A **primary amine** gives N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide. The N–H remaining on the sulphonamide is acidic enough to dissolve in aqueous alkali as a soluble salt.
- A **secondary amine** gives N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide. There is no N–H left, so it does not dissolve in alkali.
- A **tertiary amine** has no N–H and does not react.

The test is summarised below.

| Amine | Product with $\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2Cl}$ | Solubility in alkali |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1^\circ$ | N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide | soluble |
| $2^\circ$ | N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide | insoluble |
| $3^\circ$ | no reaction | — |

## 8. Ring substitution in aniline

The $-\mathrm{NH_2}$ group is strongly activating and ortho/para directing. Bromine water reacts with aniline immediately to give a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromoaniline:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2+3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3NH_2+3HBr}.
$$

To stop at one bromine, the amine is first converted into acetanilide. The acetamido group is less strongly activating, so bromination in acetic acid gives mainly p-bromoacetanilide; hydrolysis then liberates p-bromoaniline.

The same protection strategy is needed for nitration. Direct nitration of aniline with concentrated nitric and sulphuric acids protonates the amine. The anilinium ion is strongly deactivating and meta directing, so a significant amount of m-nitroaniline forms. Nitrating acetanilide instead, then hydrolysing, gives p-nitroaniline cleanly.

Sulphonation is an exception: aniline with concentrated sulphuric acid gives sulphanilic acid, $p$-aminobenzenesulphonic acid, which exists mainly as a zwitterion.

## 9. Worked example: acylation yield

Aniline, $\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}$, has molar mass $93.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. Acetanilide, $\mathrm{C_6H_5NHCOCH_3}$, has molar mass $135.2\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. A $9.30\ \text{g}$ sample of aniline is treated with excess acetyl chloride and $11.5\ \text{g}$ of acetanilide is isolated. Find the percentage yield.

**Moles of aniline:**

$$
n=\frac{9.30}{93.1}=9.99\times10^{-2}\ \text{mol}.
$$

**Theoretical mass of acetanilide.** One mole of aniline gives one mole of acetanilide:

$$
m_{\text{theoretical}}=(9.99\times10^{-2})(135.2)=13.5\ \text{g}.
$$

**Percentage yield:**

$$
\text{yield}=\frac{11.5}{13.5}\times100\%=85.2\%.
$$

**Sanity check.** The product has gained the acetyl group, $\mathrm{COCH_3}$, and lost hydrogen: $135.2-93.1=42.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, close to the molar mass of $\mathrm{C_2H_2O}$ ($42.0$). The yield is below $100\%$, as it must be for an isolated product.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Reaction errors to avoid">

- **Expecting a stable diazonium salt from a primary aliphatic amine.** It decomposes at once; only aromatic diazonium salts are stable enough to isolate at low temperature.
- **Brominating aniline directly and expecting a monobromo product.** The strongly activating amine gives the tribromo compound unless it is protected.
- **Nitrating aniline without protection.** Protonation gives the meta-directing anilinium ion and a mixture rich in m-nitroaniline.
- **Using the Hinsberg test on a tertiary amine and expecting a sulphonamide.** There is no N–H to react.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** How would you distinguish ethylamine from diethylamine using one reagent?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Use the Hinsberg test. Ethylamine, a primary amine, gives a sulphonamide that dissolves in alkali. Diethylamine, a secondary amine, gives a sulphonamide that is insoluble in alkali. The carbylamine test also works: only the primary amine gives the foul-smelling isocyanide.

</details>

**B.** Aniline is treated with sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid at $0$–$5^\circ\text{C}$, then the solution is warmed. What is the organic product?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The cold step forms benzenediazonium chloride. Warming decomposes it with loss of nitrogen; water attacks the aryl cation, giving **phenol**. The overall transformation is aniline → phenol through the diazonium intermediate.

</details>

**C.** Why is acetanilide a much weaker base than aniline?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

In acetanilide the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the adjacent carbonyl group, giving a resonance stabilised amide. Protonation would remove that delocalisation, so the amide is far less basic than the amine. The same delocalisation also makes the nitrogen a poor nucleophile.

</details>

## Remember

- Amines form soluble salts with acids; basification regenerates the free amine.
- Acylation protects the amine and sharply reduces its basicity and nucleophilicity.
- Nitrous acid gives gas with $1^\circ$ aliphatic, a yellow oil with $2^\circ$, and little change with $3^\circ$ aliphatic amines.
- The carbylamine and Hinsberg tests distinguish primary, secondary and tertiary amines.
- Aniline needs protection before bromination or nitration if a single substituent is wanted.

The aromatic diazonium salt deserves its own module: it is unstable, but it is also the most versatile intermediate in aromatic chemistry.
