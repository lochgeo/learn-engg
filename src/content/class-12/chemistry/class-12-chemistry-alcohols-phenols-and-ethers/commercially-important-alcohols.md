## From the laboratory to the factory

The alcohols in this chapter are not only exam compounds. Methanol and ethanol are made in millions of tonnes each year; ethane-1,2-diol keeps car engines from freezing; glycerol is a by-product of soap and biodiesel; and phenol is the raw material for resins, plastics and pharmaceuticals. The industrial routes are chosen for reasons that a laboratory chemist can recognise: cheap feedstocks, few steps, high atom economy and manageable hazards.

This module looks at the commercial picture for each family. The chemistry should already be familiar from the previous modules; what is new is the scale, the uses and the safety consequences that follow from those uses.

## 1. Methanol

Methanol, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$, is the simplest alcohol. It is a colourless, volatile liquid with a boiling point of $65^\circ\text{C}$, miscible with water and many organic solvents.

Industrially it is made from synthesis gas, a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen obtained from natural gas or coal:

$$
\mathrm{CO + 2H_2 \xrightarrow{Cu/ZnO/Al_2O_3,\ 573{-}673\ \text{K},\ 50{-}100\ \text{atm}} CH_3OH}.
$$

The modern low-pressure process uses a copper–zinc oxide–alumina catalyst. Older plants used zinc oxide–chromium oxide at much higher pressure, but the copper catalyst is more selective and cheaper to operate.

Methanol is mainly used as a chemical feedstock rather than as a fuel. It is oxidised to methanal (formaldehyde), which is used in resins and adhesives; carbonylated to ethanoic acid; converted to methyl *tert*-butyl ether; and used to make biodiesel. It is also a solvent and a possible fuel for fuel cells.

The hazard is severe. Methanol is metabolised in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase to methanal, which is then oxidised to methanoic acid. Methanal damages the optic nerve and methanoic acid causes metabolic acidosis. Small volumes can cause blindness or death. The treatment is to give ethanol or fomepizole, both of which compete for the enzyme and slow the formation of the toxic metabolites. This is why industrial ethanol is deliberately denatured with methanol and other unpleasant substances: it makes the ethanol unfit to drink and avoids beverage excise duty, but it also makes the mixture dangerous.

## 2. Ethanol

Ethanol, $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$, boils at $78^\circ\text{C}$ and is miscible with water. It is made by fermentation or by hydration of ethene.

Fermentation uses yeast enzymes to convert glucose:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \xrightarrow{zymase} 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2}.
$$

The reaction is carried out near $298$–$310\ \text{K}$ in aqueous solution. Yeast is inhibited once the ethanol concentration reaches about $15\%$ by volume, so fermentation alone cannot produce strong spirits. Distillation is needed to concentrate the ethanol, and fractional distillation can reach about $95\%$; the remaining water is removed by azeotropic distillation or by drying agents to give absolute ethanol.

Industrial ethanol is also made by catalytic hydration of ethene:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H_3PO_4/SiO_2,\ 573\ \text{K},\ 60{-}70\ \text{atm}} CH_3CH_2OH}.
$$

This route is faster and gives a more concentrated product, but it depends on a petrochemical feedstock. Fermentation is preferred where agricultural sugar or starch is cheap.

Ethanol is used as a solvent, in alcoholic beverages, as an antiseptic, and as a fuel. Petrol blends are labelled by the percentage of ethanol by volume: E10 contains $10\%$ ethanol, E85 contains $85\%$. Ethanol raises the octane rating and adds oxygen to the fuel, which improves combustion, but it has a lower energy density than petrol, so a blend gives slightly fewer kilometres per litre. It is also the feedstock for ethanoic acid, ethyl ethanoate and diethyl ether.

The breathalyser test uses the oxidation of ethanol by acidified potassium dichromate. The orange dichromate ion is reduced to green chromium(III), and the colour change is calibrated against the ethanol concentration in the breath.

## 3. Ethane-1,2-diol

Ethane-1,2-diol, $\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2OH}$, is a viscous, water-miscible liquid with a boiling point of $197^\circ\text{C}$. It is made from ethene in two steps. Ethene is oxidised over a silver catalyst to ethylene oxide:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + \frac12O_2 \xrightarrow{Ag,\ 500\ \text{K}} CH_2CH_2O}.
$$

The three-membered epoxide ring is then opened by water to give the diol:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2CH_2O + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} HOCH_2CH_2OH}.
$$

The main use is as an antifreeze and engine coolant. A $50\%$ aqueous solution freezes near $-37^\circ\text{C}$ and raises the boiling point of the coolant, so it protects the engine in both winter and summer. It is also a monomer for polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and is used to dehydrate natural gas.

Ethane-1,2-diol is toxic. It is oxidised in the body to glycolic acid and then to oxalic acid, which precipitates calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys and can cause kidney failure. Like methanol poisoning, it is treated with ethanol or fomepizole to block the first oxidation step. It should never be confused with ethanol, despite the similar name.

## 4. Glycerol

Glycerol, propane-1,2,3-triol, $\mathrm{HOCH_2CH(OH)CH_2OH}$, is a viscous, hygroscopic liquid with a high boiling point (about $290^\circ\text{C}$, with decomposition). It is a trihydric alcohol: three –OH groups on a three-carbon chain.

The traditional source is the saponification of fats and oils. A triglyceride reacts with sodium hydroxide to give soap and glycerol:

$$
\mathrm{C_3H_5(OOCR)_3 + 3NaOH \rightarrow 3RCOONa + C_3H_5(OH)_3}.
$$

Biodiesel production uses the same transesterification, with methanol in place of sodium hydroxide, and glycerol is the main by-product. Glycerol can also be made from propene through allyl chloride and epichlorohydrin, but the fat-based route is cheaper when soap or biodiesel is being made.

Glycerol is used in cosmetics, toothpastes, cough medicines and food as a humectant and sweetener. It is nitrated to glyceryl trinitrate (nitroglycerine), a powerful explosive and a vasodilator used to treat angina. It is also a starting material for alkyd resins and polyurethanes.

## 5. Phenol

Phenol, $\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}$, is a colourless crystalline solid with a characteristic smell and a boiling point of $182^\circ\text{C}$. It is corrosive and toxic, and it turns pink on exposure to air because it is slowly oxidised.

The dominant industrial route is the cumene process described in the preparation module:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + C_3H_6 + O_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + (CH_3)_2CO}.
$$

Phenol is used to make phenol–formaldehyde resins (Bakelite), which were among the first synthetic plastics and are still used in electrical fittings and adhesives. It is also used to make bisphenol A for polycarbonate and epoxy resins, caprolactam for nylon-6, herbicides such as 2,4-D, dyes, and pharmaceuticals. Salicylic acid, the precursor of aspirin, is made from phenol by the Kolbe–Schmitt reaction.

Phenol was once used as an antiseptic under the name carbolic acid. It is still used in some disinfectants, but its toxicity and corrosive action have led to safer substitutes. Picric acid, 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, is a strong acid and an explosive; old bottles of picric acid can be dangerous if they dry out.

## 6. Commercially important ethers

**Diethyl ether** is a volatile, highly flammable liquid with a boiling point of $35^\circ\text{C}$. It was the first widely used surgical anaesthetic, introduced in the 1840s, and it remains a common solvent for Grignard reactions and extractions. Its main hazard is fire, and old samples can contain explosive peroxides.

**Methyl *tert*-butyl ether (MTBE)** is made from methanol and isobutene. It has a high octane rating and adds oxygen to petrol, so it was widely used as a fuel oxygenate. It is water-soluble and persistent, and small amounts can give drinking water an unpleasant taste and smell; for that reason it has been phased out in several countries.

**Anisole** (methoxybenzene) is a pleasant-smelling liquid used as a solvent and as a precursor to perfumes, pharmaceuticals and dyes. Its methoxy group is the same one that appears in many natural products.

## 7. Worked example: ethanol from glucose

A fermentation vessel is charged with $1.00\ \text{kg}$ of glucose. The conversion is $92\%$ complete. Find the mass of ethanol produced and check the mass balance.

The equation is

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2}.
$$

The molar masses are $M(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6})=180.2\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, $M(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=46.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $M(\mathrm{CO_2})=44.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

$$
n(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6})=\frac{1000}{180.2}=5.55\ \text{mol}.
$$

Each mole of glucose gives two moles of ethanol, so

$$
n(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=2(5.55)=11.1\ \text{mol}.
$$

At $100\%$ conversion,

$$
m(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=11.1\times46.1=512\ \text{g}.
$$

At $92\%$ conversion,

$$
m(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=512\times0.92=471\ \text{g}.
$$

The carbon dioxide produced at the same conversion is

$$
m(\mathrm{CO_2})=11.1\times44.0\times0.92=449\ \text{g}.
$$

The products together weigh $471+449=920\ \text{g}$. The original glucose weighed $1000\ \text{g}$, so about $80\ \text{g}$ of carbon and oxygen remains in unreacted glucose and other products. At $100\%$ conversion the two products would weigh $512+488=1000\ \text{g}$, which is the mass-balance check.

At STP, the carbon dioxide would occupy

$$
V(\mathrm{CO_2})=11.1\times22.4\times0.92=229\ \text{L}.
$$

That is a large gas volume for a kilogram of sugar, and it is one reason fermentation vessels need generous venting.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Commercial and safety traps">

- **Treating methanol as a cheap substitute for ethanol.** Methanol is oxidised to methanal and methanoic acid, causing blindness and acidosis. It is not a beverage alcohol and must never be used as one.
- **Assuming fermentation can give pure ethanol.** Yeast is inhibited at about $15\%$ ethanol, and distillation is needed to concentrate the product. Absolute ethanol requires further drying.
- **Confusing ethane-1,2-diol with ethanol.** The names are similar, but the diol is toxic and is used as antifreeze, not as a drink or a solvent for food.
- **Forgetting that phenol and picric acid are hazardous.** Phenol is corrosive and toxic; dry picric acid is explosive. Treat old samples with respect.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Why is methanol deliberately added to industrial ethanol, and why does that make the product more dangerous?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Methanol is added as a denaturant to make the ethanol unfit for drinking, which avoids the taxes and controls that apply to beverage alcohol. It is effective because methanol is toxic and has a similar appearance and smell to ethanol. The danger is that someone may still drink the denatured product, or use it in a way that exposes them to methanol; the same property that makes it a good denaturant makes it a serious poison.
</details>

**2.** A triglyceride has a molar mass of $890\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. Calculate the theoretical mass of glycerol obtained from $1.00\ \text{kg}$ of the triglyceride, assuming one mole of triglyceride gives one mole of glycerol.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The amount of triglyceride is $1000/890=1.124\ \text{mol}$. One mole gives one mole of glycerol, whose molar mass is $92.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. The theoretical mass is $1.124\times92.1=104\ \text{g}$, to three significant figures. The rest of the mass becomes soap or biodiesel and the fatty acid chains.
</details>

**3.** Why does fermentation stop at about $15\%$ ethanol by volume, and how is a stronger product obtained?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Yeast cells are inhibited and eventually killed by ethanol, so the fermentation slows and stops when the concentration reaches roughly $15\%$ by volume. A stronger product is obtained by distilling the dilute ethanol solution. Simple distillation can reach about $95\%$; the remaining water is removed by azeotropic distillation or by a drying agent to give absolute ethanol.
</details>

## Remember

- Methanol is made from synthesis gas and is a major feedstock, but it is highly toxic; it is oxidised to methanal and methanoic acid.
- Ethanol is made by fermentation or ethene hydration and is used as a solvent, fuel and feedstock; fermentation is limited to about $15\%$ ethanol.
- Ethane-1,2-diol is made through ethylene oxide and is used as antifreeze; it is toxic and must not be confused with ethanol.
- Glycerol is a triol obtained from fats and oils and used in cosmetics, resins and nitroglycerine.
- Phenol is made mainly by the cumene process and is used in resins, plastics, herbicides and pharmaceuticals.
- Diethyl ether, MTBE and anisole illustrate the solvent, fuel-additive and precursor roles of ethers.
