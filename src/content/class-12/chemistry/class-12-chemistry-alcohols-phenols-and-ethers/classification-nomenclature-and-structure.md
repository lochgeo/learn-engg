## Three families, one oxygen atom

A single oxygen atom can sit in three very different places in an organic molecule, and that placement decides almost everything about the compound’s behaviour. Attach –OH to a saturated carbon and you have an **alcohol**: a hydrogen-bonding liquid that can be oxidised, dehydrated and converted into a halide. Attach the same –OH directly to a benzene ring and you have a **phenol**: a much more acidic compound that undergoes electrophilic substitution on the ring. Put the oxygen between two carbon groups and you have an **ether**: a comparatively unreactive, low-boiling solvent.

A molecular formula alone does not identify the family. The three isomers with formula $\mathrm{C_3H_8O}$ are propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol and methoxyethane. The first two are alcohols and the third is an ether, yet the alcohols boil $70$–$86\ \text{K}$ above the ether. This lesson builds the vocabulary and the structural picture needed to tell them apart before later modules ask you to predict their reactions.

## 1. Classification by the carbon that carries the –OH

Alcohols are classified by the number of carbon atoms bonded directly to the **carbinol carbon**, the carbon that bears the hydroxyl group.

| Class | Carbon atoms attached to the carbinol carbon | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary ($1^\circ$) | one | propan-1-ol, $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}$ |
| Secondary ($2^\circ$) | two | propan-2-ol, $\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_3}$ |
| Tertiary ($3^\circ$) | three | 2-methylpropan-2-ol, $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3COH}$ |

Methanol, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$, has no other carbon on its carbinol carbon. It is usually treated as a primary alcohol for naming purposes, but it is the simplest member of the family rather than a typical $1^\circ$ alcohol.

A second classification counts the hydroxyl groups. **Monohydric** alcohols have one –OH, **dihydric** alcohols have two, and **trihydric** alcohols have three. Ethane-1,2-diol (glycol) and propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) are the standard dihydric and trihydric examples.

The position of the –OH relative to a double bond or an aromatic ring gives three more labels:

- **Allylic alcohol:** the –OH is on a carbon adjacent to a $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bond, as in prop-2-en-1-ol.
- **Benzylic alcohol:** the –OH is on a carbon adjacent to a benzene ring, as in benzyl alcohol, $\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}$.
- **Vinylic alcohol:** the –OH is on a carbon of the double bond itself. These are **enols**, and simple enols are usually unstable because they rearrange to carbonyl compounds.

**Phenols** are not alcohols. In a phenol the –OH is bonded directly to an $sp^2$ carbon of an aromatic ring. Phenol itself is $\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}$. Dihydric phenols include catechol (benzene-1,2-diol), resorcinol (benzene-1,3-diol) and hydroquinone (benzene-1,4-diol); pyrogallol is a trihydric phenol. The direct ring attachment is what makes phenols acidic, and that is the subject of a later module.

**Ethers** have the general formula $\mathrm{R{-}O{-}R'}$. When the two groups are identical the ether is **simple** or **symmetrical**, as in ethoxyethane, $\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}$. When they differ it is **mixed** or **unsymmetrical**, as in methoxyethane, $\mathrm{CH_3OC_2H_5}$. In anisole the oxygen joins a methyl group to a benzene ring: $\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}$.

## 2. Naming alcohols and phenols

The IUPAC rules follow a fixed order.

1. Select the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon attached to –OH.
2. Number the chain from the end that gives the –OH carbon the lower locant.
3. Drop the final *-e* of the parent alkane and add *-ol*.
4. Name and number substituents, and list them alphabetically.
5. In a cyclic alcohol, the carbon carrying –OH is automatically C1.

Some worked names:

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(OH)CH_3}$ | butan-2-ol | sec-butyl alcohol |
| $\mathrm{(CH_3)_3COH}$ | 2-methylpropan-2-ol | tert-butyl alcohol |
| $\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2CH_2OH}$ | propane-1,3-diol | trimethylene glycol |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}$ | phenol | carbolic acid |
| $\mathrm{CH_3C_6H_4OH}$ (2-methyl) | 2-methylphenol | o-cresol |
| $\mathrm{NO_2C_6H_4OH}$ (4-nitro) | 4-nitrophenol | p-nitrophenol |

For a substituted phenol, the carbon bearing –OH is C1 and the ring is numbered so that substituents receive the lowest possible locants. The old *ortho*, *meta* and *para* labels are still common for 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-disubstitution.

When the –OH is only a substituent on a larger molecule, the prefix **hydroxy** is used instead of the suffix *-ol*. For example, $\mathrm{HOCH_2CH_2COOH}$ is 3-hydroxypropanoic acid, not a “propanoic alcohol”.

## 3. Naming ethers

For IUPAC names, the larger carbon group is the parent chain and the smaller group becomes an **alkoxy** prefix:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3OCH_2CH_2CH_3} \quad\text{is}\quad \text{1-methoxypropane}.
$$

The common style names both groups and adds “ether”:

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}$ | methoxymethane | dimethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_2CH_3}$ | methoxyethane | methyl ethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}$ | ethoxyethane | diethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}$ | methoxybenzene | anisole |

In an aryl ether the ring is the parent and the alkoxy group is the prefix, so $\mathrm{C_6H_5OC_2H_5}$ is ethoxybenzene (phenetole).

## 4. What the oxygen does to shape and bonding

The oxygen in an alcohol or ether is approximately $sp^3$ hybridised, with two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. The result is a **bent** arrangement rather than a straight line:

- the $\mathrm{C{-}O{-}H}$ angle in methanol is about $109^\circ$;
- the $\mathrm{C{-}O{-}C}$ angle in dimethyl ether is about $111^\circ$;
- a typical $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ single bond is about $143\ \text{pm}$ long, shorter than a $\mathrm{C{-}C}$ single bond at about $154\ \text{pm}$.

In phenol the oxygen is often described as $sp^2$-like because one lone pair participates in resonance with the ring. The $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bond shortens to about $136\ \text{pm}$ and gains partial double-bond character. This is an early hint that phenol is not simply “an alcohol with a ring attached”.

The $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bond is polar, with a partial positive charge on carbon and a partial negative charge on oxygen. The $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ bond is also polar, and its hydrogen can be donated to a lone pair on another oxygen. That gives alcohols and phenols **intermolecular hydrogen bonding**. Ethers have no $\mathrm{O{-}H}$, so they can accept hydrogen bonds but cannot donate them.

The consequences are easy to see in boiling points:

| Compound | Molar mass / $\text{g mol}^{-1}$ | Boiling point / $^\circ\text{C}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| propane | 44 | $-42$ |
| dimethyl ether | 46 | $-24$ |
| ethanol | 46 | $78$ |
| propan-1-ol | 60 | $97$ |
| butan-1-ol | 74 | $118$ |

Ethanol and dimethyl ether have almost the same molar mass and comparable dipole moments, yet ethanol boils about $100\ \text{K}$ higher. The difference is the $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ hydrogen bond. Branching lowers the boiling point because it makes the molecules more spherical and reduces the contact area: propan-1-ol boils at $97^\circ\text{C}$ while propan-2-ol boils at $82^\circ\text{C}$.

Solubility follows the same logic. Methanol, ethanol and propan-1-ol mix with water in all proportions because their –OH groups can join the water network. As the hydrocarbon part grows, the molecule becomes more like an alkane and less like water; butan-1-ol is only partly soluble, and hexan-1-ol is nearly insoluble. Ethers are much less soluble in water than the isomeric alcohols, because they cannot donate a hydrogen bond.

## 5. Worked example: one formula, three structures

A compound has the molecular formula $\mathrm{C_3H_8O}$. How many open-chain structures are possible, and what does the formula tell you about unsaturation?

Use the degree-of-unsaturation formula for a compound containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen:

$$
\text{DBE}=\frac{2C+2-H}{2}=\frac{2(3)+2-8}{2}=0.
$$

A DBE of zero means no double bond and no ring. The oxygen must therefore be singly bonded, and the three possible open-chain structures are:

| Structure | Name | Class | Boiling point / $^\circ\text{C}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}$ | propan-1-ol | primary alcohol | $97$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_3}$ | propan-2-ol | secondary alcohol | $82$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_2CH_3}$ | methoxyethane | ether | $11$ |

The molar mass is $3(12.0)+8(1.0)+16.0=60.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ for all three, so mass alone cannot distinguish them. The alcohol isomers have much higher boiling points because each molecule can both donate and accept an $\mathrm{O{-}H}\cdots\mathrm{O}$ hydrogen bond. Methoxyethane can only accept, so its boiling point is close to that of a molecule of similar size with no hydrogen bonding at all.

As a sanity check, the formula is saturated: $C_3H_8$ is propane, and inserting one oxygen into a $\mathrm{C{-}H}$ or $\mathrm{C{-}C}$ relationship gives an alcohol or an ether without changing the hydrogen count. That is exactly what a DBE of zero predicts.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Naming and classification traps">

- **Calling benzyl alcohol a phenol.** In benzyl alcohol, $\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}$, the –OH sits on the side-chain carbon. In phenol it sits directly on the ring. The two have different acidity and completely different reaction patterns.
- **Classifying by the number of hydrogens on the carbinol carbon.** A $3^\circ$ alcohol has no hydrogen on that carbon, but a $2^\circ$ alcohol can have one and a $1^\circ$ alcohol two. Count the carbon groups attached to the carbinol carbon, not the hydrogens.
- **Assuming all alcohols are water-soluble.** Solubility falls sharply as the hydrocarbon chain grows; the –OH group is a small polar handle on an increasingly oily molecule.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Classify each compound as a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol, or as a phenol: butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, benzyl alcohol, phenol.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Butan-2-ol is secondary: the carbinol carbon is attached to two other carbons. 2-Methylpropan-2-ol is tertiary: three carbons are attached to the carbinol carbon. Benzyl alcohol is a primary benzylic alcohol, because the –OH is on the $\mathrm{CH_2}$ group next to the ring. Phenol is not an alcohol: its –OH is bonded directly to an aromatic ring carbon.
</details>

**2.** How many structural isomers of $\mathrm{C_4H_{10}O}$ are alcohols, and how many are ethers? Name them.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

There are four alcohols: butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol. There are three ethers: 1-methoxypropane, 2-methoxypropane and ethoxyethane. The total is seven, consistent with a saturated formula and no rings.
</details>

**3.** Give the IUPAC names of $\mathrm{CH_3OCH(CH_3)_2}$ and $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(OH)CH_3}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{CH_3OCH(CH_3)_2}$ has a three-carbon parent chain with a methoxy group on C2: **2-methoxypropane** (common name methyl isopropyl ether). $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(OH)CH_3}$ has a four-carbon chain and the –OH on C2: **butan-2-ol**.
</details>

## Remember

- The position of oxygen defines the family: –OH on an $sp^3$ carbon is an alcohol, –OH on an arene carbon is a phenol, and $\mathrm{C{-}O{-}C}$ is an ether.
- Classify alcohols as $1^\circ$, $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$ by the number of carbon groups on the carbinol carbon.
- IUPAC alcohol names use the longest chain containing –OH, numbered to give –OH the lowest locant; ethers use an alkoxy prefix on the larger chain.
- Hydrogen bonding explains why alcohols boil far above isomeric ethers and why small alcohols mix with water.
- A molecular formula is only a starting point: always use the degree of unsaturation and the functional group to decide which structures are possible.
