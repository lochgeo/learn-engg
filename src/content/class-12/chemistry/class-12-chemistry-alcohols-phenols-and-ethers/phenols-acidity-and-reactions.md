## A different kind of –OH

Phenol looks like an alcohol with a benzene ring attached, but its chemistry belongs to a different family. Ethanol has a $\mathrm{p}K_a$ near $16$; phenol has a $\mathrm{p}K_a$ near $10$. That factor of a million does not come from the oxygen itself. It comes from what happens to the negative charge after the proton leaves. In an alkoxide the charge sits on one oxygen. In a phenoxide the charge is delocalised around the aromatic ring, and that delocalisation stabilises the ion enough to make the parent acid far stronger.

The same delocalisation changes the ring’s reactivity. The oxygen lone pair donates electron density into the ring, so phenol is strongly activated towards electrophilic substitution and directs new groups to the *ortho* and *para* positions. Acidity and ring reactivity are two consequences of the same electronic conversation between oxygen and arene.

## 1. Why phenol is acidic

The acid–base equilibrium is

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + H_2O \rightleftharpoons C_6H_5O^- + H_3O^+}.
$$

The phenoxide ion, $\mathrm{C_6H_5O^-}$, has four important resonance contributors. The negative charge is not confined to oxygen: it is also placed on the *ortho* and *para* ring carbons, with the *meta* positions left largely unaffected. Spreading charge over several atoms lowers the energy of the ion and shifts the equilibrium to the right.

An alkoxide has no comparable delocalisation. The negative charge remains localised on a single oxygen, so the ion is higher in energy and the alcohol is much less willing to lose its proton. The aromatic ring is therefore not a passive substituent; it is the reason phenol is acidic.

## 2. Comparing acidities

The following values are a useful reference. They are approximate, but the ordering is what matters.

| Compound | $\mathrm{p}K_a$ | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ethanol | $15.9$ | typical alcohol |
| cyclohexanol | $16.0$ | no ring delocalisation |
| phenol | $9.95$ | resonance-stabilised phenoxide |
| 4-methylphenol ($p$-cresol) | $10.26$ | methyl group donates electron density |
| 4-nitrophenol | $7.15$ | nitro group withdraws electron density |
| 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid) | $0.38$ | three nitro groups; strongly acidic |
| ethanoic acid | $4.76$ | carboxylate has two equivalent oxygens |

Phenol is much more acidic than an alcohol but still much less acidic than a carboxylic acid. The carboxylate ion delocalises its negative charge over two equivalent oxygen atoms, and oxygen is better at carrying negative charge than carbon. Phenoxide delocalises charge onto carbon atoms as well, which is helpful but less effective. That is why phenol does not liberate carbon dioxide from sodium bicarbonate, while a carboxylic acid does.

## 3. Substituent effects

Anything that stabilises the phenoxide ion increases acidity; anything that destabilises it decreases acidity.

- **Electron-withdrawing groups** such as $\mathrm{-NO_2}$, $\mathrm{-CN}$ and halogens pull electron density away from the ring and stabilise the negative charge. They make phenol more acidic.
- **Electron-donating groups** such as $\mathrm{-CH_3}$, $\mathrm{-OCH_3}$ and $\mathrm{-NH_2}$ push electron density into the ring and destabilise the phenoxide. They make phenol less acidic.
- **Position matters.** A substituent at the *ortho* or *para* position can delocalise its effect into the same positions that carry the phenoxide charge, so it has a larger influence than the same substituent at *meta*.

For the nitrophenols the order is

$$
4\text{-nitrophenol }(\mathrm{p}K_a\ 7.15) \gtrsim 2\text{-nitrophenol }(\mathrm{p}K_a\ 7.23) > 3\text{-nitrophenol }(\mathrm{p}K_a\ 8.40).
$$

The *para* isomer is slightly more acidic than the *ortho* isomer because the *ortho* –OH can form an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the nitro group, which stabilises the undissociated acid, and because the *ortho* phenoxide is less well solvated. Both effects work against ionisation. The *meta* isomer is least acidic because its nitro group cannot delocalise the charge into the ring positions that bear it.

## 4. Phenol as an acid: NaOH, Na₂CO₃ and NaHCO₃

Phenol dissolves in aqueous sodium hydroxide because the strong base removes the proton completely:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}.
$$

Sodium carbonate is a weaker base. It can deprotonate phenol to a limited extent, giving sodium phenoxide and sodium hydrogen carbonate:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + Na_2CO_3 \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + NaHCO_3}.
$$

No carbon dioxide is evolved in this reaction. Sodium hydrogen carbonate is too weak a base to deprotonate phenol appreciably, because the conjugate acid that would form, carbonic acid, is a much stronger acid than phenol. The practical test is therefore:

| Reagent | Carboxylic acid | Phenol | Alcohol |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NaOH | dissolves | dissolves | no reaction |
| Na₂CO₃ | dissolves, CO₂ evolved | partly dissolves, no CO₂ | no reaction |
| NaHCO₃ | dissolves, CO₂ evolved | no reaction | no reaction |

This table is one of the quickest ways to identify an unknown acidic functional group. A compound that dissolves in NaHCO₃ with effervescence is a carboxylic acid; a compound that dissolves in NaOH but not NaHCO₃ is a phenol.

## 5. Esters and ethers from phenol

Phenol can be converted into an ester, but direct Fischer esterification with a carboxylic acid is slow. The phenoxide ion is a poor nucleophile towards a carbonyl carbon, and the ring is more crowded than an alkyl chain. Acid chlorides and acid anhydrides are more reactive and are normally used:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + (CH_3CO)_2O \rightarrow C_6H_5OCOCH_3 + CH_3COOH}.
$$

The product is phenyl ethanoate (phenyl acetate). The same reaction appears in the synthesis of aspirin: salicylic acid, which is 2-hydroxybenzoic acid, is acetylated with acetic anhydride to give acetylsalicylic acid.

Ether formation uses the Williamson approach. Phenol is first converted to sodium phenoxide, then treated with a primary alkyl halide:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CH_3I \rightarrow C_6H_5OCH_3 + NaI}.
$$

The product is anisole. A tertiary halide would give mainly elimination rather than ether, for the same reason it fails in the aliphatic Williamson synthesis.

## 6. Electrophilic substitution on the ring

The –OH group is strongly activating and *ortho*/*para* directing. It donates a lone pair into the ring, raising the electron density at the positions that can stabilise the resulting positive charge. The ring is therefore much more reactive than benzene, and many substitutions need no Lewis acid catalyst.

**Bromination.** Phenol reacts immediately with bromine water to give a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3OH + 3HBr}.
$$

If a mono-brominated product is wanted, the reaction is run with bromine in carbon disulphide at low temperature; the *para* product predominates for steric reasons.

**Nitration.** Dilute nitric acid at room temperature gives a mixture of 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol. Concentrated nitric and sulphuric acids give 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, known as picric acid.

**Sulphonation.** Concentrated sulphuric acid at about $373\ \text{K}$ gives *ortho*- and *para*-phenolsulphonic acid.

**Kolbe–Schmitt reaction.** Sodium phenoxide reacts with carbon dioxide at about $400\ \text{K}$ and $4$–$7\ \text{atm}$, and acidification gives salicylic acid:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CO_2 \xrightarrow{400\ \text{K}} C_6H_4(OH)COONa \xrightarrow{H^+} C_6H_4(OH)COOH}.
$$

Salicylic acid is 2-hydroxybenzoic acid, the starting material for aspirin.

**Reimer–Tiemann reaction.** Phenol reacts with chloroform and aqueous sodium hydroxide to give salicylaldehyde:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + CHCl_3 + 3NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_4(OH)CHO + 3NaCl + 2H_2O}.
$$

The product is 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde. The reaction introduces a formyl group at the *ortho* position through a dichlorocarbene intermediate.

## 7. Oxidation and coupling

Phenol is easily oxidised, which is why old phenol samples turn pink or brown on exposure to air. Strong oxidants such as chromic acid convert phenol to *p*-benzoquinone:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH \xrightarrow{[O]} O{=}C_6H_4{=}O}.
$$

The product is a conjugated cyclic diketone, not a simple aldehyde or ketone. Catechol and hydroquinone can be oxidised to the corresponding *ortho*- and *para*-quinones, and this interconversion is the basis of some redox systems in biology.

Phenols also couple with diazonium salts. In alkaline solution, phenol reacts with benzenediazonium chloride at the *para* position to give an azo dye:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{OH^-} HOC_6H_4N{=}NC_6H_5 + HCl}.
$$

The product, *p*-hydroxyazobenzene, is orange. The coupling is an electrophilic substitution in which the diazonium ion acts as the electrophile.

A simple laboratory test for phenol is the violet colour produced with neutral iron(III) chloride solution. Alcohols do not give this colour. Bromine water gives a white precipitate, and the iodoform test is negative for phenol itself.

## 8. Worked example: pH of a phenol solution

Phenol has $K_a=1.0\times10^{-10}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Find the pH and the percentage ionisation of a $0.10\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ aqueous solution.

Let $x$ be the concentration of phenoxide and of $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ at equilibrium. The initial concentration of phenol is $0.10\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$, and the amount that ionises is small, so

$$
K_a=\frac{x^2}{0.10-x}\approx\frac{x^2}{0.10}.
$$

Thus

$$
x^2=K_a(0.10)=(1.0\times10^{-10})(0.10)=1.0\times10^{-11},
$$

$$
x=3.16\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

The pH is

$$
\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}(3.16\times10^{-6})=5.50.
$$

The percentage ionisation is

$$
\frac{3.16\times10^{-6}}{0.10}\times100\%=3.16\times10^{-3}\%.
$$

The approximation is justified because $x/0.10=3.2\times10^{-5}$, which is much less than $1$. The answer is acidic but only mildly so: a $0.10\ \text{M}$ solution of a strong acid would have $\mathrm{pH}=1$, and a $0.10\ \text{M}$ carboxylic acid with $\mathrm{p}K_a\approx4.8$ would have $\mathrm{pH}\approx2.9$. Phenol sits between an alcohol and a carboxylic acid, exactly as its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ predicts.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Phenol traps">

- **Expecting phenol to dissolve in sodium bicarbonate with effervescence.** It does not. Phenol is not acidic enough to protonate $\mathrm{HCO_3^-}$ appreciably, so there is no carbon dioxide. That reaction belongs to carboxylic acids.
- **Treating phenol as an alcohol for esterification.** Direct Fischer esterification is slow; use an acid chloride or anhydride. The phenoxide is a poor nucleophile and the ring is crowded.
- **Forgetting that the ring is activated.** Bromination, nitration and sulphonation of phenol need much milder conditions than the corresponding reactions of benzene, and poly-substitution is common.
- **Expecting oxidation of phenol to give a simple carbonyl compound.** Strong oxidation gives a quinone, a cyclic conjugated diketone, not an aldehyde or ketone.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Arrange ethanol, phenol, $p$-cresol and $p$-nitrophenol in order of increasing acidity.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Ethanol is the weakest acid because its alkoxide has no resonance stabilisation. $p$-Cresol is next: the methyl group donates electron density and destabilises the phenoxide relative to phenol. Phenol follows, and $p$-nitrophenol is the strongest because the nitro group withdraws electron density and stabilises the phenoxide. The order is ethanol < $p$-cresol < phenol < $p$-nitrophenol.
</details>

**2.** Why does phenol react with bromine water to give a white precipitate, while benzene needs a catalyst and gives only mono-bromination under ordinary conditions?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The –OH group donates a lone pair into the ring, making phenol much more electron-rich than benzene. Electrophilic attack by bromine is therefore fast and does not need a Lewis acid. The *ortho* and *para* positions are strongly activated, so bromine substitutes all three available positions to give 2,4,6-tribromophenol, which is insoluble and appears as a white precipitate. Benzene is less activated and normally requires $\mathrm{FeBr_3}$; without a strong driving force, poly-bromination is not favoured.
</details>

**3.** Give the product of phenol with (a) NaOH followed by $\mathrm{CH_3I}$, (b) $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$ and NaOH, and (c) bromine water.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

(a) Sodium phenoxide forms first, then attacks iodomethane in a Williamson reaction to give anisole, methoxybenzene. (b) The Reimer–Tiemann reaction gives salicylaldehyde, 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde. (c) Bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol as a white precipitate.
</details>

## Remember

- Phenol is acidic because the phenoxide ion delocalises its negative charge around the ring; an alkoxide cannot.
- Electron-withdrawing substituents increase phenol acidity, electron-donating substituents decrease it, and *ortho*/*para* substituents have the largest effect.
- Phenol dissolves in NaOH but not in NaHCO₃; that is the standard distinction from a carboxylic acid.
- Phenol is strongly activated towards electrophilic substitution and directs *ortho*/*para*; bromination, nitration, sulphonation, Kolbe–Schmitt and Reimer–Tiemann all follow that pattern.
- Phenol is easily oxidised to a quinone and couples with diazonium salts to give azo dyes.

The next module turns to the ethers. Their oxygen is in the same oxidation state as in an alcohol, but it has no –OH hydrogen, so their chemistry is dominated by cleavage of the $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bond rather than by acid–base reactions.
