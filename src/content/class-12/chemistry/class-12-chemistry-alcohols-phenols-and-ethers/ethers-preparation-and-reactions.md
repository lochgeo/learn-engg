## The quiet functional group

Ethers are the least dramatic family in this chapter. They have no –OH hydrogen to donate, no acidic proton to lose and no carbonyl group to attack. That unreactivity is exactly why they are useful: diethyl ether is a classic solvent for Grignard reactions, and MTBE has been used as an oxygenate in petrol. But “unreactive” is not the same as “inert”. Strong acids can split the $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bond, and oxygen in the air can slowly convert an ether into a peroxide that makes an old bottle dangerous to distil.

The chemistry of an ether is therefore the chemistry of its two carbon–oxygen bonds. The questions are: how is the bond made, and which side breaks when it is cleaved?

## 1. Structure and physical properties

The oxygen in an ether is approximately $sp^3$ hybridised, with two lone pairs and a bent $\mathrm{C{-}O{-}C}$ angle of about $111^\circ$. The $\mathrm{C{-}O}$ bonds are polar, so an ether has a small dipole moment, but it has no $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ bond and cannot donate a hydrogen bond.

The consequences are visible in the boiling points:

| Compound | Molar mass / $\text{g mol}^{-1}$ | Boiling point / $^\circ\text{C}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pentane | 72 | $36$ |
| diethyl ether | 74 | $35$ |
| butan-1-ol | 74 | $118$ |

Diethyl ether and pentane have almost the same boiling point because the dipole–dipole forces in the ether are similar in strength to the dispersion forces in the alkane. Butan-1-ol, with the same molar mass as diethyl ether, boils $83\ \text{K}$ higher because its –OH group can donate a hydrogen bond.

Ethers can accept hydrogen bonds from water, so small ethers are more soluble in water than alkanes of similar size. Diethyl ether dissolves to about $7\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{mL}$ of water at room temperature; tetrahydrofuran is fully miscible because its oxygen is more exposed and the ring is less hydrophobic. Ethers are good solvents for many organic compounds because they are polar enough to solvate dipoles but not so polar that they react with nucleophiles.

## 2. Naming ethers

The IUPAC system treats the larger carbon group as the parent chain and the smaller group as an **alkoxy** substituent:

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}$ | methoxymethane | dimethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{CH_3OCH_2CH_3}$ | methoxyethane | methyl ethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}$ | ethoxyethane | diethyl ether |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}$ | methoxybenzene | anisole |
| $\mathrm{C_6H_5OC_2H_5}$ | ethoxybenzene | phenetole |

The common style simply names both organic groups in alphabetical order and adds “ether”. It is still widely used for simple symmetrical ethers such as diethyl ether.

## 3. Williamson synthesis

The most reliable way to make an ether is to join an alkoxide to a haloalkane:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}O^-Na^+ + R'{-}X \rightarrow R{-}O{-}R' + NaX}.
$$

This is the **Williamson synthesis**. It is an SN2 reaction: the alkoxide oxygen attacks the carbon bearing the halogen from the back, and the halide leaves. The reaction works best when the haloalkane is methyl or primary, because the backside is open and the transition state is not crowded.

The substrate order is therefore

$$
\mathrm{CH_3X > 1^\circ > 2^\circ \gg 3^\circ}.
$$

A tertiary halide gives mainly an alkene by E2 elimination, because the alkoxide is also a strong base and the crowded carbon is easier to deprotonate than to attack. A secondary halide gives a mixture of ether and alkene. For an aryl ether, the phenoxide is the nucleophile and the alkyl halide must still be primary or methyl:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CH_3I \rightarrow C_6H_5OCH_3 + NaI}.
$$

The Williamson synthesis is the method of choice for mixed ethers, because the two groups can be chosen independently. For a symmetrical ether, a simpler route is often available.

## 4. Dehydration of alcohols

Two molecules of a primary alcohol can condense to give a symmetrical ether when heated with concentrated sulphuric acid at about $413\ \text{K}$:

$$
\mathrm{2C_2H_5OH \xrightarrow[413\ \text{K}]{conc.\ H_2SO_4} C_2H_5OC_2H_5 + H_2O}.
$$

The reaction is a nucleophilic substitution: one alcohol molecule is protonated and loses water to form a carbocation or a reactive oxonium species, and a second alcohol molecule attacks. The temperature is critical. At about $443\ \text{K}$ the same mixture gives mainly ethene by elimination, so a small change in conditions switches the product from an ether to an alkene.

The dehydration route is limited to symmetrical ethers, because a mixture of two different alcohols gives a mixture of three ethers. It also works poorly for secondary and tertiary alcohols, which eliminate more readily than they substitute.

## 5. Other preparations

Symmetrical ethers can be made by heating a primary alkyl halide with dry silver oxide:

$$
\mathrm{2R{-}X + Ag_2O \rightarrow R{-}O{-}R + 2AgX}.
$$

The silver ion helps the halide leave, and the oxide ion attacks the alkyl halide to form an alkoxide, which then attacks a second molecule of halide. The method is useful in the laboratory but expensive on a large scale.

Industrially, diethyl ether can be made by passing ethanol vapour over heated alumina at about $523\ \text{K}$. At higher temperatures the same catalyst dehydrates ethanol to ethene, so the temperature again selects the product.

## 6. Cleavage by hydrogen halides

Ethers are cleaved by strong hydrogen halides:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}O{-}R' + HX \rightarrow R{-}OH + R'{-}X}.
$$

The first step is protonation of the ether oxygen, which converts a poor leaving group into a good one. The halide ion then attacks one of the alkyl groups, displacing the alcohol:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}O^+(H){-}R' + X^- \rightarrow R{-}OH + R'{-}X}.
$$

With an excess of the hydrogen halide, the alcohol that is produced is converted into a second molecule of alkyl halide:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}OH + HX \rightarrow R{-}X + H_2O}.
$$

The reactivity order is $\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}$. HI is the usual reagent because it is the strongest acid and the iodide ion is the best nucleophile of the three.

With simple primary and secondary groups, the smaller, less hindered alkyl group is usually the one that leaves as the halide, because the cleavage has SN2 character. A tertiary group can leave by an SN1 pathway instead. The important exception is an aryl alkyl ether: the aryl–oxygen bond is strong and a phenoxide is a poor leaving group, so the **alkyl–oxygen** bond breaks instead:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3 + HI \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + CH_3I}.
$$

Anisole therefore gives phenol and iodomethane, not benzene and methanol. That regiochemistry is a favourite exam question because it tests whether you understand which bond is actually broken.

## 7. Reactions of anisole

The methoxy group is an electron-donating, *ortho*/*para*-directing substituent, so anisole undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution more easily than benzene. Nitration with dilute nitric acid gives a mixture of 2-nitroanisole and 4-nitroanisole; bromination with bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromoanisole as a white precipitate. Friedel–Crafts acylation also works, although the Lewis acid can coordinate to the methoxy oxygen and reduce the yield if it is used in excess.

The ring is still strongly activated, although somewhat less so than phenol, because the methoxy oxygen is bonded to a methyl group rather than to a hydrogen; the methyl group’s inductive effect moderates the resonance donation. Even so, the methoxy group is a much stronger activator than hydrogen.

## 8. Peroxide formation and the Zeisel method

Ethers slowly react with oxygen from the air to form hydroperoxides and peroxides, especially on exposure to light:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}O{-}R' + O_2 \rightarrow R{-}O{-}O{-}R' \ \text{or related peroxides}}.
$$

The peroxides are much less volatile than the ether and can concentrate when an old bottle is distilled. They are explosive and can cause fires. Never distil an ether to dryness, and test an old sample before use. A simple test is to shake it with acidified potassium iodide solution: peroxides oxidise iodide to iodine, which gives a blue-black colour with starch.

The **Zeisel method** uses the cleavage reaction analytically. An ether containing a methoxy group is heated with HI, and the iodomethane produced is distilled into silver nitrate solution:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}O{-}CH_3 + HI \rightarrow R{-}OH + CH_3I},
$$

$$
\mathrm{CH_3I + AgNO_3 + H_2O \rightarrow AgI(s) + CH_3OH + HNO_3}.
$$

The mass of silver iodide precipitate gives the amount of methoxy groups in the original sample. The method is a good illustration of how a preparative reaction can be turned into a quantitative analysis.

## 9. Worked example: Williamson yield

Sodium ethoxide reacts with bromoethane to give diethyl ether:

$$
\mathrm{C_2H_5ONa + C_2H_5Br \rightarrow C_2H_5OC_2H_5 + NaBr}.
$$

What mass of diethyl ether is obtained from $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of sodium ethoxide and $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of bromoethane if the yield is $75\%$?

The two reactants are present in a $1:1$ mole ratio, which matches the equation, so neither is in excess. The theoretical amount of ether is therefore $0.10\ \text{mol}$.

The molar mass of diethyl ether is

$$
M(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}O})=4(12.0)+10(1.0)+16.0=74.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The theoretical mass is

$$
m=0.10\times74.0=7.40\ \text{g}.
$$

At $75\%$ yield,

$$
m_{\text{actual}}=7.40\times0.75=5.55\ \text{g}.
$$

A quick mass check is possible from the reactants: $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of sodium ethoxide has mass $0.10\times68.0=6.80\ \text{g}$, and $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of bromoethane has mass $0.10\times109.0=10.9\ \text{g}$. The product is much lighter than the sum because sodium bromide is also formed and because the yield is only $75\%$. The answer, about $5.6\ \text{g}$, is a realistic laboratory quantity for a small-scale ether synthesis.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Ether traps">

- **Using a tertiary alkyl halide in a Williamson synthesis.** The alkoxide acts as a base and eliminates HX, giving an alkene instead of the ether. Use a methyl or primary halide.
- **Thinking an ether is completely inert.** It is stable to base and to many oxidants, but strong acids cleave it, and air slowly forms explosive peroxides.
- **Cleaving an aryl alkyl ether at the wrong bond.** Anisole with HI gives phenol and iodomethane. The aryl–oxygen bond stays intact because phenoxide is a poor leaving group.
- **Forgetting the temperature in alcohol dehydration.** About $413\ \text{K}$ favours the ether; about $443\ \text{K}$ favours the alkene.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Why is diethyl ether a better solvent than ethanol for preparing and using a Grignard reagent?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

A Grignard reagent is destroyed by any acidic proton. Ethanol has an –OH hydrogen that protonates the carbanion-like carbon, converting $\mathrm{RMgX}$ into the alkane $\mathrm{RH}$. Diethyl ether is aprotic: it has no –OH and no acidic hydrogen, so it solvates the magnesium and keeps the reagent intact. Its oxygen lone pairs coordinate to the magnesium, which also helps stabilise the reagent.
</details>

**2.** What products are formed when anisole is heated with concentrated HI? Explain why the other possible cleavage does not occur.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Anisole gives phenol and iodomethane. Protonation of the ether oxygen is followed by attack of iodide on the methyl carbon, so the methyl–oxygen bond breaks. The alternative would require iodide to attack the aromatic carbon, displacing a phenoxide ion. That is unfavourable because the aryl–oxygen bond has partial double-bond character and phenoxide is a poor leaving group. The alkyl–oxygen bond is the weaker, more accessible one.
</details>

**3.** An old bottle of diethyl ether is suspected of containing peroxides. How would you test it, and why is distillation dangerous?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Shake a small sample with acidified potassium iodide solution. Peroxides oxidise iodide to iodine, and starch gives a blue-black colour if iodine is present. Distillation is dangerous because the peroxides are less volatile than the ether and concentrate in the flask as the ether boils off. When the concentration becomes high enough, they can decompose explosively. Never distil an ether to dryness, and dispose of old samples through a safe chemical-waste route.
</details>

## Remember

- Ethers are bent, polar molecules with no –OH, so they boil near alkanes of similar mass and are much more soluble in water than those alkanes.
- Williamson synthesis joins an alkoxide to a methyl or primary halide by SN2; tertiary halides eliminate instead.
- Dehydration of a primary alcohol at about $413\ \text{K}$ gives a symmetrical ether; at about $443\ \text{K}$ it gives an alkene.
- Strong HI or HBr cleaves ethers; in aryl alkyl ethers the alkyl–oxygen bond breaks, giving a phenol and an alkyl halide.
- Ethers form explosive peroxides on storage; test old samples and never distil to dryness.
