## The O–H bond is the handle

Ethanol and dimethyl ether have almost the same molar mass, comparable dipole moments and the same formula, yet ethanol boils at $78^\circ\text{C}$ and dimethyl ether at $-24^\circ\text{C}$. The difference is a single hydrogen on oxygen. That hydrogen lets ethanol donate a hydrogen bond to a neighbouring molecule, and it also makes the –OH group the place where most alcohol chemistry begins. Remove the hydrogen and replace it with a metal, an acyl group or a halogen, and the rest of the molecule is largely unchanged.

This module follows that handle through the main reactions of alcohols: acid–base behaviour, esterification, substitution by halide, dehydration and oxidation. The goal is not to memorise a list but to see that each reaction asks a different question of the same $\mathrm{C{-}O{-}H}$ unit.

## 1. Physical properties

Alcohols are polar and hydrogen-bonded. The –OH group can both donate and accept a hydrogen bond, so alcohols associate strongly in the liquid:

| Alcohol | Boiling point / $^\circ\text{C}$ | Solubility in water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| methanol | $65$ | miscible |
| ethanol | $78$ | miscible |
| propan-1-ol | $97$ | miscible |
| propan-2-ol | $82$ | miscible |
| butan-1-ol | $118$ | partly soluble |
| hexan-1-ol | $157$ | almost insoluble |

Two trends run side by side. Within a homologous series, boiling point rises with molar mass because the dispersion forces between the hydrocarbon parts grow. For isomers, branching lowers the boiling point because the molecule becomes more compact and the contact area falls: propan-1-ol boils at $97^\circ\text{C}$, propan-2-ol at $82^\circ\text{C}$.

Solubility in water depends on the balance between the polar –OH group and the non-polar alkyl chain. The small alcohols in the table are miscible with water because the hydrogen-bonding network can accommodate the small hydrocarbon part. As the chain lengthens, the energy cost of creating a cavity in water rises and solubility falls sharply.

## 2. How acidic is an alcohol?

An alcohol can lose its –OH hydrogen to form an **alkoxide** ion:

$$
\mathrm{ROH + H_2O \rightleftharpoons RO^- + H_3O^+}.
$$

The equilibrium lies far to the left. Typical acid dissociation constants are small:

| Compound | $\mathrm{p}K_a$ in water |
| --- | --- |
| methanol | $15.5$ |
| ethanol | $15.9$ |
| propan-2-ol | $16.5$ |
| 2-methylpropan-2-ol | $18.0$ |
| water | $15.7$ |
| phenol | $10.0$ |

Ethanol is slightly **weaker** than water as an acid, which is why sodium ethoxide is hydrolysed by water:

$$
\mathrm{C_2H_5ONa + H_2O \rightarrow C_2H_5OH + NaOH}.
$$

The order methanol > ethanol > propan-2-ol > tert-butanol is partly an inductive effect: alkyl groups donate electron density and destabilise the negative charge on oxygen. Solvation also matters, because a small alkoxide is better solvated by water. The important comparison for this chapter is with phenol: phenol is about a million times more acidic than ethanol, for reasons developed in the phenol module.

## 3. Reaction with alkali metals

Sodium and potassium react with alcohols to give an alkoxide and hydrogen:

$$
\mathrm{2CH_3CH_2OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2CH_3CH_2ONa + H_2}.
$$

The reaction is a redox process: the metal is oxidised to $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and the alcohol hydrogen is reduced to $\mathrm{H_2}$. It is slower and less violent than the reaction of sodium with water, which is consistent with ethanol being the weaker acid. Sodium ethoxide is a strong base and a good nucleophile, and it is the reagent behind the Williamson ether synthesis.

## 4. Esterification

An alcohol and a carboxylic acid form an ester in the presence of a small amount of concentrated sulphuric acid:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}.
$$

The reaction is reversible and usually reaches an equilibrium. To improve the yield, use an excess of one reactant or remove water as it forms. The acid catalyst protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic; the alcohol then attacks, a tetrahedral intermediate forms, and water leaves.

Esterification is much easier with an acid chloride or an acid anhydride than with a carboxylic acid. Those derivatives are more reactive and do not produce water as the leaving group in the same way. This matters for phenols, whose –OH is a poor nucleophile towards carboxylic acids.

## 5. Reaction with hydrogen halides

Alcohols are converted to haloalkanes by hydrogen halides:

$$
\mathrm{ROH + HX \rightarrow RX + H_2O}.
$$

The reactivity order is

$$
3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ,
$$

because the reaction has increasing SN1 character as the carbocation becomes more stable. The halogen order is $\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}$; HCl is the slowest and often needs a Lewis acid such as $\mathrm{ZnCl_2}$.

The **Lucas test** uses concentrated hydrochloric acid and anhydrous zinc chloride. A tertiary alcohol gives immediate cloudiness as the insoluble alkyl chloride separates; a secondary alcohol gives cloudiness within about five minutes; a primary alcohol reacts only on heating, or not at all under the test conditions. Allylic and benzylic alcohols react rapidly because their carbocations are resonance-stabilised.

## 6. Dehydration

Heating an alcohol with concentrated sulphuric acid or phosphoric acid removes water and forms an alkene:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[443\ \text{K}]{conc.\ H_2SO_4} CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O}.
$$

The reaction usually follows an E1 pathway: the –OH is protonated, water leaves, and a carbocation loses a proton to form the double bond. The ease of dehydration follows carbocation stability, $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ$. When more than one alkene is possible, the more substituted alkene usually predominates (Saytzeff’s rule).

Temperature matters. At about $443\ \text{K}$ ethanol gives ethene, but at a lower temperature, about $413\ \text{K}$, with an excess of ethanol, the main product is diethyl ether:

$$
\mathrm{2C_2H_5OH \xrightarrow[413\ \text{K}]{conc.\ H_2SO_4} C_2H_5OC_2H_5 + H_2O}.
$$

The same reagent therefore gives either an alkene or an ether depending on the conditions. That competition is the starting point for the ether module.

## 7. Oxidation and dehydrogenation

The product of oxidation depends on the class of alcohol.

- **Primary alcohol:** first to an aldehyde, then to a carboxylic acid. Gentle reagents such as pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) in dichloromethane stop at the aldehyde:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow{PCC} CH_3CHO}.
$$

  Strong acidic oxidants such as $\mathrm{KMnO_4/H^+}$ or $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+}$ carry the oxidation to the acid:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CHO \xrightarrow{KMnO_4/H^+} CH_3COOH}.
$$

- **Secondary alcohol:** to a ketone. The carbonyl carbon already has two carbon groups, so further oxidation of the carbon skeleton is difficult:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_3 \xrightarrow{K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+} CH_3COCH_3}.
$$

- **Tertiary alcohol:** no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon, so mild oxidation cannot remove hydrogen from that carbon. Strong hot acidic permanganate can cleave a C–C bond, giving a mixture of products, but a clean ketone or aldehyde is not formed.

Dehydrogenation over copper at about $573\ \text{K}$ follows the same pattern without an oxygen supply: a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde, a secondary alcohol gives a ketone, and a tertiary alcohol gives an alkene by loss of water or decomposes.

The familiar breathalyser test uses the oxidation of ethanol by acidified potassium dichromate. The orange $\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}$ ion is reduced to green $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$:

$$
\mathrm{3CH_3CH_2OH + 2Cr_2O_7^{2-} + 16H^+ \rightarrow 3CH_3COOH + 4Cr^{3+} + 11H_2O}.
$$

## 8. The haloform reaction

Ethanol and alcohols with a $\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH){-}}$ group react with iodine in aqueous sodium hydroxide to give a yellow precipitate of iodoform, $\mathrm{CHI_3}$:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + 4I_2 + 6NaOH \rightarrow CHI_3 + HCOONa + 5NaI + 5H_2O}.
$$

Propan-2-ol gives iodoform and sodium ethanoate. Methanol and propan-1-ol do not give a positive iodoform test, because they cannot form the required $\mathrm{CH_3CO{-}}$ intermediate. The test is therefore a useful way to recognise ethanol and methyl carbinols.

## 9. Worked example: hydrogen from ethanol

Ethanol reacts with excess sodium:

$$
\mathrm{2C_2H_5OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2C_2H_5ONa + H_2}.
$$

What volume of hydrogen, measured at STP, is produced when $4.6\ \text{g}$ of ethanol reacts completely?

The molar mass of ethanol is

$$
M(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=2(12.0)+6(1.0)+16.0=46.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The amount of ethanol is

$$
n=\frac{4.6}{46.0}=0.10\ \text{mol}.
$$

The equation shows that two moles of ethanol give one mole of hydrogen, so

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})=\frac{0.10}{2}=0.050\ \text{mol}.
$$

At STP, one mole of an ideal gas occupies $22.4\ \text{L}$:

$$
V(\mathrm{H_2})=0.050\times22.4=1.12\ \text{L}.
$$

The answer is a little over one litre, which is a sensible laboratory volume for a tenth of a mole of alcohol. A quick mass check also works: $4.6\ \text{g}$ of ethanol contains $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of –OH hydrogen atoms, and two such atoms combine to make one $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Alcohol reaction traps">

- **Expecting a tertiary alcohol to oxidise to a ketone.** There is no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon. Mild oxidation does nothing; strong hot permanganate cleaves the carbon skeleton instead of giving a clean carbonyl compound.
- **Reversing the Lucas test order.** Tertiary alcohols react immediately, secondary within minutes, and primary only slowly or on heating. Immediate cloudiness means a tertiary alcohol, not a primary one.
- **Using $443\ \text{K}$ for ether formation.** That temperature favours ethene. Ether formation from ethanol is carried out at about $413\ \text{K}$ with an excess of alcohol.
- **Treating esterification as irreversible.** It is an equilibrium, so the yield depends on removing water or using an excess of one reactant.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Which of ethanol, propan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol gives immediate turbidity with Lucas reagent, and why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

2-Methylpropan-2-ol, a tertiary alcohol, gives immediate turbidity. It forms a relatively stable tertiary carbocation, so the C–O bond ionises quickly and the insoluble alkyl chloride separates. Propan-2-ol is secondary and reacts within a few minutes; ethanol is primary and reacts only slowly, if at all, under the test conditions.
</details>

**2.** Give the product of propan-1-ol with (a) PCC in dichloromethane, (b) hot acidic $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$, and (c) copper at $573\ \text{K}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

(a) PCC stops the oxidation at the aldehyde: propanal. (b) Hot acidic permanganate oxidises the aldehyde all the way to the acid: propanoic acid. (c) Copper at $573\ \text{K}$ dehydrogenates the primary alcohol to the aldehyde: propanal. In each case the carbon skeleton is unchanged.
</details>

**3.** Ethanol reacts vigorously with sodium metal but only very slightly with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Explain both observations.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Sodium metal is a strong reducing agent and removes the –OH hydrogen as $\mathrm{H_2}$, driving the reaction to sodium ethoxide. Sodium hydroxide is a base, and the acid–base equilibrium

$$
\mathrm{C_2H_5OH + OH^- \rightleftharpoons C_2H_5O^- + H_2O}
$$

lies to the left because water is a slightly stronger acid than ethanol. Hydroxide is therefore not strong enough to convert a significant fraction of ethanol into ethoxide.
</details>

## Remember

- Hydrogen bonding raises the boiling points of alcohols far above those of isomeric ethers and alkanes.
- Alcohols are very weak acids: ethanol is slightly weaker than water, and phenol is about a million times stronger.
- Sodium gives an alkoxide and hydrogen; the alkoxide is a strong base and a useful nucleophile.
- Hydrogen halides convert alcohols to haloalkanes with the reactivity order $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ$; the Lucas test uses that order.
- Dehydration gives an alkene at about $443\ \text{K}$ and an ether at about $413\ \text{K}$ with excess alcohol.
- Oxidation stops at an aldehyde with PCC, goes to an acid with strong acidic oxidants, and gives a ketone from a secondary alcohol; tertiary alcohols resist mild oxidation.
