## Mastery check

Try each question without looking at the answer first. The questions get harder as you go.

### 1. Classify and name

Classify each compound as a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol, or as a phenol: butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, benzyl alcohol, phenol.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Butan-2-ol is a secondary alcohol: the carbinol carbon is attached to two other carbons. 2-Methylpropan-2-ol is tertiary: three carbons are attached to the carbinol carbon. Benzyl alcohol, $\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}$, is a primary benzylic alcohol because the –OH is on the $\mathrm{CH_2}$ carbon next to the ring. Phenol has its –OH directly on an aromatic ring carbon, so it is a phenol, not an alcohol.
</details>

### 2. Boiling-point order

Arrange ethanol, dimethyl ether and propane in increasing order of boiling point, and explain the order in one sentence.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Propane ($-42^\circ\text{C}$) < dimethyl ether ($-24^\circ\text{C}$) < ethanol ($78^\circ\text{C}$). Propane is non-polar and has only dispersion forces; dimethyl ether has a dipole but no –OH, so it can accept but not donate hydrogen bonds; ethanol can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds, which raises its boiling point sharply.
</details>

### 3. Four reactions of one alcohol

Give the product of propan-2-ol with (a) sodium metal, (b) PCC in dichloromethane, (c) concentrated sulphuric acid at $443\ \text{K}$, and (d) HI.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) Sodium propan-2-olate and hydrogen gas:

$$
\mathrm{2(CH_3)_2CHOH + 2Na \rightarrow 2(CH_3)_2CHONa + H_2}.
$$

(b) PCC oxidises the secondary alcohol to propanone, $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2C{=}O}$.

(c) Dehydration gives propene, $\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2}$.

(d) HI converts the alcohol to 2-iodopropane, $\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHI}$.
</details>

### 4. Acidity ranking

Arrange ethanol, $p$-cresol, phenol and $p$-nitrophenol in increasing order of acidity.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Ethanol < $p$-cresol < phenol < $p$-nitrophenol. Ethanol has no resonance stabilisation of its alkoxide. $p$-Cresol has a methyl group that donates electron density and destabilises the phenoxide relative to phenol. $p$-Nitrophenol has a nitro group that withdraws electron density and stabilises the phenoxide, so it is the most acidic of the four.
</details>

### 5. Distinguishing two oxygen compounds

You are given two colourless liquids: phenol and cyclohexanol. Describe two tests that distinguish them and state the observation for each.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Iron(III) chloride:** phenol gives a violet colour with neutral $\mathrm{FeCl_3}$ solution; cyclohexanol gives no colour.

**Bromine water:** phenol gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol; cyclohexanol does not react under the mild conditions of the test.

A third option is solubility in NaOH: phenol dissolves as sodium phenoxide, while cyclohexanol is not acidic enough to dissolve. Neither compound dissolves in NaHCO₃ with effervescence, so that test does not distinguish them.
</details>

### 6. Williamson synthesis

Show how ethoxybenzene can be made from phenol and bromoethane. Why would the alternative pairing, bromobenzene and ethanol, fail?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Phenol is first converted to sodium phenoxide with NaOH:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}.
$$

Sodium phenoxide then attacks bromoethane in an SN2 reaction:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CH_3CH_2Br \rightarrow C_6H_5OCH_2CH_3 + NaBr}.
$$

The product is ethoxybenzene.

The alternative pairing would require ethanol to attack bromobenzene. That fails because the aryl C–Br bond is strong and the ring blocks backside attack; bromobenzene does not undergo simple SN2 substitution. The Williamson synthesis must put the nucleophile on the phenol side and the leaving group on the alkyl side.
</details>

### 7. Ether cleavage

Anisole is heated with concentrated HI. What are the products, and which C–O bond breaks? Would a second mole of HI change the organic product?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Anisole gives phenol and iodomethane:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3 + HI \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + CH_3I}.
$$

The methyl–oxygen bond breaks. Protonation of the ether oxygen makes methanol a good leaving group, and iodide attacks the methyl carbon. The aryl–oxygen bond is not broken because it has partial double-bond character and phenoxide would be a poor leaving group.

A second mole of HI does not convert phenol to iodobenzene under these conditions; the aryl C–O bond remains intact. The organic product stays phenol.
</details>

### 8. Hydrogen from ethanol

What volume of hydrogen, measured at STP, is produced when $92\ \text{g}$ of ethanol reacts completely with excess sodium?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The molar mass of ethanol is $46.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so

$$
n(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH})=\frac{92}{46.0}=2.0\ \text{mol}.
$$

The equation

$$
\mathrm{2C_2H_5OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2C_2H_5ONa + H_2}
$$

shows that two moles of ethanol give one mole of hydrogen:

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})=\frac{2.0}{2}=1.0\ \text{mol}.
$$

At STP, $V=1.0\times22.4=22.4\ \text{L}$.
</details>

### 9. Tribromophenol yield

Bromine water is added to $4.70\ \text{g}$ of phenol. Assuming a $90\%$ yield, what mass of 2,4,6-tribromophenol is obtained?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The molar mass of phenol is $94.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so

$$
n(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH})=\frac{4.70}{94.1}=0.0500\ \text{mol}.
$$

The reaction is

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3OH + 3HBr},
$$

so one mole of phenol gives one mole of tribromophenol. The molar mass of $\mathrm{C_6H_2Br_3OH}$ is

$$
6(12.0)+3(1.0)+3(79.9)+16.0=330.7\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The theoretical mass is $0.0500\times330.7=16.5\ \text{g}$, and at $90\%$ yield the actual mass is $16.5\times0.90=14.9\ \text{g}$.
</details>

### 10. Explain the acidity pattern

Explain why phenol is much more acidic than ethanol but still much less acidic than ethanoic acid.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Phenol loses a proton to give the phenoxide ion, in which the negative charge is delocalised by resonance onto the *ortho* and *para* ring carbons. That delocalisation stabilises the ion and makes phenol about a million times more acidic than ethanol, whose alkoxide has the charge localised on one oxygen.

Ethanoic acid is still stronger because its carboxylate ion delocalises the negative charge over two equivalent oxygen atoms. Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so a charge shared between two oxygens is lower in energy than a charge shared between one oxygen and several carbons. The $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values reflect this: about $16$ for ethanol, $10$ for phenol and $4.8$ for ethanoic acid.
</details>
