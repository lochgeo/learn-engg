## Building a C–O bond on purpose

An alcohol or phenol is not usually found ready-made in a bottle; it has to be assembled. The routes in this module all answer the same question: where does the oxygen come from, and what happens to the carbon skeleton while it arrives? There are two broad strategies. The first adds oxygen to an existing hydrocarbon framework, usually through water, a hydroxide ion or an oxidation. The second keeps a carbonyl oxygen and reduces the carbon around it, or builds a new carbon–carbon bond with a Grignard reagent.

Phenols are a special case. The oxygen is almost always introduced through a substituent that is already on the ring, because direct hydroxylation of benzene is not a clean laboratory reaction. The industrial cumene route and the older chlorobenzene and sulphonate routes all follow that logic.

## 1. Hydration of alkenes

Water adds across a $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ bond under acid catalysis. The proton adds first to give the more stable carbocation, so the –OH ends up on the more substituted carbon:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} CH_3CH(OH)CH_3}.
$$

Propene therefore gives propan-2-ol, not propan-1-ol. The reaction is the reverse of acid-catalysed dehydration, so it is an equilibrium. Industrially, ethene is hydrated over phosphoric acid on silica at about $573\ \text{K}$ and $60$–$70\ \text{atm}$:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H_3PO_4} CH_3CH_2OH}.
$$

The acid-catalysed route is limited by carbocation rearrangements. A substrate that can form a more stable rearranged cation may give a mixture of alcohols.

**Hydroboration–oxidation** avoids the carbocation entirely and gives the anti-Markovnikov alcohol. Diborane adds boron to the less hindered carbon, and oxidation replaces the boron with –OH with retention:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 \xrightarrow[2.\ \mathrm{H_2O_2/OH^-}]{1.\ \mathrm{B_2H_6}} CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}.
$$

The two routes are complementary: acid-catalysed hydration gives the more substituted alcohol, hydroboration–oxidation gives the less substituted one.

## 2. Hydrolysis of alkyl halides

A haloalkane is hydrolysed by aqueous alkali:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}X + KOH(aq) \rightarrow R{-}OH + KX}.
$$

Primary halides react mainly by SN2, which needs a strong nucleophile and an unhindered carbon. Tertiary halides react by SN1 and are fastest in a polar protic solvent. The reaction is useful when the carbon skeleton is already correct and only the functional group needs changing. It is also the reverse of the alcohol-to-halide conversion you met in the haloalkane chapter.

## 3. Reduction of carbonyl compounds

Reduction adds hydrogen across the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ bond and leaves the carbon skeleton unchanged. The product class depends on the starting carbonyl:

| Starting compound | Reagent | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aldehyde $\mathrm{RCHO}$ | $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$ or $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$ | primary alcohol $\mathrm{RCH_2OH}$ |
| ketone $\mathrm{R_2CO}$ | $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$ or $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$ | secondary alcohol $\mathrm{R_2CHOH}$ |
| carboxylic acid $\mathrm{RCOOH}$ | $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$ | primary alcohol $\mathrm{RCH_2OH}$ |
| ester $\mathrm{RCOOR'}$ | $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$ | two alcohols, $\mathrm{RCH_2OH}$ and $\mathrm{R'OH}$ |

Sodium borohydride is the gentler reagent: it reduces aldehydes and ketones but leaves carboxylic acids and most esters alone. Lithium aluminium hydride is stronger and reduces acids and esters as well. The difference matters in synthesis, because it lets you choose how far the reduction goes.

## 4. Grignard reagents: adding carbon and oxygen together

A Grignard reagent, $\mathrm{RMgX}$, is made from a haloalkane and magnesium in dry ether. The carbon–magnesium bond is strongly polarised towards carbon, so the organic group behaves as a nucleophile and attacks a carbonyl carbon. After acidic work-up, the alkoxide becomes an alcohol.

The product class is fixed by the carbonyl:

| Carbonyl compound | Alcohol formed after hydrolysis |
| --- | --- |
| methanal, $\mathrm{HCHO}$ | primary, $\mathrm{RCH_2OH}$ |
| another aldehyde, $\mathrm{R'CHO}$ | secondary, $\mathrm{RCH(OH)R'}$ |
| a ketone, $\mathrm{R'COR''}$ | tertiary, $\mathrm{RC(OH)R'R''}$ |
| an ester, $\mathrm{R'COOR''}$ | tertiary, with two $\mathrm{R}$ groups from the Grignard |
| ethylene oxide | primary, $\mathrm{RCH_2CH_2OH}$ |

For example, methylmagnesium bromide with propanone gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3MgBr + (CH_3)_2C{=}O \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} (CH_3)_3COH}.
$$

A Grignard reagent is destroyed by any acidic hydrogen, including water, alcohols and carboxylic acids:

$$
\mathrm{RMgX + H_2O \rightarrow RH + Mg(OH)X}.
$$

All glassware and solvent must therefore be dry. Carbon dioxide is not a carbonyl compound in the same sense: it gives a carboxylic acid, not an alcohol, when it reacts with a Grignard reagent.

## 5. Industrial routes to methanol and ethanol

Methanol is made from synthesis gas over a copper–zinc oxide–alumina catalyst at about $573$–$673\ \text{K}$ and $50$–$100\ \text{atm}$:

$$
\mathrm{CO + 2H_2 \rightarrow CH_3OH}.
$$

Older plants used a zinc oxide–chromium oxide catalyst at higher pressure. The modern low-pressure process is cheaper and gives a cleaner product.

Ethanol is made by fermentation of sugars or by hydration of ethene. Fermentation uses the enzymes in yeast, collectively called zymase:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \xrightarrow{zymase} 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2}.
$$

The fermentation is carried out near $298$–$310\ \text{K}$ and becomes self-limiting at about $15\%$ ethanol by volume, because yeast cannot survive a higher alcohol concentration. Industrial ethanol for fuel or solvent is often made by ethene hydration instead, because it is faster and gives a more concentrated product.

## 6. Phenol from cumene

The cumene process is the dominant industrial route because it produces two valuable products, phenol and acetone. Benzene is alkylated with propene:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + CH_3CH{=}CH_2 \xrightarrow{H_3PO_4} C_6H_5CH(CH_3)_2}.
$$

The product is cumene (isopropylbenzene). Air oxidises the tertiary benzylic C–H bond to a hydroperoxide:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5CH(CH_3)_2 + O_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5C(CH_3)_2OOH}.
$$

Dilute acid then cleaves the hydroperoxide into phenol and acetone:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5C(CH_3)_2OOH \xrightarrow{H^+} C_6H_5OH + (CH_3)_2C{=}O}.
$$

The overall reaction is

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + C_3H_6 + O_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + (CH_3)_2CO}.
$$

Every atom in the reactants appears in one of the two products, so the process has a theoretical atom economy of $100\%$ when both phenol and acetone are counted as useful products. That is one reason it replaced older routes that generated large salt wastes.

## 7. Older phenol routes

**From chlorobenzene (Dow process).** Chlorobenzene is heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide at about $623\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{atm}$:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl + 2NaOH \xrightarrow{623\ \text{K},\ 300\ \text{atm}} C_6H_5ONa + NaCl + H_2O}.
$$

Acidification gives phenol:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + HCl \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + NaCl}.
$$

The harsh conditions are needed because the aryl C–Cl bond is strong and the ring blocks backside attack. The reaction proceeds through an addition–elimination mechanism, not a simple SN2 displacement.

**From benzene sulphonic acid.** Benzene is sulphonated, the sulphonate is fused with sodium hydroxide at about $573\ \text{K}$, and the resulting phenoxide is acidified:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_3Na + 2NaOH \xrightarrow{573\ \text{K}} C_6H_5ONa + Na_2SO_3 + H_2O}.
$$

This route is older and produces a large amount of sulphite waste, but it illustrates the general principle: a substituent that can act as a leaving group is installed first, then replaced by –OH under forcing conditions.

**From diazonium salts.** Aniline is diazotised at $273$–$278\ \text{K}$ and the diazonium salt is warmed with water:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 + NaNO_2 + 2HCl \xrightarrow{273{-}278\ \text{K}} C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + NaCl + 2H_2O},
$$

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + H_2O \xrightarrow{283{-}293\ \text{K}} C_6H_5OH + N_2 + HCl}.
$$

This is a laboratory route rather than an industrial one, but it is valuable because it converts an aromatic amine into a phenol under mild conditions and tolerates many substituents on the ring.

## 8. Worked example: mass, yield and atom economy

Benzene is converted to phenol by the cumene process. Starting with $78.0\ \text{g}$ of benzene and excess propene, and assuming an $85\%$ yield, find the mass of phenol and the mass of acetone produced.

The molar masses are $M(\mathrm{C_6H_6})=78.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, $M(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH})=94.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $M((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CO})=58.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

$$
n(\mathrm{C_6H_6})=\frac{78.0}{78.0}=1.00\ \text{mol}.
$$

The overall equation shows one mole of benzene giving one mole of phenol and one mole of acetone. At $100\%$ yield:

$$
m(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH})=1.00\times94.1=94.1\ \text{g},
$$

$$
m((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CO})=1.00\times58.1=58.1\ \text{g}.
$$

At $85\%$ yield:

$$
m(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH})=94.1\times0.85=80.0\ \text{g},
$$

$$
m((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CO})=58.1\times0.85=49.4\ \text{g}.
$$

The mass of products is $80.0+49.4=129.4\ \text{g}$, which is less than the total mass of benzene, propene and oxygen consumed:

$$
78.0+42.1+32.0=152.1\ \text{g}.
$$

The missing mass is unreacted starting material and by-products, not “lost” matter.

For the atom economy, add the molar masses of all reactants in the overall equation:

$$
78.0+42.1+32.0=152.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The two products together have mass $94.1+58.1=152.2\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so the atom economy is essentially $100\%$. If only phenol is counted as the desired product, the atom economy falls to

$$
\frac{94.1}{152.1}\times100\%=61.9\%.
$$

That distinction is worth remembering: a process can be atom-economical overall and still have a modest atom economy for one particular product.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Preparation errors">

- **Using a ketone and expecting a secondary alcohol from a Grignard reaction.** A ketone has two carbon groups already, so the Grignard adds a third and the product is tertiary.
- **Trying to reduce a carboxylic acid with sodium borohydride.** $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$ is too gentle; use $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$ when the acid must be reduced all the way to a primary alcohol.
- **Forgetting that a Grignard reagent is destroyed by water or alcohol.** The reaction must be run in dry ether, and the carbonyl compound must not contain an acidic –OH or –COOH group unless that group is protected first.
- **Assuming the cumene process makes only phenol.** Acetone is produced in equal molar amount and is part of the economic case for the route.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Which reagent would you use to convert propanal to propan-1-ol, and which to convert propanone to propan-2-ol? Explain whether one reagent can do both.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Both are reduced by $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$ or $\mathrm{LiAlH_4}$. Propanal, an aldehyde, gives propan-1-ol; propanone, a ketone, gives propan-2-ol. Sodium borohydride is sufficient for both because it reduces aldehydes and ketones; lithium aluminium hydride also works but is more forceful than necessary.
</details>

**2.** Starting from bromoethane and propanone, outline a Grignard route to 2-methylbutan-2-ol.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Convert bromoethane to ethylmagnesium bromide in dry ether. Add propanone, then hydrolyse with dilute acid:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2MgBr + (CH_3)_2C{=}O \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} CH_3CH_2C(OH)(CH_3)_2}.
$$

The product has an ethyl group and two methyl groups on the carbinol carbon, so it is a tertiary alcohol: 2-methylbutan-2-ol.
</details>

**3.** Why is chlorobenzene converted to phenol under much harsher conditions than bromoethane is hydrolysed to ethanol?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene has partial double-bond character from resonance with the ring, and the ring’s electron density repels a nucleophile approaching from the back. Simple SN2 displacement is therefore blocked. The reaction needs high temperature and pressure and proceeds by an addition–elimination pathway through a stabilised anionic intermediate. In bromoethane the C–Br bond is a normal single bond and the primary carbon is open to backside attack, so ordinary SN2 hydrolysis is easy.
</details>

## Remember

- Acid-catalysed hydration of an alkene is Markovnikov; hydroboration–oxidation is anti-Markovnikov.
- Hydrolysis of a haloalkane replaces –X by –OH without changing the carbon skeleton.
- Reduction of an aldehyde gives a primary alcohol, a ketone gives a secondary alcohol, a carboxylic acid gives a primary alcohol with a strong hydride donor, and an ester gives two alcohols.
- A Grignard reagent adds a carbon group to a carbonyl; the number of carbon groups already on the carbonyl decides whether the product is $1^\circ$, $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$.
- Phenol is made industrially from cumene, with acetone as a co-product, or from chlorobenzene or benzene sulphonate under forcing conditions.
