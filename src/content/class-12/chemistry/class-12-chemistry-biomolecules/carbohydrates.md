## More than "hydrates of carbon"

The sugar in ripe fruit and the cellulose in a cotton shirt share a superficial formula pattern: carbon combined with hydrogen and oxygen in roughly $1:2:1$. That is why the family was once called the "hydrates of carbon", $C_n(H_2O)_m$. The name is a first guess, not a definition. Deoxyribose ($C_5H_{10}O_4$) and rhamnose ($C_6H_{12}O_5$) do not fit the pattern yet are carbohydrates, while a few compounds that do fit it are not.

The structural definition is what matters: a **carbohydrate** is a polyhydroxy aldehyde, a polyhydroxy ketone, or a substance that hydrolyses to one of these. Many $-OH$ groups plus one carbonyl group explain the water solubility, the ring formation, the sweetness and the ability to link into polymers.

## 1. Three families, one building block

| Family | Unit | On hydrolysis | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monosaccharide | single polyhydroxy carbonyl | cannot be split further | glucose, fructose, ribose |
| Disaccharide | two monosaccharides joined | gives two monosaccharides | sucrose, maltose, lactose |
| Polysaccharide | many monosaccharides joined | gives many monosaccharides | starch, cellulose, glycogen |

Two further labels describe a monosaccharide. If the carbonyl is at the end of the chain it is an **aldose** (glucose); if it is inside the chain it is a **ketose** (fructose). The carbon count gives the rest of the name: triose (3 C), tetrose (4 C), pentose (5 C), hexose (6 C). Glucose is therefore an aldohexose and fructose a ketohexose, even though both have formula $C_6H_{12}O_6$ — they are structural isomers.

## 2. Chiral centres and the $2^n$ count

Open-chain glucose has an aldehyde at C1, then four carbons C2–C5 each carrying $-H$ and $-OH$, and a $CH_2OH$ at C6. Those four internal carbons are **chiral centres**: four stereogenic centres give a maximum of

$$
\text{number of stereoisomers}=2^n=2^4=16,
$$

eight of the $D$ series and eight of the $L$ series. Fructose has its carbonyl at C2 and only three chiral centres (C3–C5), so it has $2^3=8$ stereoisomers.

The $D/L$ label is fixed by the **penultimate carbon**, the chiral centre farthest from the carbonyl. If its $-OH$ lies on the right in a Fischer projection the sugar is $D$; on the left it is $L$ — it says nothing about the direction of optical rotation. Two sugars that differ at exactly one chiral centre are **epimers**: $D$-glucose and $D$-galactose differ at C4, while $D$-glucose and $D$-mannose differ at C2.

## 3. Rings, anomers and mutarotation

A five- or six-membered ring forms when an $-OH$ group attacks the carbonyl internally, giving a hemiacetal (from an aldose) or a hemiketal (from a ketose). Glucose mainly forms a six-membered **pyranose** ring; fructose often forms a five-membered **furanose** ring. The carbonyl carbon becomes a new chiral centre called the **anomeric carbon** (C1 in glucose, C2 in fructose).

The two possibilities at that centre are **anomers**:

- $\alpha$: the anomeric $-OH$ is *trans* to the $CH_2OH$ group at the reference carbon;
- $\beta$: the anomeric $-OH$ is *cis* to it.

In solution the ring opens and recloses, interconverting the anomers until an equilibrium is reached. This **mutarotation** is seen in the specific rotation: pure $\alpha$-$D$-glucose has $[\alpha]_D \approx +112^\circ$, pure $\beta$-$D$-glucose about $+18.7^\circ$, and the equilibrium mixture about $+52.7^\circ$.

## 4. Disaccharides: the glycosidic bond

A **glycosidic bond** joins the anomeric carbon of one sugar to an $-OH$ of another, with loss of water.

| Disaccharide | Units | Linkage | Free anomeric carbon? | Reducing? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sucrose | glucose + fructose | $\alpha$-$C1 \to \beta$-$C2$ | no | non-reducing |
| Maltose | glucose + glucose | $\alpha$-$C1 \to C4$ | yes | reducing |
| Lactose | galactose + glucose | $\beta$-$C1 \to C4$ | yes | reducing |

In sucrose **both** anomeric carbons are tied up in the bond, so no ring can open to expose an aldehyde. That is why sucrose is non-reducing, while maltose and lactose each retain one free anomeric carbon.

## 5. Polysaccharides

- **Starch** is the plant storage polymer. Amylose is largely linear with $\alpha(1\to4)$ links; amylopectin adds $\alpha(1\to6)$ branch points roughly every 24–30 glucose units.
- **Cellulose** is the structural polymer of plant cell walls: linear chains joined by $\beta(1\to4)$ links. Human digestive enzymes recognise $\alpha$ links, not $\beta$ links, so cellulose passes through as dietary fibre.
- **Glycogen** is the animal storage polymer, similar to amylopectin but branched much more frequently (about every 8–12 units).

The change from $\alpha$ to $\beta$ at a single link is enough to turn a digestible energy store into an indigestible structural fibre.

## 6. Worked example: hydrolysing sucrose

Sucrose hydrolyses in acid (or with the enzyme invertase) according to

$$
\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + C_6H_{12}O_6}.
$$

Take $34.2\ \text{g}$ of sucrose. With $M(\text{sucrose}) \approx 342\ \text{g/mol}$,

$$
n=\frac{34.2\ \text{g}}{342\ \text{g/mol}}=0.100\ \text{mol}.
$$

The stoichiometry is $1:1:1:1$, so hydrolysis consumes $0.100\ \text{mol}$ of water ($1.8\ \text{g}$) and produces $0.100\ \text{mol}$ glucose ($18.0\ \text{g}$) and $0.100\ \text{mol}$ fructose ($18.0\ \text{g}$), because $M(\text{hexose}) \approx 180\ \text{g/mol}$.

**Sanity check:** products total $36.0\ \text{g}$ and reactants total $34.2+1.8=36.0\ \text{g}$. Mass balances, and the product mass exceeds the starting sucrose precisely by the mass of water added. The mixture is called invert sugar because its rotation changes sign as the products form.

## 7. Spotting a reducing sugar

A reducing sugar can reduce mild oxidising agents because its free anomeric carbon can open to a carbonyl. Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror, and Fehling's solution gives a brick-red precipitate of $Cu_2O$. Maltose and lactose answer both tests; sucrose does not until it has first been hydrolysed.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Anomers are not D and L">

Calling $\alpha$-$D$-glucose "L" because it rotates light one way confuses two independent labels. $\alpha/\beta$ describes the anomeric carbon in a ring; $D/L$ describes the penultimate carbon in the open chain. An $\alpha$-$D$ sugar and a $\beta$-$D$ sugar are both $D$. Also, a positive rotation is labelled $+$ or $d$, not $D$.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** How many stereoisomers are possible for an aldohexose, and how many of them belong to the $D$ series?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

An aldohexose has four chiral centres (C2–C5), so $2^4=16$ stereoisomers. They split evenly into eight $D$ and eight $L$ forms. The count assumes no meso reduction in symmetry, which holds for these sugars.

</details>

**B.** A disaccharide gives two molecules of $D$-glucose on hydrolysis and reduces Fehling's solution. Identify the linkage and explain the reducing behaviour.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The disaccharide is maltose, joined by an $\alpha(1\to4)$ glycosidic bond. The anomeric carbon of the second glucose unit is free, so that ring can open to an aldehyde group, which reduces $Cu^{2+}$ to $Cu_2O$. Sucrose would give glucose *and* fructose and would not reduce Fehling's solution.

</details>

**C.** Why can humans digest starch but not cellulose, although both are glucose polymers?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Starch uses $\alpha(1\to4)$ links and cellulose uses $\beta(1\to4)$ links. Human glycosidases fit the $\alpha$ geometry; they cannot bind and cleave the $\beta$ geometry. The monomer is the same, but the bond stereochemistry changes the enzyme's ability to recognise it.

</details>

## Remember

- Define a carbohydrate by structure — polyhydroxy aldehyde/ketone or its hydrolysis product — not by the formula $C_n(H_2O)_m$.
- Number stereoisomers as $2^n$ from the count of chiral centres, and assign $D/L$ from the penultimate carbon.
- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ differ only at the anomeric carbon; their interconversion is mutarotation.
- A sugar reduces Tollens' or Fehling's reagent only if it has a free anomeric carbon — sucrose does not.
- $\alpha$ links are digestible energy stores; $\beta$ links make structural fibre.

Next, proteins use the same condensation idea to join amino acids into chains, but the chemistry is dominated by nitrogen and by folding rather than by carbonyl rings.
