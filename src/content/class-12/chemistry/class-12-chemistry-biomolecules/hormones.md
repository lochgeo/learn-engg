## Chemical messages with a destination

A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, released into the blood and carried to **target cells** that carry the matching receptor. The message is not nutrition and not information in the genetic sense; it is a regulation signal. Insulin tells cells to take up glucose; adrenaline prepares the body for sudden effort. Same molecule, different tissues respond according to the receptors they display.

Endocrine glands are **ductless**: they secrete directly into the bloodstream, unlike sweat or salivary glands, which empty through ducts. Because the signal travels in blood, it reaches many tissues but affects only those with the right receptor — which is why hormones can be powerful at tiny concentrations.

## 1. Three chemical classes

The class determines where the receptor sits and how quickly the response appears.

| Class | Examples | Solubility | Receptor location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peptide / protein | insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, ADH | water soluble | cell surface |
| Steroid (from cholesterol) | cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone | lipid soluble | intracellular (cytoplasmic or nuclear) |
| Amino-acid derived | adrenaline, thyroxine, melatonin | varies | adrenaline: cell surface; thyroxine: nuclear |

Peptide hormones cannot cross the lipid membrane, so they bind a **cell-surface receptor** and trigger a **second messenger** such as cAMP inside the cell. The effect is fast — seconds to minutes — because it changes the activity of proteins already present. Steroid hormones diffuse through the membrane, bind an intracellular receptor and regulate **gene transcription**, so their effects appear more slowly but last longer.

## 2. Glands and what they signal

| Gland | Hormone | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pancreas ($\beta$ cells) | insulin | lowers blood glucose: uptake and storage as glycogen |
| Pancreas ($\alpha$ cells) | glucagon | raises blood glucose: glycogen breakdown and glucose synthesis |
| Thyroid | thyroxine ($T_4$), $T_3$ | raises basal metabolic rate |
| Thyroid | calcitonin | lowers blood calcium |
| Parathyroid | PTH | raises blood calcium |
| Adrenal medulla | adrenaline, noradrenaline | fight-or-flight response |
| Adrenal cortex | cortisol | stress response, raises blood glucose |
| Adrenal cortex | aldosterone | $Na^+$ and water retention, $K^+$ loss |
| Anterior pituitary | growth hormone, TSH, ACTH | growth; drives thyroid and adrenal axes |
| Gonads | testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone | reproduction and secondary sexual characteristics |

The hypothalamus links the nervous and endocrine systems: it sends releasing hormones to the pituitary and produces ADH and oxytocin, which are stored and released by the posterior pituitary.

## 3. Feedback keeps the system steady

Hormone secretion is usually controlled by **negative feedback**. When blood glucose rises after a meal, $\beta$ cells release insulin; glucose falls as cells take it up and the liver stores glycogen; the fall in glucose then reduces insulin secretion. Glucagon does the reverse when glucose is low. The pair acts like a thermostat rather than a switch.

The thyroid axis shows the same design one level up: hypothalamic TRH stimulates the pituitary to release TSH, TSH stimulates the thyroid to release $T_4$, and a high $T_4$ level feeds back to suppress both TRH and TSH. A goitre can result when this loop is broken — for example, an iodine-deficient thyroid cannot make enough $T_4$, so TSH stays high and the gland enlarges.

**Diabetes mellitus** illustrates failure of the glucose loop. In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of $\beta$ cells leaves too little insulin, so blood glucose stays high; insulin injections are essential. In type 2 diabetes, insulin is present but tissues respond poorly — insulin resistance — and the condition is often linked to lifestyle and obesity.

## 4. Worked example: converting a glucose reading

A fasting blood glucose reading is $90\ \text{mg/dL}$. Express it in $\text{mmol/L}$ and compare it with the usual reference range of $3.9$–$5.5\ \text{mmol/L}$. ($M(\text{glucose}) = 180\ \text{g/mol}$.)

First convert the units carefully:

$$
90\ \text{mg/dL}=\frac{0.090\ \text{g}}{0.10\ \text{L}}=0.90\ \text{g/L}.
$$

Then divide by the molar mass:

$$
c=\frac{0.90\ \text{g/L}}{180\ \text{g/mol}}=5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}=5.0\ \text{mmol/L}.
$$

**Sanity check:** $5.0\ \text{mmol/L}$ lies inside the reference range $3.9$–$5.5$, so this reading is normal. As a cross-check, the common diabetes threshold of $126\ \text{mg/dL}$ becomes $1.26/180=7.0\ \text{mmol/L}$, matching the quoted diagnostic value. Using $mg$ directly as $g$, or forgetting that $1\ \text{dL}=0.10\ \text{L}$, would give answers wrong by a factor of ten or a hundred.

## 5. A second numerical pattern

If the body contains about $5.0\ \text{L}$ of blood, the amount of glucose in the circulation at $5.0\ \text{mmol/L}$ is

$$
n=cV=(5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L})(5.0\ \text{L})=0.025\ \text{mol},
$$

or $0.025\times180=4.5\ \text{g}$ — roughly a teaspoon. Keeping this small pool within narrow limits is what the insulin–glucagon pair does continuously.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Messengers are not consumed by the message">

A hormone does not supply the energy for the response and is not used up in proportion to the effect; one molecule can trigger many second messengers through a receptor cascade. Equally wrong is saying insulin "destroys" glucose: it promotes uptake and conversion to glycogen, and the glucose is still chemically accounted for. Finally, do not mix up the receptor locations — steroid hormones cross the membrane and bind inside, whereas peptide hormones bind at the surface.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Why does adrenaline act within seconds while cortisol takes much longer?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Adrenaline is a catecholamine that binds a cell-surface receptor and works through second messengers, rapidly modifying proteins already in the cell. Cortisol is a steroid that must enter the cell, bind an intracellular receptor and change gene transcription and new protein synthesis, which inherently takes longer.

</details>

**B.** A blood glucose reading is $108\ \text{mg/dL}$. Convert it to $\text{mmol/L}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$108\ \text{mg/dL}=1.08\ \text{g/L}$, so $c=1.08/180=6.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol/L}=6.0\ \text{mmol/L}$. That is above the usual fasting range of $3.9$–$5.5\ \text{mmol/L}$ but below the diabetes threshold of $7.0$, so it suggests impaired fasting glucose rather than a diagnostic value on its own.

</details>

**C.** In the thyroid axis, what happens to TSH secretion when $T_4$ falls, and why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

TSH rises. Low $T_4$ reduces negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary, so more TRH and more TSH are released, driving the thyroid to try to restore $T_4$. Persistent low $T_4$ with high TSH points to an underactive thyroid rather than a pituitary problem.

</details>

## Remember

- Hormones are ductless-gland messengers; only target cells with the matching receptor respond.
- Peptide hormones bind surface receptors and use second messengers; steroid hormones bind intracellular receptors and change transcription.
- Negative feedback, as in the insulin–glucagon and thyroid loops, maintains homeostasis.
- Convert $mg/dL$ to $g/L$ before dividing by molar mass to obtain $mmol/L$.
- Type 1 diabetes is insulin deficiency; type 2 is insulin resistance.
