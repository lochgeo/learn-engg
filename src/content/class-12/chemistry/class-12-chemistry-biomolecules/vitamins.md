## Small molecules with outsized jobs

Scurvy killed sailors on long voyages centuries before anyone knew why, yet a few weeks of citrus fruit cured it. The responsible compound is ascorbic acid, needed in milligrams rather than grams — a **vitamin**. Vitamins are organic compounds that an organism requires in small amounts for normal metabolism but cannot synthesise in sufficient quantity, so they must come from the diet. They are not fuels and not building blocks; they are chemical helpers, and the previous lesson already showed where many of them work: as enzyme coenzymes.

## 1. Two solubility classes

The single most useful division is by solubility, because it predicts storage, dosing and toxicity.

| | Fat-soluble | Water-soluble |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamins | A, D, E, K | B-complex ($B_1$–$B_{12}$), C |
| Absorption | with dietary fat and bile | directly into the bloodstream |
| Storage | liver and adipose tissue | little storage; excess excreted in urine |
| Deficiency | develops slowly | develops relatively quickly |
| Excess | can accumulate and become toxic | generally low toxicity (B12 is an exception) |

The mnemonic "ADEK" separates the two groups. Because fat-soluble vitamins are stored, taking far more than the recommended amount can cause **hypervitaminosis** — excess vitamin A and D are the classic hazards. Water-soluble vitamins generally need regular intake because the body does not hold a large reserve.

## 2. The fat-soluble four

| Vitamin | Other name | Key role | Deficiency sign |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | retinol | visual pigment, epithelial tissue | night blindness, xerophthalmia |
| D | calciferol | $Ca^{2+}$ and phosphate absorption, bone mineralisation | rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults |
| E | tocopherol | antioxidant protecting cell membranes | rare; neurological and red-cell problems |
| K | phylloquinone | $\gamma$-carboxylation of clotting factors | bleeding tendency |

Vitamin D is unusual: the body can make it. Ultraviolet light converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin to cholecalciferol ($D_3$), which is then activated in the liver and kidney to **calcitriol**, the hormone-like active form that acts on intestinal cells. Vitamin $K_2$ is also produced by gut bacteria.

## 3. The water-soluble group and coenzymes

Most B vitamins are precursors of coenzymes — exactly the organic cofactors enzymes need.

| Vitamin | Coenzyme form | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $B_1$ thiamine | thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) | decarboxylation; deficiency causes beriberi |
| $B_2$ riboflavin | FAD, FMN | redox carrier |
| $B_3$ niacin | $NAD^+$, $NADP^+$ | redox carrier; deficiency causes pellagra |
| $B_5$ pantothenic acid | coenzyme A | acyl-group transfer |
| $B_6$ pyridoxine | pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) | amino-group transfer |
| $B_7$ biotin | carboxylase cofactor | $CO_2$ transfer |
| $B_9$ folic acid | tetrahydrofolate | one-carbon transfer; deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia |
| $B_{12}$ cobalamin | methylcobalamin | one-carbon transfer; deficiency causes pernicious anaemia |

Vitamin $B_{12}$ absorption needs a glycoprotein called **intrinsic factor** secreted by the stomach; its absence causes pernicious anaemia even when the diet contains plenty of $B_{12}$. During pregnancy, adequate folate ($B_9$) reduces the risk of neural-tube defects.

**Vitamin C** (ascorbic acid) is water soluble but is not primarily a coenzyme. It is a reducing agent that keeps iron in the absorbable $Fe^{2+}$ form and is required to hydroxylate proline and lysine during collagen synthesis. Deficiency causes scurvy: weakened connective tissue, bleeding gums and poor wound healing.

## 4. Worked example: a vitamin C tablet

A supplement tablet contains $1000\ \text{mg}$ of ascorbic acid, $C_6H_8O_6$. Find the amount in moles and the number of molecules.

First the molar mass:

$$
M=6(12.01)+8(1.008)+6(16.00)=72.06+8.06+96.00=176.12\ \text{g/mol}.
$$

Convert the mass to grams before dividing: $1000\ \text{mg}=1.000\ \text{g}$.

$$
n=\frac{1.000\ \text{g}}{176.12\ \text{g/mol}}=5.68\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol},
$$

$$
N=nN_A=(5.68\times10^{-3})(6.022\times10^{23})=3.42\times10^{21}\ \text{molecules}.
$$

**Sanity check:** a recommended daily allowance of vitamin C is about $90\ \text{mg}$, so one tablet is roughly eleven daily allowances. The arithmetic is consistent: $90/176.12 \approx 5.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$, and $5.68\times10^{-3}/5.1\times10^{-4}\approx 11$. Because vitamin C is water soluble, most of an extra-large dose is excreted rather than stored — a chemical reason not to equate "more" with "better".

## 5. Reading a deficiency pattern

The symptom usually points to the vitamin's job:

- poor night vision $\to$ vitamin A (visual pigment);
- bleeding or slow clotting $\to$ vitamin K (clotting factors);
- bone deformity in a growing child $\to$ vitamin D (calcium absorption);
- bleeding gums and poor wound healing $\to$ vitamin C (collagen);
- anaemia with large red cells $\to$ folate or $B_{12}$ (DNA synthesis).

Matching the *function* first, and the name second, is more reliable than memorising lists in isolation.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Not every vitamin is a coenzyme">

It is tempting to say "vitamins are coenzymes". Most B vitamins do become coenzymes, but A, C, D, E and K work mainly in other ways — visual cycles, antioxidant protection, gene regulation and protein modification. Another common slip is to assume water-soluble vitamins are harmless in any dose; even they can cause harm at very large intakes, although the risk is lower than for A and D.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** A patient on a strict fat-free diet is most likely to become deficient in which vitamins, and why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Vitamins A, D, E and K. They are fat soluble and need dietary fat and bile for absorption, so a diet containing essentially no fat impairs their uptake even if the vitamins are present in food.

</details>

**B.** A person eats plenty of meat but develops pernicious anaemia. Explain why the diet alone does not cure it.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The problem is absorption, not intake. Vitamin $B_{12}$ requires intrinsic factor secreted by the stomach to be absorbed in the ileum. Without intrinsic factor, dietary $B_{12}$ passes through unabsorbed, so supplementation by mouth may not help; the underlying absorption defect must be treated.

</details>

**C.** Why is vitamin D described as both a vitamin and a hormone?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

It is a vitamin because it must often be obtained from food, but skin can also synthesise it from 7-dehydrocholesterol using UV light. Its active form, calcitriol, travels in the blood to distant target cells and regulates gene transcription there — the behaviour of a hormone.

</details>

## Remember

- Split vitamins into fat-soluble ADEK and water-soluble B-complex plus C; the split predicts storage and toxicity.
- Most B vitamins become coenzymes (TPP, FAD, $NAD^+$, CoA, PLP), linking back to enzyme action.
- Vitamin C supports collagen synthesis and iron absorption; deficiency is scurvy.
- Convert milligrams to grams before using $n=m/M$, and count molecules with $nN_A$.
- Diagnose by function: the symptom usually points to what the vitamin does.
