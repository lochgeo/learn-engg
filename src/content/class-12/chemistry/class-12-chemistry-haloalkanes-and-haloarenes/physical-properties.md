## Why a polar molecule refuses to dissolve in water

A haloalkane carries a polar C–X bond, so it is tempting to expect it to dissolve in water. It usually does not. The molecule has no O–H or N–H group, so it cannot donate a hydrogen bond to water, and the energy needed to break water's hydrogen-bond network is not repaid. At the same time, the polar C–X bond raises the boiling point relative to an alkane of similar size. Physical properties therefore follow from a short list of intermolecular forces, not from the size of the dipole alone.

## 1. Boiling point: dispersion plus dipole–dipole attraction

Haloalkanes boil higher than alkanes of comparable molar mass because they have dipole–dipole attractions in addition to London dispersion forces. Chloromethane ($M=50.5$) boils at $-24\ ^\circ\text{C}$, while propane ($M=44$) boils at $-42\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The difference is not enormous, because the dipole is moderate and the molecules are small, but it is systematic.

Within a series, the boiling point rises with chain length as the electron cloud and surface area grow:

| Compound | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$ | $-24$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Cl}$ | $12$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2Cl}$ | $47$ |
| $\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Cl}$ | $78$ |

For the same alkyl group, the order is

$$
\mathrm{RF < RCl < RBr < RI}.
$$

This is the opposite of what a simple "most polar boils highest" argument predicts. The C–F bond is the most polar, but fluorine is small and its electron cloud is not easily distorted. Iodine is much more polarisable, so dispersion forces between R–I molecules are stronger and dominate. The methyl halides show the trend clearly: $\mathrm{CH_3F}$ boils at $-78\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$ at $-24\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $\mathrm{CH_3Br}$ at $3.6\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $\mathrm{CH_3I}$ at $42\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

Branching lowers the boiling point because a compact molecule has less surface contact with its neighbours. For the chlorobutanes:

| Isomer | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- |
| 1-chlorobutane | $78$ |
| 2-chlorobutane | $68$ |
| 2-chloro-2-methylpropane | $51$ |

Haloarenes boil higher than haloalkanes of similar size because the aromatic ring is larger and more polarisable. Chlorobenzene boils at $132\ ^\circ\text{C}$; bromobenzene at $156\ ^\circ\text{C}$; iodobenzene at $188\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

## 2. Melting point: packing beats polarity

Melting depends on how well molecules fit into a crystal lattice, so molecular shape matters as much as intermolecular attraction. Symmetrical molecules pack more efficiently and usually have higher melting points. The dichlorobenzenes are the standard example:

| Isomer | Melting point / °C |
| --- | --- |
| 1,2-dichlorobenzene (ortho) | $-17$ |
| 1,3-dichlorobenzene (meta) | $-25$ |
| 1,4-dichlorobenzene (para) | $53$ |

The para isomer is much more symmetrical, packs into a tighter lattice, and melts far above the ortho and meta isomers. A high boiling point does not guarantee a high melting point; the two properties probe different aspects of the condensed phase.

## 3. Solubility: like dissolves like

Haloalkanes are sparingly soluble in water. Chloroform dissolves to about $0.8\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{mL}$ of water, dichloromethane to about $1.3\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{mL}$, and carbon tetrachloride to only about $0.08\ \text{g}$ per $100\ \text{mL}$. These are not zero, but they are small compared with the solubility of an alcohol such as ethanol, which hydrogen-bonds to water.

The reason is energetic. Dissolving a haloalkane in water requires breaking some water–water hydrogen bonds, and the haloalkane can offer only dipole–dipole and dispersion interactions in return. The balance is unfavourable. In an organic solvent such as benzene, ether or acetone, the solvent–solute interactions are similar in kind and strength to the solvent–solvent interactions, so dissolution is easy. Haloalkanes are therefore good solvents for other organic compounds and poor solvents for ionic salts.

## 4. Density: which layer is on the bottom?

Density generally rises with the mass of the halogen and with the number of halogens:

| Compound | Density / g cm$^{-3}$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1-chlorobutane | $0.89$ |
| 1-bromobutane | $1.28$ |
| 1-iodobutane | $1.62$ |
| Dichloromethane | $1.33$ |
| Trichloromethane | $1.49$ |
| Tetrachloromethane | $1.59$ |
| Chlorobenzene | $1.11$ |
| Bromobenzene | $1.50$ |
| Iodobenzene | $1.82$ |

Not every haloalkane is denser than water. Chloromethane and chloroethane are gases at room temperature, and 1-chlorobutane is lighter than water. Bromides and iodides are usually denser, which is why a bromoalkane forms the lower layer in a separating funnel.

### Worked example: a layer calculation

Take $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of chloroform, density $1.49\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$, and $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of water, density $1.00\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$. The masses are

$$
m(\mathrm{CHCl_3}) = 25.0 \times 1.49 = 37.3\ \text{g}, \qquad m(\mathrm{H_2O}) = 25.0\ \text{g}.
$$

For the same volume, chloroform is heavier, so it forms the **lower** layer. The amount of chloroform is $n = 37.3/119.5 = 0.312\ \text{mol}$. The calculation assumes the two liquids are mutually insoluble enough for the volumes to be additive, which is a good approximation for chloroform and water.

## 5. Deriving molar mass from vapour density

The ideal-gas equation links density to molar mass. Starting from $PV=nRT$ and $n=m/M$,

$$
PV = \frac{m}{M}RT \quad\Rightarrow\quad M = \frac{mRT}{PV} = \frac{\rho RT}{P}.
$$

This is a standard way to identify a volatile haloalkane. At $373\ \text{K}$ and $1.00\ \text{atm}$, a vapour has density $4.47\ \text{g L}^{-1}$. With $R = 0.0821\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$,

$$
M = \frac{(4.47)(0.0821)(373)}{1.00} = 136.9\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

This is consistent with $\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}$, whose molar mass is $136.9\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$; 1-bromobutane is a plausible identity. The calculation assumes ideal-gas behaviour, complete vaporisation and no decomposition. Molar mass alone does not distinguish isomers: 1-bromobutane and 2-bromobutane have the same formula and the same mass.

## 6. Summary of the forces

| Force | Present in haloalkanes? | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| London dispersion | Yes, in every molecule | Rises with size and polarisability; dominates for iodides |
| Dipole–dipole | Yes, because C–X is polar | Raises boiling point relative to alkanes |
| Hydrogen bonding | No, unless another group is present | Explains low water solubility |

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Physical-property traps">

- **"The most polar bond gives the highest boiling point."** C–F is the most polar, but RI has the highest boiling point because dispersion forces dominate for heavy halogens.
- **"Polar means water-soluble."** A polar C–X bond does not provide hydrogen bonding, so water solubility is low.
- **"All haloalkanes are denser than water."** 1-chlorobutane is lighter than water; many small chloroalkanes are gases.
- **"Branching raises the boiling point."** Branching reduces surface contact and lowers the boiling point.
- **"Molar mass identifies the compound."** It identifies the formula, not the isomer; 1-bromobutane and 2-bromobutane have the same molar mass.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Arrange $\mathrm{CH_3F}$, $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$, $\mathrm{CH_3Br}$ and $\mathrm{CH_3I}$ in order of increasing boiling point.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The order is $\mathrm{CH_3F < CH_3Cl < CH_3Br < CH_3I}$. The molecules have similar size and shape, so the increasing polarisability and size of the halogen raise the dispersion forces faster than the decreasing bond dipole lowers them. The measured values are $-78$, $-24$, $3.6$ and $42\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

</details>

**B.** Why does 1,4-dichlorobenzene melt much higher than 1,2-dichlorobenzene, even though both have the same formula and similar molar mass?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The para isomer is more symmetrical, so its molecules pack more efficiently in the crystal lattice. Stronger crystal packing means more energy is needed to melt it. The ortho isomer is bent and packs less regularly. Boiling points are closer because both liquids have similar intermolecular forces; melting is more sensitive to shape.

</details>

**C.** A vapour of a chloroalkane has density $3.03\ \text{g L}^{-1}$ at $373\ \text{K}$ and $1.00\ \text{atm}$. Estimate its molar mass and suggest a formula.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$M = \rho RT/P = (3.03)(0.0821)(373)/1.00 = 92.8\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. A chloroalkane $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+1}Cl}$ has molar mass $14n + 36.5$, so $14n = 56.3$ and $n \approx 4$. The formula is $\mathrm{C_4H_9Cl}$, and the compound could be 1-chlorobutane or 2-chlorobutane.

</details>

## Remember

- Boiling point rises with chain length and with halogen mass ($\mathrm{RI > RBr > RCl > RF}$), and falls with branching.
- Melting point depends strongly on symmetry and crystal packing, not just on molar mass.
- Haloalkanes have no hydrogen-bond donor, so they are sparingly soluble in water and soluble in organic solvents.
- Density rises with halogen mass and number, but not every haloalkane is denser than water.
- Vapour density gives molar mass through $M=\rho RT/P$; it does not distinguish isomers. The same polar C–X bond that raises the boiling point makes the carbon electrophilic in the next module.
