## First choose the tool

| Clue in the question | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| A structure is given and a name is asked | Longest chain, halogen prefixes, lowest locant set, alphabetical tie-break |
| A name is given and a structure is asked | Draw the parent chain, then place each substituent at its locant |

| Boiling point or melting point is asked | Dispersion, dipole–dipole, branching and crystal packing |
| Solubility or density is asked | Hydrogen bonding, “like dissolves like”, halogen mass |
| A rate law is given | Second order means SN2; first order means SN1 |
| Stereochemistry is given | Inversion means SN2; racemisation means SN1 |

## Mastery check

Try each question without looking at the answer. Use the atomic masses $C=12.0$, $H=1.0$, $O=16.0$, $Cl=35.5$, $Br=79.9$ and $I=127.0$ where needed.

### 1. Name and classify

Give the IUPAC name of $\mathrm{CH_3CH(Cl)CH_2CH(CH_3)CH_3}$ and classify the carbon that carries chlorine.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The longest chain has five carbons. Numbering from the left gives chlorine at 2 and methyl at 4; numbering from the right gives methyl at 2 and chlorine at 4. Both give the set $\{2,4\}$, so the alphabetical tie-break applies: chloro comes before methyl, and chlorine receives the lower locant. The name is **2-chloro-4-methylpentane**.

The carbon carrying chlorine is bonded to two other carbons (a $\mathrm{CH_3}$ and a $\mathrm{CH_2}$), so it is **secondary**.

</details>

### 2. Leaving-group logic

Explain why iodide is a better leaving group than fluoride, even though the C–I bond is weaker.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

A leaving group departs with the bonding electron pair, so a stable anion is a good leaving group. Iodide is large and polarisable, and its negative charge is spread over a larger volume, so it is a weaker base and more stable than fluoride. The C–I bond is also weaker, which lowers the energy needed to break it. Fluoride is small, strongly solvated in protic solvents, and a strong base, so it is a poor leaving group. The order is $\mathrm{I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-}$.

</details>

### 3. Boiling-point ranking

Arrange these in order of increasing boiling point: 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The order is **2-chloro-2-methylpropane ($51\ ^\circ\text{C}$) < 1-chlorobutane ($78\ ^\circ\text{C}$) < 1-bromobutane ($102\ ^\circ\text{C}$) < 1-iodobutane ($130\ ^\circ\text{C}$)**.

The tertiary chloride is branched and compact, so it has less surface contact and weaker dispersion forces than the straight-chain chloride. Within the straight-chain halides, the halogen becomes larger and more polarisable from chlorine to iodine, so dispersion forces increase. The C–I bond is the least polar, but polarisability wins for boiling point.

</details>

### 4. Choose the mechanism

Predict the faster pathway and the main product for each pair:

(a) 1-bromobutane with NaCN in DMSO versus 2-bromo-2-methylpropane with NaCN in DMSO.

(b) 2-bromo-2-methylpropane with water versus 1-bromobutane with water.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) 1-Bromobutane is primary, cyanide is a strong nucleophile, and DMSO is polar aprotic, so **SN2** is fast and gives pentanenitrile. The tertiary halide cannot undergo SN2; with cyanide it may give some substitution by SN1, but elimination is a strong competitor. The primary halide is the faster SN2 case.

(b) 2-Bromo-2-methylpropane is tertiary and water is a weak nucleophile in a polar protic solvent, so **SN1** is fast and gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol. 1-Bromobutane is primary and cannot form a stable carbocation, so its SN1 reaction with water is extremely slow.

</details>

### 5. Stereochemistry

$(R)$-2-Bromobutane reacts with (a) aqueous NaOH and (b) water. Predict the product and the optical activity in each case.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) Hydroxide is a strong nucleophile and the substrate is secondary; SN2 dominates. Backside attack inverts the configuration, giving **$(S)$-butan-2-ol**. The product is optically active because one enantiomer predominates.

(b) Water is a weak nucleophile and a polar protic solvent; SN1 dominates. The planar carbocation is attacked from both faces, giving a **racemic mixture** of $(R)$- and $(S)$-butan-2-ol. The mixture is optically inactive.

</details>

### 6. Zaitsev versus Hofmann

2-Bromo-2-methylbutane is treated with (a) sodium ethoxide in ethanol under heat and (b) potassium tert-butoxide under heat. Predict the major alkene in each case and explain the difference.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(a) Ethoxide is a small, strong base, so E2 follows **Zaitsev's rule**. Removing a hydrogen from the $\mathrm{CH_2}$ group gives the more substituted alkene, **2-methylbut-2-ene**.

(b) tert-Butoxide is bulky, so it removes the more accessible hydrogen from one of the equivalent methyl groups. The major product is the less substituted alkene, **2-methylbut-1-ene** (Hofmann product).

Both reactions are E2; the difference is steric access to the β-hydrogen, not a change in mechanism.

</details>

### 7. Haloarene reactivity

Arrange chlorobenzene, 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene and 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene in order of increasing reactivity towards aqueous hydroxide by the SNAr mechanism. Explain the order.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The order is **chlorobenzene < 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene < 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene**.

SNAr proceeds by addition of hydroxide to the ring, forming a negatively charged Meisenheimer complex, followed by loss of chloride. Nitro groups at the ortho and para positions withdraw electron density and stabilise that negative charge, lowering the activation energy. Two nitro groups make the reaction feasible at about $370\ \text{K}$; three make it faster still. Unactivated chlorobenzene needs the extreme conditions of the Dow process.

</details>

### 8. Yield calculation

$13.7\ \text{g}$ of 2-bromobutane is heated with alcoholic KOH and gives $4.48\ \text{g}$ of a mixture of but-1-ene and but-2-ene. Calculate the percentage yield.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$M(\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}) = 136.9\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so $n = 13.7/136.9 = 0.100\ \text{mol}$. Each mole of halide can give one mole of alkene, and both alkenes have $M = 56.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. The theoretical mass is $0.100 \times 56.0 = 5.60\ \text{g}$.

The percentage yield is $4.48/5.60 \times 100 = 80.0\%$. The calculation assumes the reaction goes to completion in theory and that the product mixture is pure.

</details>

### 9. Chloroform storage

Why is chloroform stored in a dark, filled bottle with a little ethanol added?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Chloroform slowly reacts with oxygen in air and light to form phosgene, $\mathrm{COCl_2}$, which is highly toxic. A dark bottle and a full bottle reduce exposure to light and air. The added ethanol reacts with any phosgene formed, converting it to diethyl carbonate, so the stored liquid is safer. The ethanol also helps to inhibit further oxidation.

</details>

### 10. The aromatic case

Write the overall equation for the conversion of chlorobenzene to phenol by the Dow process. Then explain why chlorine is ortho/para-directing but deactivating in electrophilic substitution.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The Dow process uses aqueous sodium hydroxide at about $623\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{atm}$:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl + 2NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5ONa + NaCl + H_2O},
$$

followed by acidification to give phenol. The high temperature and pressure are needed because the unactivated aryl C–Cl bond is strong.

In electrophilic substitution, chlorine donates a lone pair into the ring by resonance, which raises electron density at the ortho and para positions and directs the incoming electrophile there. At the same time, chlorine is more electronegative than carbon and withdraws electron density inductively, so the ring is less reactive overall than benzene. The resonance effect controls orientation; the inductive effect controls the rate.

</details>
