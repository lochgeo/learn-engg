## A halogen turns an alkane into a reactive handle

Replace one hydrogen of ethane with bromine and the molecule changes character. Bromoethane is denser than ethane, far less flammable, and reacts at the carbon–bromine bond with water, hydroxide, cyanide and ammonia. That reactivity is why alkyl halides sit near the centre of organic synthesis: the halogen both polarises the carbon and acts as a leaving group. Put the same halogen on a benzene ring and the chemistry reverses — the bond becomes stronger and the ring, not the halogen, dominates the reactions.

Because the family is large, the first job is to classify a structure and name it without ambiguity. A correct name fixes the carbon skeleton and the position of every halogen, and therefore tells you which mechanism a later question is really testing.

## 1. Classification by the carbon that carries the halogen

| Class | General structure | Carbon bearing X | Example | Reactivity headline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Haloalkane (alkyl halide) | R–X | $sp^3$ | bromoethane | SN1, SN2, E1, E2 |
| Haloarene (aryl halide) | Ar–X | $sp^2$ aromatic | chlorobenzene | resists SN1/SN2; electrophilic substitution |
| Vinylic halide | X on C=C | $sp^2$ | chloroethene | very slow substitution |
| Allylic halide | X on C next to C=C | $sp^3$ | 3-chloroprop-1-ene | fast SN1/SN2 |
| Benzylic halide | X on C next to arene | $sp^3$ | chloromethylbenzene | fast SN1/SN2 |

The labels *allylic* and *benzylic* describe the position of the halogen, not a separate hybridisation class. The carbon carrying X is still $sp^3$, but the adjacent π system stabilises both the transition state and any carbocation. Vinylic and aryl halides are the opposite case: the carbon carrying X is $sp^2$, the C–X bond has partial double-bond character, and backside attack is blocked.

A second classification counts the halogens: monohalo ($\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$), dihalo ($\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_2BrCH_2Br}$), trihalo ($\mathrm{CHCl_3}$), tetrahalo ($\mathrm{CCl_4}$) and polyhalo generally. The count matters for density, boiling point and the number of possible substitution products.

A third classification counts the carbon atoms directly bonded to the carbon that carries X:

- **Primary (1°)**: one carbon neighbour, as in 1-chlorobutane.
- **Secondary (2°)**: two carbon neighbours, as in 2-chlorobutane.
- **Tertiary (3°)**: three carbon neighbours, as in 2-chloro-2-methylpropane.

This count is the best single predictor of SN1 versus SN2 behaviour. Neopentyl chloride, $\mathrm{1\text{-}chloro\text{-}2,2\text{-}dimethylpropane}$, is primary by this count but is so crowded that SN2 is very slow. Steric hindrance can override the label.

## 2. Nomenclature: parent chain, prefixes, locants

IUPAC naming for haloalkanes follows four steps:

1. Choose the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon bonded to X.
2. Number the chain so that the substituents receive the lowest possible set of locants.
3. Cite the halogens as prefixes: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo. Cite alkyl groups as methyl, ethyl and so on.
4. Arrange prefixes alphabetically. The multiplying prefixes di, tri and tetra are ignored when alphabetising.

If two numberings give the same locant set, the prefix cited first alphabetically receives the lower locant. For example, $\mathrm{CH_3CH(Cl)CH_2CH(Br)CH_3}$ is **2-bromo-4-chloropentane**, not 4-bromo-2-chloropentane: both directions give locants 2 and 4, and bromo comes before chloro.

Haloarenes are named from benzene as the parent, with the halogen as a prefix. Number to give the lowest locant set: chlorobenzene, 1-bromo-4-chlorobenzene and 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene. The carbon carrying the halogen is C1 only when that choice gives the lowest set; with several substituents, the lowest set decides.

Common names still appear in laboratory work and in exam questions:

| Common name | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- |
| n-propyl chloride | 1-chloropropane |
| isopropyl chloride | 2-chloropropane |
| tert-butyl bromide | 2-bromo-2-methylpropane |
| neopentyl chloride | 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane |
| vinyl chloride | chloroethene |
| allyl chloride | 3-chloroprop-1-ene |
| benzyl chloride | chloromethylbenzene |
| chloroform | trichloromethane |
| carbon tetrachloride | tetrachloromethane |

Benzyl chloride is a trap. Its $\mathrm{CH_2Cl}$ carbon is $sp^3$, so it behaves as a reactive haloalkane, not as a haloarene. Chlorobenzene, with chlorine directly on the ring, is the haloarene.

## 3. From a name to a formula: the board-exam pattern

A name can be converted into a molecular formula, a molar mass and a percentage composition. Take 2-bromobutane, $\mathrm{CH_3CHBrCH_2CH_3}$.

The skeleton has four carbons and nine hydrogens, plus one bromine: $\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}$. Using $C=12.0$, $H=1.0$ and $Br=79.9$,

$$
M = 4(12.0) + 9(1.0) + 79.9 = 136.9\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The mass percentage of bromine is

$$
\%\mathrm{Br} = \frac{79.9}{136.9}\times 100 = 58.4\%.
$$

A $2.50\ \text{g}$ sample therefore contains $2.50 \times 0.584 = 1.46\ \text{g}$ of bromine. The sanity check is that the halogen is a large fraction of the mass: replacing H (1.0) by Br (79.9) roughly doubles the molar mass of butane (58.0).

A useful formula for checking a structure is the **degree of unsaturation** (DBE). A halogen counts as one hydrogen, because it forms one bond:

$$
\mathrm{DBE} = \frac{2C + 2 - H - X}{2}.
$$

For $\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}$, $\mathrm{DBE} = (8 + 2 - 9 - 1)/2 = 0$: a saturated acyclic chain. For chlorobenzene, $\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl}$, $\mathrm{DBE} = (12 + 2 - 5 - 1)/2 = 4$: three double bonds plus one ring. With nitrogen present, use $(2C + 2 + N - H - X)/2$. The formula assumes a neutral, closed-shell molecule.

## 4. Why the classification predicts the chemistry

The C–X bond is polar, with carbon $\delta+$ and halogen $\delta-$. A nucleophile therefore attacks carbon and the halide leaves. The next module develops this bond in detail; for now, keep three consequences in view:

- A 3° halide forms a stable carbocation, so SN1 and E1 are accessible.
- A 1° halide is open to backside attack, so SN2 is fast unless the carbon is crowded.
- An aryl or vinylic halide has the halogen on an $sp^2$ carbon, with partial double-bond character and no accessible backside, so ordinary SN1/SN2 is shut down.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Naming and classification traps">

- **Treating the halogen as part of the parent name.** In this chapter the halogen is a prefix: 1-chlorobutane, not "butyl chloride" in IUPAC.
- **Calling chlorobenzene an alkyl halide.** The halogen is on an aromatic $sp^2$ carbon; it is a haloarene.
- **Numbering to give the halogen the lowest locant even when the overall set is worse.** Use the lowest set for all substituents, then the alphabetical tie-break.
- **Assuming every primary halide is fast in SN2.** Neopentyl chloride is primary but heavily hindered.
- **Reading benzyl chloride as a haloarene.** Its $\mathrm{CH_2Cl}$ carbon is $sp^3$, so it behaves like a reactive haloalkane.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Name $\mathrm{CH_3CH(Br)CH_2CH_2CH_2Cl}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The longest chain has five carbons. Numbering from the chlorine end gives locants 1 and 5; numbering from the bromine end gives 2 and 5. The lower set is 1,5, so the name is **5-bromo-1-chloropentane**. Bromo is cited before chloro alphabetically, so it appears first even though chlorine has the lower locant.

</details>

**B.** Classify each compound as a haloalkane, haloarene, vinylic halide or allylic halide: (i) chlorobenzene, (ii) 3-chloroprop-1-ene, (iii) 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (iv) chloroethene.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

(i) Haloarene: chlorine is on an aromatic $sp^2$ carbon. (ii) Allylic halide: chlorine is on an $sp^3$ carbon next to a C=C. (iii) Haloalkane, tertiary: three carbons are attached to the C–Cl carbon. (iv) Vinylic halide: chlorine is on a doubly bonded $sp^2$ carbon.

</details>

**C.** Find the degree of unsaturation of $\mathrm{C_5H_9Br}$ and state what it implies about the structure.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{DBE} = (2\times5 + 2 - 9 - 1)/2 = (12 - 10)/2 = 1$. The molecule has one ring or one double bond. It cannot be a saturated acyclic haloalkane, which would have formula $\mathrm{C_5H_{11}Br}$ and DBE 0.

</details>

## Remember

- A haloalkane has X on an $sp^3$ carbon; a haloarene has X on an aromatic $sp^2$ carbon. Allylic and benzylic halides are $sp^3$ but activated by an adjacent π system.
- Primary, secondary and tertiary count the carbons attached to the C–X carbon; steric crowding can override the label.
- IUPAC naming uses the longest chain, halogen prefixes, the lowest locant set and an alphabetical tie-break.
- DBE treats a halogen as a hydrogen: $(2C + 2 - H - X)/2$.
- The classification predicts the mechanism. The next module examines the C–X bond and how to make it.
