## The bond that makes the chapter work

A carbon–halogen bond is covalent, but it is not balanced. The halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon carries a partial positive charge and the halogen a partial negative one. That single polarisation explains why nucleophiles attack the carbon, why the halide can leave, and why the bond strength falls steadily from C–F to C–I. It also explains why a haloalkane is not simply "an alkane with a halogen stuck on": the C–X bond is a chemical handle.

This module examines that handle and the main ways to install it.

## 1. What the C–X bond looks like

In a haloalkane the carbon is approximately $sp^3$ hybridised. The C–X bond is a σ bond formed by overlap of a carbon $sp^3$ orbital with a halogen p orbital. Its properties change systematically down the group:

| Bond | Length / pm | Bond enthalpy / kJ mol$^{-1}$ | Dipole moment of $\mathrm{CH_3X}$ / D | Boiling point of $\mathrm{CH_3X}$ / °C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C–F | 139 | 452 | 1.85 | $-78$ |
| C–Cl | 178 | 351 | 1.87 | $-24$ |
| C–Br | 193 | 293 | 1.81 | $3.6$ |
| C–I | 214 | 234 | 1.62 | $42$ |

Two trends pull in opposite directions. The C–F bond is the most polar because fluorine has the largest electronegativity difference from carbon, yet the molecular dipole of $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$ is slightly larger: dipole moment is charge separation multiplied by distance, and the longer C–Cl bond more than compensates for its smaller charge separation. Bond strength falls from C–F to C–I because the larger halogen orbitals overlap poorly with the carbon orbital. Polarisability rises in the same direction, which is why the heavier halides have stronger dispersion forces and higher boiling points.

For reaction chemistry the important consequences are:

- The carbon is **electrophilic** ($\delta+$), so nucleophiles attack it.
- The halide is a **leaving group**, and leaving-group ability follows $\mathrm{I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-}$.
- The C–X bond is polar but cannot donate a hydrogen bond, so haloalkanes are poor solvents for ionic salts and poorly soluble in water.

## 2. Deriving the ionic character from the dipole moment

A useful board-exam calculation treats the bond as a pair of separated charges. For separated charge $q$ at distance $d$,

$$
\mu = qd.
$$

For $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$, $\mu = 1.87\ \text{D}$ and $d = 178\ \text{pm}$. Using $1\ \text{D} = 3.336\times10^{-30}\ \text{C m}$,

$$
\mu = 1.87(3.336\times10^{-30}) = 6.24\times10^{-30}\ \text{C m}.
$$

Hence

$$
q = \frac{\mu}{d} = \frac{6.24\times10^{-30}}{1.78\times10^{-10}} = 3.51\times10^{-20}\ \text{C}.
$$

The fraction of an electronic charge is $3.51\times10^{-20}/1.60\times10^{-19} = 0.219$, so the bond is about **22% ionic**. Equivalently, a fully ionic bond would have

$$
\mu_{\text{ionic}} = ed = (1.60\times10^{-19})(1.78\times10^{-10}) = 2.85\times10^{-29}\ \text{C m} = 8.55\ \text{D},
$$

and $1.87/8.55 = 0.219$. The result is sensible: the bond is strongly polar but not a salt. This is why haloalkanes dissolve in organic solvents rather than dissociating into ions in water.

## 3. Preparation from alcohols

Alcohols are the most common starting point because the OH group is easy to replace. The general transformation is

$$
\mathrm{R-OH + HX \rightarrow R-X + H_2O}.
$$

The order of reactivity is tertiary > secondary > primary. Tertiary and secondary alcohols can ionise through a carbocation; primary alcohols react more slowly, mainly by a bimolecular pathway. Hydrogen chloride needs anhydrous zinc chloride and heat; the mixture is Lucas reagent. Hydrogen bromide and hydrogen iodide are usually generated in situ, for example from NaBr and concentrated $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$, or from NaI and $\mathrm{H_3PO_4}$.

Three reagents give cleaner control:

**Phosphorus tribromide** converts alcohols to bromides:

$$
\mathrm{3\,R-OH + PBr_3 \rightarrow 3\,R-Br + H_3PO_3}.
$$

**Phosphorus pentachloride** gives chlorides:

$$
\mathrm{R-OH + PCl_5 \rightarrow R-Cl + POCl_3 + HCl}.
$$

**Thionyl chloride** is the preferred laboratory reagent for chlorides:

$$
\mathrm{R-OH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow R-Cl + SO_2 + HCl}.
$$

The last reaction is popular because both by-products are gases, so the product needs little purification. A base such as pyridine is often added to neutralise the HCl produced.

## 4. Preparation from alkanes and alkenes

**Free-radical halogenation** replaces a hydrogen on an alkane:

$$
\mathrm{CH_4 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} CH_3Cl + HCl}.
$$

Chlorination is not selective: further substitution gives $\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$ and $\mathrm{CCl_4}$. Bromination is much more selective for the more substituted carbon because the bromine radical abstracts a hydrogen through a later, more product-like transition state. Iodination is reversible and thermodynamically unfavourable unless the HI is removed by an oxidising agent such as nitric acid or hydrogen peroxide. Fluorination is too violent to control without special dilution.

**Addition to alkenes** is the other major route. Hydrogen halides add according to Markovnikov's rule:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CHBrCH_3}.
$$

With a peroxide, HBr adds anti-Markovnikov through a radical chain:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + HBr \xrightarrow{\text{peroxide}} CH_3CH_2CH_2Br}.
$$

The peroxide effect is useful for HBr only; HCl and HI do not reverse under ordinary conditions. Bromine or chlorine adds across the double bond to give a vicinal dihalide, and N-bromosuccinimide (NBS) brominates an allylic position:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + NBS \xrightarrow{h\nu} BrCH_2CH{=}CH_2}.
$$

## 5. Halogen exchange and haloarenes

Two exchange reactions convert one halide into another.

**Finkelstein reaction.** Treating a chloride or bromide with sodium iodide in dry acetone gives the iodide:

$$
\mathrm{R-Cl + NaI \xrightarrow{\text{acetone}} R-I + NaCl}.
$$

Sodium chloride is insoluble in acetone and precipitates, which drives the equilibrium to the right. The reaction is an SN2 substitution and works best for primary and secondary halides; tertiary halides tend to eliminate instead.

**Swarts reaction.** A chloride or bromide is converted to a fluoride with a metal fluoride:

$$
\mathrm{R-X + AgF \rightarrow R-F + AgX}.
$$

Silver fluoride is expensive, so industrial fluorination often uses cobalt(III) fluoride or anhydrous HF under controlled conditions.

Haloarenes are made differently. Direct electrophilic halogenation of benzene with $\mathrm{Cl_2/FeCl_3}$ or $\mathrm{Br_2/FeBr_3}$ gives chlorobenzene or bromobenzene. Fluorobenzene and iodobenzene are usually made through the diazonium salt, a route developed in the haloarene module.

## 6. Worked example: mass, yield and atom economy

Suppose $15.0\ \text{g}$ of propan-1-ol is converted to 1-chloropropane with thionyl chloride. Using $M(\mathrm{C_3H_8O}) = 60.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$,

$$
n = \frac{15.0}{60.1} = 0.2496\ \text{mol}.
$$

The reaction is 1:1, so the minimum mass of $\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ is

$$
m = 0.2496 \times 119.0 = 29.7\ \text{g}.
$$

The theoretical mass of 1-chloropropane, $M = 78.5\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, is

$$
m = 0.2496 \times 78.5 = 19.6\ \text{g}.
$$

The atom economy is the mass of the desired product divided by the total mass of reactants:

$$
\text{atom economy} = \frac{78.5}{60.1 + 119.0}\times100 = 43.8\%.
$$

The remaining mass leaves as $\mathrm{SO_2}$ and HCl. If the experiment gives $17.0\ \text{g}$ of product, the percentage yield is $17.0/19.6 = 86.7\%$. The check is consistent: some mass must be lost as gases, so atom economy below 100% is expected.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Preparation errors">

- **Using HCl alone on a primary alcohol.** Primary alcohols react very slowly without anhydrous $\mathrm{ZnCl_2}$ and heat; the Lucas test relies on this difference.
- **Writing water as the only by-product of thionyl chloride.** The by-products are $\mathrm{SO_2}$ and HCl, not water.
- **Treating chlorination as selective.** Chlorination gives a mixture; bromination is the selective radical reaction.
- **Applying the peroxide effect to HCl or HI.** Only HBr reverses its regiochemistry under ordinary conditions.
- **Using NaF in a Finkelstein reaction.** Fluoride is a poor leaving group, and the equilibrium does not favour the fluoride product; the Swarts reaction is used instead.
- **Forgetting the 3:1 stoichiometry with $\mathrm{PBr_3}$.** One mole of $\mathrm{PBr_3}$ converts three moles of alcohol.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Why is C–I the weakest bond in the series, while C–F is the most polar?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Bond strength depends on orbital overlap: iodine is large and its 5p orbital overlaps poorly with carbon's $sp^3$ orbital, so the C–I bond is weakest. Polarity depends on electronegativity difference: fluorine is much more electronegative than carbon, so C–F has the largest charge separation. The molecular dipole of $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$ can still exceed that of $\mathrm{CH_3F}$ because the C–Cl bond is longer.

</details>

**B.** How much $\mathrm{PBr_3}$ is needed to convert $0.20\ \text{mol}$ of propan-2-ol completely to 2-bromopropane?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The balanced equation is $\mathrm{3\,ROH + PBr_3 \rightarrow 3\,RBr + H_3PO_3}$, so $\mathrm{PBr_3}$ required is $0.20/3 = 0.0667\ \text{mol}$. With $M(\mathrm{PBr_3}) = 270.7\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, the mass is $0.0667 \times 270.7 = 18.1\ \text{g}$. In practice a slight excess is used.

</details>

**C.** You need 1-iodobutane from 1-chlorobutane. Which reaction would you use, and why not simply heat butane with iodine?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Use the Finkelstein reaction: 1-chlorobutane with NaI in dry acetone gives 1-iodobutane and precipitated NaCl. Direct iodination of butane is reversible, unselective and thermodynamically unfavourable because HI accumulates; an oxidising agent would be needed to remove it, and even then the mixture is difficult to control.

</details>

## Remember

- The C–X bond is polar with an electrophilic carbon; bond strength falls and leaving-group ability rises from C–F to C–I.
- Alcohols react with HX, $\mathrm{PBr_3}$, $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ or $\mathrm{SOCl_2}$; thionyl chloride is preferred for chlorides because the by-products are gases.
- Alkanes undergo radical halogenation, alkenes undergo addition, and NBS brominates allylic positions.
- Finkelstein converts R–Cl or R–Br to R–I in acetone; Swarts converts them to R–F.
- A percentage yield or atom-economy calculation must account for the atoms that leave in by-products.
