## A halogen on a ring is not a halogen on a chain

Chlorobenzene looks like chloromethane with a benzene ring attached, but it does not hydrolyse to phenol under ordinary reflux. The C–Cl bond is shorter and stronger because the chlorine lone pair is delocalised into the aromatic ring, giving the bond partial double-bond character. The ring also blocks backside attack. As a result, haloarenes resist the SN1 and SN2 reactions of haloalkanes and instead undergo electrophilic substitution on the ring, or nucleophilic substitution only under forcing conditions.

Polyhalogen compounds extend the same theme. They include useful solvents and refrigerants, but also chloroform's tendency to form phosgene, and the environmental problems of DDT and CFCs.

## 1. Structure and bonding in haloarenes

In chlorobenzene the chlorine is attached to an $sp^2$ aromatic carbon. The C–Cl bond length is about $169\ \text{pm}$, compared with $178\ \text{pm}$ in chloromethane, and the bond is stronger. The reason is resonance: a chlorine lone pair can be donated into the ring, so the C–Cl bond has some double-bond character. The molecular dipole moment is also smaller, $1.69\ \text{D}$ for chlorobenzene versus $1.87\ \text{D}$ for chloromethane, because the ring's π electrons are polarised in the opposite direction and partly cancel the C–Cl dipole.

Three consequences follow:

- The C–X bond is stronger, so the halide is a poorer leaving group.
- The carbon carrying X is $sp^2$, so a carbocation at that carbon would be very unstable.
- The ring is a flat, electron-rich face, so electrophiles attack the ring rather than the halogen.

## 2. Nucleophilic substitution in haloarenes

Ordinary SN1 and SN2 are not available. SN2 requires backside attack, which the ring blocks; SN1 would require an aryl carbocation, which is far too unstable. Nucleophilic substitution therefore needs either harsh conditions or an activated ring.

### The Dow process

Chlorobenzene can be converted to phenol by heating with aqueous sodium hydroxide at about $623\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{atm}$:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl + 2NaOH \xrightarrow{623\ \text{K},\ 300\ \text{atm}} C_6H_5ONa + NaCl + H_2O}.
$$

Acidification then gives phenol. The extreme conditions are necessary because the unactivated C–Cl bond is strong and the ring does not stabilise the anionic intermediate.

### The benzyne mechanism

With a very strong base such as sodium amide in liquid ammonia, chlorobenzene reacts through an **elimination–addition** pathway. The base removes a hydrogen ortho to chlorine, chloride leaves, and a benzyne intermediate forms:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl \xrightarrow{NaNH_2/NH_3} C_6H_4 + NaCl + NH_3}.
$$

The $\mathrm{C_6H_4}$ species is benzyne, drawn with a strained triple bond. It is highly reactive, so amide adds quickly to either carbon of the triple bond; protonation then gives aniline. For a substituted aryl halide, attack can occur at either end of the benzyne bond, so the amino group may end up on a different carbon from the one that carried the halogen. This is called **cine substitution**.

### Activated aryl halides: SNAr

Electron-withdrawing groups at the ortho or para positions stabilise the anionic intermediate, so the reaction becomes much easier. The mechanism is **addition–elimination**:

1. The nucleophile adds to the ring, forming a negatively charged **Meisenheimer complex**.
2. The leaving group departs, restoring aromaticity.

For example, 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide at about $370\ \text{K}$ to give 2,4-dinitrophenol. The two nitro groups withdraw electron density and stabilise the negative charge in the Meisenheimer complex. With three nitro groups (picryl chloride), the reaction is faster still.

The leaving-group order in SNAr is

$$
\mathrm{F > Cl > Br > I},
$$

which is the reverse of the SN1/SN2 order. Fluorine is the most electronegative halogen and stabilises the Meisenheimer complex most strongly, so it leaves fastest even though fluoride is a poor leaving group in substitution at $sp^3$ carbon.

## 3. Electrophilic substitution on the ring

The halogen is **ortho/para-directing but deactivating**. The lone pair donates electron density into the ring by resonance, which favours attack at the ortho and para positions. The halogen is also electronegative and withdraws electron density inductively, which makes the ring less reactive than benzene overall. The two effects act together: the ring is slower to react than benzene, but the products are mainly ortho and para.

Nitration of chlorobenzene gives a mixture of 2-nitrochlorobenzene and 4-nitrochlorobenzene, with the para isomer usually major because it is less hindered:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl + HNO_3 \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4} p\text{-}C_6H_4ClNO_2 + H_2O}.
$$

The ortho product, $o\text{-}C_6H_4ClNO_2$, forms alongside it.

Sulphonation and halogenation follow the same orientation. Friedel–Crafts reactions are difficult on a deactivated ring; strongly deactivated rings such as nitrobenzene fail outright, and chlorobenzene is a poor substrate. Exam questions usually concentrate on nitration, sulphonation and halogenation.

## 4. Preparing haloarenes

Direct halogenation of benzene uses a Lewis acid:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{FeCl_3} C_6H_5Cl + HCl}.
$$

Bromobenzene is made similarly with $\mathrm{Br_2/FeBr_3}$. Fluorobenzene and iodobenzene are usually made through the diazonium salt.

**Sandmeyer reaction.** Aniline is diazotised with sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid at $273\text{–}278\ \text{K}$, then treated with copper(I) chloride:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow{NaNO_2/HCl} C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{CuCl/HCl} C_6H_5Cl + N_2}.
$$

Copper(I) bromide gives bromobenzene. **Balz–Schiemann** reaction: the diazonium tetrafluoroborate salt is heated to give fluorobenzene. Potassium iodide gives iodobenzene.

## 5. Polyhalogen compounds

| Compound | Formula | Key facts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dichloromethane | $\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}$ | volatile solvent, bp $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$; low water solubility |
| Trichloromethane (chloroform) | $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$ | bp $61\ ^\circ\text{C}$, density $1.49\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$; oxidises to phosgene |
| Tetrachloromethane | $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ | bp $77\ ^\circ\text{C}$, density $1.59\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$; solvent and fire extinguisher; toxic |
| Iodoform | $\mathrm{CHI_3}$ | yellow solid, antiseptic; formed in the haloform reaction |
| Freon-11 | $\mathrm{CFCl_3}$ | refrigerant; releases chlorine radicals in the stratosphere |
| Freon-12 | $\mathrm{CCl_2F_2}$ | refrigerant and aerosol propellant; ozone-depleting |
| DDT | $\mathrm{C_{14}H_9Cl_5}$ | insecticide; lipophilic and persistent |
| BHC (benzene hexachloride) | $\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}$ | insecticide; the γ-isomer (lindane) is the active one |
| Halothane | $\mathrm{CF_3CHBrCl}$ | volatile anaesthetic |

**Chloroform** is prepared by heating ethanol or acetone with bleaching powder. It is a useful solvent and was once an anaesthetic, but it slowly oxidises in air and light to phosgene, $\mathrm{COCl_2}$, a highly toxic gas:

$$
\mathrm{CHCl_3 + \tfrac12 O_2 \rightarrow COCl_2 + HCl}.
$$

It is therefore stored in dark bottles, filled to the brim, with about $1\%$ ethanol. The ethanol converts any phosgene formed into harmless diethyl carbonate.

**DDT** is the classic example of a persistent organic pollutant. It is highly soluble in fats, so it accumulates in the food chain — a process called biomagnification — and it caused reproductive harm in birds. Many countries banned or restricted it, although it is still used in some regions for malaria control.

**CFCs** are stable in the lower atmosphere but break down in the stratosphere under ultraviolet light, releasing chlorine radicals that destroy ozone:

$$
\mathrm{CFCl_3 \xrightarrow{h\nu} CFCl_2^\bullet + Cl^\bullet},
$$

$$
\mathrm{Cl^\bullet + O_3 \rightarrow ClO^\bullet + O_2}.
$$

One chlorine atom can destroy many ozone molecules through a catalytic cycle, which is why even small emissions matter.

## 6. Worked example: chlorine content of DDT

DDT has formula $\mathrm{C_{14}H_9Cl_5}$. Its molar mass is

$$
M = 14(12.0) + 9(1.0) + 5(35.5) = 354.5\ \text{g mol}^{-1}.
$$

The mass of chlorine in one mole is $5(35.5)=177.5\ \text{g}$, so the percentage is

$$
\%\mathrm{Cl} = \frac{177.5}{354.5}\times100 = 50.1\%.
$$

A $2.00\ \text{g}$ sample therefore contains about $1.00\ \text{g}$ of chlorine. The result is a useful check on a formula: if a proposed structure gives a wildly different percentage, the number of chlorine atoms is probably wrong.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Haloarene and polyhalogen traps">

- **"Haloarenes undergo SN2 like haloalkanes."** The $sp^2$ carbon, the partial double bond and the blocked backside all prevent it.
- **"Chlorobenzene plus aqueous NaOH under reflux gives phenol."** The unactivated reaction needs about $623\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{atm}$, or an activated ring.
- **"Halogen is ortho/para-directing because it is activating."** It is ortho/para-directing but deactivating: resonance donation controls orientation, inductive withdrawal controls overall rate.
- **"SNAr follows the usual leaving-group order $\mathrm{I > Br > Cl > F}$."** In SNAr, fluoride is often fastest because it stabilises the Meisenheimer complex.
- **"Chloroform can be kept in a clear bottle open to air."** It forms phosgene; store it dark, full and with ethanol.
- **"DDT is harmless because it is insoluble in water."** Its fat solubility is exactly why it biomagnifies.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Why is chlorobenzene much less reactive than chloromethane towards hydroxide?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is shorter and stronger because the chlorine lone pair is delocalised into the ring, giving partial double-bond character. The carbon is $sp^2$, so a carbocation there is very unstable, and the ring blocks backside attack. Chloromethane has a normal $sp^3$ C–Cl bond, an accessible backside and a much better leaving-group geometry.

</details>

**B.** Predict the main products of the nitration of chlorobenzene and explain the orientation.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Chlorine is ortho/para-directing, so the products are 2-nitrochlorobenzene and 4-nitrochlorobenzene. The para isomer is usually major because the ortho position is more hindered. The ring is less reactive than benzene because chlorine also withdraws electron density inductively, but the resonance donation still favours the ortho and para positions.

</details>

**C.** Calculate the percentage of chlorine in chloroform.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

For $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$, $M = 12.0 + 1.0 + 3(35.5) = 119.5\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. The chlorine mass is $106.5\ \text{g}$, so $\%\mathrm{Cl} = 106.5/119.5 \times 100 = 89.1\%$. Chloroform is mostly chlorine by mass.

</details>

## Remember

- Haloarenes have a stronger, partial-double C–X bond and resist SN1/SN2.
- Unactivated aryl halides need harsh conditions; activated rings with ortho/para nitro groups react by SNAr through a Meisenheimer complex.
- Strong bases can force a benzyne elimination–addition, which may give cine substitution.
- Halogens are ortho/para-directing but deactivating in electrophilic aromatic substitution.
- Chloroform forms phosgene, DDT biomagnifies and CFCs destroy ozone; each is a different kind of hazard.
- The practice module and timed set mix these ideas; use the mechanism decision table before predicting products.
