## The table as a seating chart of electrons

The modern periodic table is not an arrangement to memorise; it is a picture of electronic configurations. Every element sits where its outermost electrons sit, so a configuration like $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^2 4s^2$ converts directly into a period number, a group number and a block. Learn the conversion once and you can locate all 118 elements without a memorised chart.

Two vocabulary items first, because the whole table hangs on them:

- **Period number** = the highest principal quantum number $n$ occupied in the ground-state configuration.
- **Block** = the subshell ($s$, $p$, $d$ or $f$) into which the *last* electron enters, in the Aufbau filling order.

## 1. From configuration to position

The rule for the group depends on the block:

| Block | Last electron enters | Groups | Group-number rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| s-block | $ns$ | 1 and 2 (plus He) | group number = number of outer electrons |
| p-block | $np$ | 13–18 | group number = 10 + number of outer electrons |
| d-block | $(n-1)d$ | 3–12 | read off the +10 convention, or ns + (n−1)d electrons |
| f-block | $(n-2)f$ | — (placed separately) | lanthanoids in period 6, actinoids in period 7 |

Helium is the one deliberate exception: its configuration is $1s^2$, yet it sits in group 18, because its chemistry (closed shell, noble-gas behaviour) is that of the group 18 column, not that of the alkaline earth metals.

**Examples.**

- Aluminium: $[\mathrm{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^1$. Outermost shell $n=3$, so period 3; outer electrons $2+1=3$, so group $10+3=13$; the last electron enters $3p$, so p-block.
- Iron: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2$. Period 4 (note $4s$ fills before $3d$); valence electrons $6+2=8$, group 8; d-block.
- Selenium: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^2 4p^4$. Period 4; outer electrons $6$, group 16; p-block.

The $d^{10}$ electrons in selenium's configuration are not counted as outer: group logic for s- and p-block elements uses only the electrons outside the noble-gas core.

## 2. The four blocks at a glance

| Block | Members | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s | H, He and groups 1–2 | reactive metals (H and He the odd ones out) |
| p | groups 13–18 | metals, metalloids, non-metals, noble gases |
| d | groups 3–12 | transition metals: hard, lustrous, good conductors |
| f | 57–71 (lanthanoids), 89–103 (actinoids) | inner transition metals, mostly drawn below the main table |

Two boundary notes used constantly in problems:

- The **transition metals** proper are d-block elements with a partially filled $d$ subshell in the element or its common ions. Zinc, cadmium and mercury ($d^{10}$ in both atom and common ion) sit in group 12 and are usually treated as d-block but not transition.
- The f-block elements sit inside periods 6 and 7 ($6s$ and $7s$ fill first), even though the electrons enter $4f$ and $5f$ subshells one and two rows deeper.

## 3. Why each period is as long as it is

A period runs from the first element that opens a new shell to the noble gas that closes it. Its length is therefore set by how many electrons that shell sequence can accept:

| Period | Opens with | Length | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | H ($1s^1$) | 2 | $n=1$ holds 2 electrons only |
| 2 | Li ($2s^1$) | 8 | $2s$ and $2p$: $2+6$ |
| 3 | Na ($3s^1$) | 8 | $3s$ and $3p$: $2+6$; $3d$ waits for the next round |
| 4 | K ($4s^1$) | 18 | $4s$, then $3d$ ($10$), then $4p$ ($6$) |
| 5 | Rb ($5s^1$) | 18 | $5s$, $4d$, $5p$ |
| 6 | Cs ($6s^1$) | 32 | $6s$, $4f$ ($14$), $5d$, $6p$ |
| 7 | Fr ($7s^1$) | 32 | $7s$, $5f$, $6d$, $7p$ (completed to element 118) |

The odd reshuffling — $4s$ before $3d$ — follows from energy: the $(n+l)$ ordering places $4s$ (value 4) below $3d$ (value 5). This is why period 4 suddenly contains eighteen elements instead of eight, and where the metallic centre of the table appears.

## 4. Metals, non-metals and metalloids

A staircase line running from boron down to tellurium separates the electropositive left of the table from the electronegative right:

- **Metals** lie left of and below the line — groups 1, 2, the d-block, the f-block and the lower p-block. They are lustrous, malleable, conduct electricity and heat, lose electrons to form cations, and their oxides tend to be basic.
- **Non-metals** lie above and right of the line — hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, the halogens and the noble gases. They are brittle or gaseous, poor conductors, form anion-like species or covalent bonds, and their oxides tend to be acidic.
- **Metalloids** step along the line — boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. They conduct middling well (silicon's semiconductivity built the electronics industry) and show intermediate chemistry, including amphoteric oxides.

Metallic character is not fixed within a group: it grows down every group. In group 14, carbon is a non-metal, silicon and germanium are metalloids, and tin and lead are metals.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Placement errors that recur">

- **Putting helium in group 2.** Its configuration ($1s^2$) looks alkaline-earth-like, but its filled shell governs; it belongs with the noble gases.
- **Reading lanthanoid atomic numbers as if they sat in period 4 or 5.** Elements 57–71 fill $4f$ but sit in **period 6** (their outermost electrons are $6s$); actinoids sit in period 7.
- **Calling every element left of the metalloids a "metal" including hydrogen.** Hydrogen is classified separately: it gives up one electron like group 1 but forms a covalent chemistry all its own.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Locate manganese ($Z=25$) in the table: period, group and block.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Configuration: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^2$. Highest $n$ is 4, so period 4. Valence electrons $5+2=7$, so group 7. The last electron enters $3d$, so it is a d-block transition metal.
</details>

**2.** How many elements have their outermost electrons in the shell $n=3$?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Exactly the eight elements sodium to argon, whose outermost electrons run $3s^1$ through $3s^2 3p^6$. Potassium and calcium come next with outermost $4s$, so they do not count. (For the same reason period 3 has eight elements.)
</details>

**3.** An element has the configuration $[\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7 5d^1 6s^2$. Identify its period, block and family.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Outermost shell is $n=6$, so period 6; the electron of interest enters $4f$, so the f-block; with $4f^7$ it is the middle of the lanthanoid series — the element is gadolinium ($Z=64$).
</details>

## Remember

- Period number is the highest $n$ in the configuration; block is the subshell the last electron enters.
- Groups 1–2 are s-block; 13–18 are p-block with group = 10 + outer electrons; 3–12 are d-block; f-block subrows sit in periods 6 and 7.
- Period lengths 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 follow directly from subshell capacities and the Aufbau order.
- The s/p boundary staircase separates metals from non-metals, with metalloids on the line.
- Helium is s-block by configuration but group 18 by behaviour.

Position is only the start. Everything the table predicts — size first — comes from the tug between nucleus and outer electrons, which is the subject of the next module.
