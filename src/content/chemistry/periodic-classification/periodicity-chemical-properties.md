## Same outer shell, same chemistry — almost

Every qualitative trend so far — radius, ionisation, electron pull — pointed at one claim: the outermost electrons decide the chemistry. This module cashes that claim in. Walk across any period and the metallic character, the acid–base behaviour of the oxides, and the nature of the hydrides all march in step with the electronic changes; walk down a group and they march the other way. Then meet the two wrinkles that make the study interesting: the second-period elements that break their own family's pattern, and the diagonal pairings that run across the grain of the table.

## 1. Metallic character

An atom behaves as a metal when it lets electrons go cheaply: big radius, low ionisation enthalpy, low electronegativity. Since size grows and ionisation falls down a group and the reverse along a period:

- metallic character **decreases across a period** from the alkali metal to the halogen/noble-gas end;
- metallic character **increases down a group**, which is why the light p-block can hold non-metals while the heavy p-block holds metals: nitrogen is a gas, bismuth is a metal.

Caesium (0.7 on the Pauling scale) is the most reactive metal of the stable elements; fluorine (4.0) anchors the opposite corner. Francium is theoretically placed beyond caesium but exists only in trace amounts, radioactive.

## 2. Oxides: the acid–base mirror

Oxide character is the single most revealing chemical trend across a period. Period 3 carries the whole story in seven oxides:

| Element | Oxide | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Na | $\mathrm{Na_2O}$ | strongly basic |
| Mg | $\mathrm{MgO}$ | basic |
| Al | $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ | amphoteric |
| Si | $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ | weakly acidic |
| P | $\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}$ | acidic |
| S | $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{SO_3}$ | acidic |
| Cl | $\mathrm{Cl_2O_7}$ | strongly acidic |

The basic-to-acidic walk mirrors the metallic-to-non-metallic walk, because oxide nature follows the metal character of the element: a metallic element's oxide reacts like a solid containing $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$, which grabs protons; a non-metal's oxide holds oxygen covalently and instead accepts electron pairs, behaving as an acid.

$$
\mathrm{Na_2O + H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH}
$$

$$
\mathrm{MgO + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2O}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Cl_2O_7 + H_2O \rightarrow 2HClO_4}
$$

Aluminium sits exactly on the metal–non-metal boundary and shows both faces:

$$
\mathrm{Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH + 3H_2O \rightarrow 2Na[Al(OH)_4]}
$$

Down group 2 the same trend runs the other way: $\mathrm{BeO}$ is amphoteric (like $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$, which is no coincidence — see the diagonal relationship below), and each oxide below it dissolves less easily in alkali and more readily in acid.

A second, purely structural trend rides along: the bonding across period 3 shifts from ionic ($\mathrm{Na_2O}$, $\mathrm{MgO}$) through intermediate ($\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) to clearly covalent ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$, $\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2O_7}$), as the electronegativity difference between element and oxygen shrinks.

## 3. Hydrides

The hydrides of period 2 tell the metallic-character story from the hydrogen end:

| Hydride | $\mathrm{LiH}$ | $\mathrm{BeH_2}$ | $\mathrm{B_2H_6}$ | $\mathrm{CH_4}$ | $\mathrm{NH_3}$ | $\mathrm{H_2O}$ | $\mathrm{HF}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Character | ionic | polymeric covalent | electron-deficient | covalent, neutral | basic | neutral, polar | acidic |

Across the period the element–hydrogen electronegativity difference shrinks, so ionic character drains away; lithium hydride contains $\mathrm{H^-}$ ions, while hydrogen fluoride is a covalent, strongly polar acid.

Two tidy sub-trends appear in hydride chemistry:

- **Thermal stability rises along period 2** and **falls down a group**: $\mathrm{HF > HCl > HBr > HI}$ for bond strength, so hydrogen iodide gives up its hydrogen (as a reducing agent or an acid) most easily of the four.
- **Acidity follows the reverse**: $\mathrm{HF < HCl < HBr < HI}$ in water, and $\mathrm{CH_4 < NH_3 < H_2O < HF}$ across period 2. Stability and ease of proton release are two sides of the same bond bookkeeping.

## 4. Why the second period breaks the pattern

The elements lithium to fluorine sit apart from their heavier group-mates for four stacked reasons:

1. **Very small size** — unusually concentrated charge density, stronger specific interactions (lattice and hydration energies, hydrogen bonding: $\mathrm{HF}$ is a liquid, $\mathrm{HCl}$ is a gas).
2. **Very high ionisation enthalpy and electronegativity** — the lightest member of a group is always the least metallic member.
3. **No accessible $d$ orbitals** — the valence set is one $s$ and three $p$ orbitals, eight slots at most, so second-period elements cannot expand their octet. Nitrogen stops at $\mathrm{NCl_3}$; phosphorus goes on to $\mathrm{PCl_5}$. Anions like $\mathrm{CF_6^{2-}}$ or $\mathrm{CCl_6^{2-}}$ do not form, while $\mathrm{SiF_6^{2-}}$ does.
4. **Strong multiple bonding** — the compact $2p$ orbitals overlap side-on well, so carbon and nitrogen build $\pi$ systems with ease: $\mathrm{N_2}$ is a triple-bonded gas while white phosphorus is $\mathrm{P_4}$ with only single bonds.

Two experimentally memorable consequences: carbon tetrachloride resists hydrolysis because carbon offers no low-energy orbital for water to attack, while silicon tetrachloride hydrolyses readily through its vacant $3d$ orbitals; and nitrogen's triple bond makes $\mathrm{N_2}$ so inert that its chemistry overwhelmingly needs high temperatures or catalysts.

## 5. Diagonal relationships

One more piece of order runs across the grain of the table: an element resembles the element one period down and one group right — its **diagonal partner** — almost as much as its own group. The severest cases sit at the sharp top-left of the p-block where size changes fastest between periods:

| Pair | Shared traits |
| --- | --- |
| Li–Mg | both form nitrides ($\mathrm{Li_3N}$, $\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}$) directly from the elements; carbonates decompose on heating (unlike $\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}$); both form useful organometallic reagents |
| Be–Al | amphoteric oxides ($\mathrm{BeO}$, $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) and hydroxides soluble in both acid and alkali; covalent, volatile halides; carbides that release hydrocarbons with water |
| B–Si | acidic, glass-forming covalent oxides ($\mathrm{B_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{SiO_2}$); covalent, volatile hydrides; both semiconducting or borderline |

The cause is a size compromise: a diagonal step (down one period) roughly cancels the right step (smaller, more electronegative), so the paired elements match in radius and electronegativity more closely than the vertical neighbours do.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Chemical-trend errors to avoid">

- **Reading the oxide row mechanically and calling $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ basic.** Amphoteric is a testable claim, not a hedge: two opposing equations prove it.
- **Claiming acidity increases down a halogen-hydride series because electronegativity is quoted there.** Down a group electronegativity falls while aqueous acidity rises — bond strength, not electronegativity, decides.
- **Forgetting the hydrolysis asymmetry.** $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ is inert to water while $\mathrm{SiCl_4}$ fumes and hydrolyses — the classic second-period evidence, often feared instead of used.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Write the two balancing equations that establish $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ as amphoteric.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

As a base, reacting with acid: $\mathrm{Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O}$. As an acid, reacting with concentrated alkali: $\mathrm{Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH + 3H_2O \rightarrow 2Na[Al(OH)_4]}$. One reaction shows electron-pair acceptance (acid behaviour), the other proton acceptance (base behaviour).
</details>

**2.** Arrange $\mathrm{Na_2O}$, $\mathrm{MgO}$, $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, $\mathrm{SO_3}$ in order of increasing acidic character and justify with one sentence of theory.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{Na_2O < MgO < SiO_2 < SO_3}$. Moving right across period 3 the element becomes less metallic and more electronegative, so its oxide shifts from ionic basic through weakly acidic to strongly acidic.
</details>

**3.** Phosphorus forms $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ but nitrogen does not form $\mathrm{NCl_5}$. Explain both limits.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Nitrogen's valence set has only four orbitals ($2s$, three $2p$) and no accessible $d$ orbital, so it cannot exceed the octet with five bonds. Phosphorus has vacant, not-too-high $3d$ orbitals, so sharing beyond the octet is sterically and energetically possible.

</details>

## Remember

- Metallic character falls across a period and rises down a group; it is read directly from radius, ionisation enthalpy and electronegativity together.
- Oxides mirror metals: basic on the left, amphoteric at the boundary, acidic on the right, with $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ as the two-sided demonstration case.
- Hydrides shift from ionic to covalent across a period; stability falls down a group while aqueous acidity rises.
- Second-period oddities come from small size, high electronegativity, an unexpandable octet and easy multiple bonding.
- Diagonal pairs Li–Mg, Be–Al and B–Si share traits because the size and electronegativity changes along the diagonal roughly cancel.
