## First choose the tool

| Clue in the question | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| "Period", "group", "block" of an element | Configuration: highest $n$, last-entering subshell, 10 + outer electrons |
| Size comparison of atoms or ions | Same-number-of-electrons first, then rank by $Z$; never mix radius definitions |
| Which loses an electron more easily | Ionisation enthalpy: size, $Z_{eff}$, shielding, configuration stability |
| "Explain the dip between X and Y" | Be–B/Mg–Al and N–O/P–S dips: penetration and half-filled/fully-filled stability |
| Which accepts an electron more readily | Electron gain enthalpy; check for the cramped-shell anomalies (F vs Cl, O vs S) |
| Bond polarity or " attracts shared electrons more" | Electronegativity, not electron gain enthalpy |
| Basic, amphoteric or acidic oxide | Metallic character: left = basic, boundary = amphoteric, right = acidic |
| Why the second period behaves differently | No vacant $d$ orbitals, small size, strong $\pi$ bonding |
| "Which element resembles which?" | Diagonal pairs: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si |

## Mastery check

Try each without looking at the answer first.

### 1. Ordering a mass anomaly

Mendeleev met argon (39.9) and potassium (39.1). Where did each element finally sit in the modern table, and what determined it?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Argon sits in group 18 (period 3), potassium in group 1 (period 4). The order parameter is atomic number: $Z_{\mathrm{Ar}} = 18 < Z_{\mathrm{K}} = 19$, so argon comes first despite being heavier. The pair is one of the classical demonstrations that mass, not nuclear charge, was the wrong variable.

</details>

### 2. Position from a configuration

An element has the configuration $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^2 4s^2$. State its period, group, block and name.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Highest $n = 4$, so period 4; the last electron enters $3d$, so d-block; valence electrons $2 + 2 = 4$, group 4. The element is titanium ($Z = 22$). Remember to add the $s$ and $d$ electrons together for d-block group numbers.

</details>

### 3. Radius ranking with a twist

Arrange in order of decreasing size: $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{S^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{Cl}$, $\mathrm{Ar}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{S^{2-} > Cl^- > Ar > Cl}$. $\mathrm{S^{2-}}$ (18 electrons, $Z = 16$) pips $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ (18 electrons, $Z = 17$) on nuclear charge alone. Neutral argon and the anions differ in electron count, so compare qualitatively: the anions carry extra repulsion without extra pull, so both exceed isoelectronic-sized argon, and neutral covalent chlorine ($99\ \text{pm}$ covalent) is clearly the smallest of the set.

</details>

### 4. Reading a jump

Successive ionisation enthalpies of an element are 738, 1451, 7733, 10 550, 13 513 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. Which group is it in, and why?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

The jump comes between the second and third values ($\times 5.3$), so two electrons sit outside a noble-gas core: group 2. The values are magnesium's. A jump ratio near or above 3 marks the core; ratios near 2 are ordinary same-shell steps.

</details>

### 5. The period 2 dips

Arrange in increasing first ionisation enthalpy: B, Be, N, O. Then explain in one sentence each why the two "wrong" positions occur.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{B\ (801) < Be\ (900) < O\ (1314) < N\ (1402)}$. Even though boron has one electron more than beryllium, its lone $2p$ electron is further out and less penetrating than a $2s$ electron, so it leaves more easily; oxygen's fourth $2p$ electron is paired, adding repulsion, while nitrogen's $2p^3$ is exactly half-filled and specially stable, so oxygen sits below nitrogen.

</details>

### 6. Most willing acceptor

Which of F, Cl, N, Ne has the most negative electron gain enthalpy, and which of these accepts almost nothing? Justify both.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Chlorine ($-349\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) is the most willing acceptor: fluorine's cramped $2p$ shell raises the interelectronic repulsion, pushing its value to only $-328$. Neon accepts essentially nothing (positive value): its closed shell would force the electron into a new, unattracted outer shell. Nitrogen sits close to zero because its $2p^3$ half-filled subshell resists pairing.

</details>

### 7. Polarity from scales

The bond enthalpies are $\mathrm{H-H} = 436$, $\mathrm{I-I} = 151$, $\mathrm{H-I} = 299\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. Estimate $|\chi_{\mathrm{I}} - \chi_{\mathrm{H}}|$ on Pauling's scheme and state which atom is $\delta^-$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Convert to kcal: 104, 36.1, 71.5. Reference: $\tfrac{1}{2}(104 + 36.1) = 70.05$; excess $\Delta = 71.5 - 70.05 = 1.45$; difference $= 0.208\sqrt{1.45} = 0.208(1.2) \approx 0.25$. Iodine ($\chi \approx 2.7$) exceeds hydrogen ($2.2$), so iodine carries the $\delta^-$ end. The bond is only slightly polar — consistent with hydrogen iodide being a moderate dipole and a strong acid.

</details>

### 8. Acid–base ladder

Arrange in order of increasing basic character: $\mathrm{MgO}$, $\mathrm{Na_2O}$, $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{SiO_2}$. Which is amphoteric?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{SiO_2 < Al_2O_3 < MgO < Na_2O}$. Basic character reverses the acidic trend across period 3: sodium is the most metallic, silicon the least. $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ is amphoteric, sitting on the metal–non-metal boundary and reacting with both HCl and NaOH.

</details>

### 9. Octet economics

Explain in your own words why $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ exists while $\mathrm{NCl_5}$ does not — and connect the same reasoning to $\mathrm{SiCl_4}$ hydrolysing while $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ does not.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Second-period atoms have only one $s$ and three $p$ valence slots (four orbitals, eight electrons); no low-lying $d$ orbital exists, so both $\mathrm{NCl_5}$ and any six-coordinate carbon species are impossible by construction. Third-period atoms have vacant $3d$ orbitals: phosphorus can share five pairs, and silicon can accept lone pairs from water molecules in the hydrolysis mechanism — which is exactly why $\mathrm{SiCl_4}$ is destroyed by water and $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ is indifferent to it.

</details>

### 10. The diagonal test

A learner claims lithium behaves "more like sodium than anything else in the table". Give two experimental facts supporting the diagonal Li–Mg pairing against that claim.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

(1) Both lithium and magnesium form nitrides directly from the elements ($\mathrm{Li_3N}$ and $\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}$), while sodium does not react with nitrogen under comparable conditions. (2) Both lithium carbonate and magnesium carbonate decompose on heating to the oxide with release of $\mathrm{CO_2}$, while sodium carbonate is thermally stable. Similar chemistry despite different vertical groups is the diagonal relationship at work.

</details>
