## Why chemistry needed a map

By the 1860s a working chemist could meet 63 different elements, each with its own density, melting point, oxide and valency. Keeping that catalogue in the head was hopeless unless the elements could be ordered so that look-alikes sat together. Atomic mass was the only quantity measurable for every element, so the natural move was to stack them in increasing mass — and the surprises that ordering produced led, over about forty years, from several half-right schemes to the modern periodic law.

## 1. Early orderings: triads, octaves and curves

**Döbereiner's triads (1829).** Some elements form threes of similar chemistry in which the mass of the middle one is close to the mean of the outer two:

$$
M_B \approx \frac{M_A+M_C}{2}.
$$

Lithium ($7.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$), sodium ($23.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$) and potassium ($39.1\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$): the mean of 7.0 and 39.1 is $23.1$ — sodium, almost exactly. Calcium, strontium and barium (40.1, 87.6, 137.3): the mean is $88.7$, close to strontium's 87.6. The triads were genuine, but only a handful of the known elements fitted them, so the idea stalled.

**Newlands' law of octaves (1865).** Listing elements by increasing mass, Newlands noticed that every eighth element (counting from the start of the list) repeated the properties of the first, like the eighth note of a musical scale repeating. The pattern worked up to calcium and then collapsed; chemists ridiculed the musical analogy because heavier elements kept breaking the count. The repetition was real, but the ordering variable was not yet right.

**Lothar Meyer's volume curves (1868).** Meyer plotted atomic volume against atomic mass and obtained a curve that repeated its same distinctive shape — peaks on the alkali metals, dips on the small central elements — once every so many elements. He saw the periodicity clearly, but Mendeleev, who published first and made testable predictions, captured the credit and the influence.

## 2. Mendeleev's table and its predictions

In 1869 Mendeleev arranged the elements in order of increasing atomic mass, starting a new row whenever the chemical pattern required it, so that elements of similar behaviour stood in the same vertical column, or **group**. Two decisions made the table more than a tidy chart:

- **Gaps as predictions.** Where the pattern demanded an element no one had found, he left a space and named it after its lighter neighbour in the group above: *eka*-boron, *eka*-aluminium, *eka*-silicon (*eka* = one in Sanskrit). For each he predicted atomic mass, density and the formulae of its oxide and other compounds.
- **Chemistry over mass.** Wherever mass and chemistry clashed, he inverted the pair rather than spoil a group: tellurium (127.6) before iodine (126.9), cobalt (58.9) before nickel (58.7), and — once argon was discovered — argon (39.9) before potassium (39.1).

### Worked example: checking an *eka*-aluminium prediction

Mendeleev predicted for eka-aluminium an atomic mass of about 68 and a density near $5.9\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$. Gallium, discovered in 1875, measured 69.7 and $5.91\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$.

To size the agreement, compare relative differences. For the mass, $\frac{69.7-68}{69.7}=0.024$, about $2\%$ — remarkable for a quantity the predictor could only extrapolate. For the density, $\frac{5.91-5.9}{5.91}=0.002$, a fraction of a per cent. A prediction that lands within a few per cent, made four years before the element existed, is strong evidence that the ordering captures something deep — and gallium's discovery is the moment the periodic law stopped being a classification and became a theory.

The same test passed for eka-silicon: Mendeleev predicted mass about 72 and density near 5.5; germanium, found in 1886, measured 72.6 and 5.32.

## 3. Moseley's evidence: the order parameter is Z

The inverted pairs (Ar/K, Co/Ni, Te/I) and the crowding of the rare earths showed that atomic mass is the wrong ruler. In 1913 Moseley measured the frequency $\nu$ of a characteristic X-ray line for a long run of elements and found a strikingly simple rule:

$$
\sqrt{\nu}=a(Z-b),
$$

a straight line when $\sqrt{\nu}$ is plotted against $Z$, the atom's **atomic number** — its number of protons. The X-ray spectrum of every element sits in an exact, gap-free sequence fixed by $Z$, with no room for the disorder that mass-based ordering allowed. Two consequences follow:

- Atomic number, not atomic mass, is the fundamental order parameter of the elements.
- Mendeleev's inversions were correct: iodine does not really follow tellurium; both sit where nuclear charge puts them, and the mass coincidence that caused the trouble (127.6 against 126.9) is irrelevant.

With $Z$ as the organiser, the modern periodic law can be stated cleanly:

> The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

## 4. Periods and groups, numbered

IUPAC settled the notation that modern tables use: **18 groups**, numbered 1 to 18 from the alkali metals to the noble gases, and **7 periods**, numbered by row. Period number and group number are not just labels; they can be read off an element's electronic configuration, which is what the next module shows. The old A/B notations (IA, VIIA, IB…) still circulate in books and exams, but every unambiguous reference — group 13, period 4, block p — uses the IUPAC numbers.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Order is not mass">

A stubborn habit: writing "heavier elements always stand to the right". Argon (39.9) stands before potassium (39.1); cobalt before nickel; tellurium before iodine. When a trend argument appeals to mass — "iodine is heavier than bromine, so …" — pause and rebuild it in terms of nuclear charge and electronic configuration, which are what actually generate the trends.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** The halogen neighbours fluorine, chlorine and bromine have atomic masses 19.0, 35.5 and 79.9. Does a Döbereiner triad work across them? What does the failure tell you?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The predicted middle mass is $\frac{19.0+79.9}{2}=49.45$, nowhere near chlorine's 35.5, so the triad fails. Failing triads are not numerical mistakes: they show the rule was a lucky pattern over a small sample and not a law. The scheme's value was historical — it was a first demonstration that masses carry structure, even if mass is not the ordering variable.
</details>

**2.** Mendeleev placed tellurium (mass 127.6) before iodine (126.9). Justify his decision in one sentence.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Tellurium's chemistry (oxides and hydrides like those of sulphur and selenium) places it with group 16, while iodine forms salts like chlorine and bromine and belongs in group 17; preserving the chemical pattern mattered more to Mendeleev than preserving mass order.
</details>

**3.** Why did the $\sqrt{\nu}=a(Z-b)$ plot settle the rare-earth jumble that mass ordering could not?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The rare earths, with masses bunched together and uncertain, had no trustworthy mass sequence, but each has a distinct number of protons, and each gives its own X-ray frequency on the same straight line in $Z$. Measuring the line gave each element a definite position, which is why Moseley's work completed the table's numbering before most of the heavy elements had even been discovered.
</details>

## Remember

- Atomic number (proton count), not atomic mass, orders the periodic table.
- Midpoint-mass triads and eighth-element octaves were early, partial glimpses of periodicity.
- Mendeleev's major contributions were prediction from gaps and ranking chemistry above mass.
- The inverted pairs Ar/K, Co/Ni and Te/I were evidence that mass was the wrong ruler.
- X-ray line frequencies increase linearly as $\sqrt{\nu}$ with $Z$; the modern law makes properties periodic in $Z$.
