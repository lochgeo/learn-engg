## Size is a measurement, not a surface

An atom has no skin. Its electrons cloud outward and fade gradually, so "the size of an atom" means nothing until you say how the size is measured. That subtle point is not pedantry: three different radius definitions exist, trends look different in each, and exam questions routinely exploit the difference. Once the definitions are clear, though, every atomic-radius trend follows from one idea — how strongly the nucleus pulls on its outermost electrons.

## 1. Three ways to cut an atom in half

Every "radius" here is half of some measured distance:

| Radius | Half of what | Typical situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covalent | distance between two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond | $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{C-C}$ in diamond |
| Metallic | distance between neighbours in a metallic crystal | sodium metal |
| van der Waals | closest approach of two non-bonded atoms in a crystal | neighbouring $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ molecules |

For the same element the three values are not equal, and their order is fixed by physics. Two bonded atoms pull close together against the nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron repulsions, but two atoms that are merely touching feel only repulsion and stay further apart. Hence, for a given element,

$$
r_{\text{covalent}} < r_{\text{metallic}} < r_{\text{van der Waals}}.
$$

Chlorine shows the spread: covalent radius about $99\ \text{pm}$ (from the $198\ \text{pm}$ $\mathrm{Cl-Cl}$ bond), metallic not applicable, van der Waals radius about $180\ \text{pm}$. The comparison between chlorine atoms in the same molecule and chlorine atoms of neighbouring molecules is the whole difference.

**Practical rule:** never compare radii of different kinds. Compare covalent with covalent, or metallic with metallic.

## 2. Across a period: the radius shrinks

Along a period the new electrons all enter the *same* shell $n$. The nuclear charge rises by one each step, but the added electron shields the others poorly (electrons in the same shell shield each other only weakly). The effective nuclear charge $Z_{eff}$ — the pull each outer electron actually feels — therefore climbs steadily, and the shell is pulled in tighter.

For period 3 (values are metallic radii for the metals, covalent radii for the non-metals):

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Radius (pm) | 186 | 160 | 143 | 117 | 110 | 104 | 99 |

The fall from sodium to chlorine is nearly a factor of two, achieved without adding a single shell. Halving one of the largest quantities on the chemist's bench using only nuclear charge is the single most consequential trend in the periodic table.

**Two wrinkles.** In the d-block the added electrons enter an inner $(n-1)$ subshell that shields well, so radii shrink only slightly across the row. And in period 6 the fourteen lanthanoids ahead of the 5d metals shield so poorly that the 5d block starts unusually contracted — hafnium ($159\ \text{pm}$) is the same size as zirconium ($160\ \text{pm}$) one period above. That is the **lanthanoid contraction**.

## 3. Down a group: the radius grows

Down a group each element opens a fresh outer shell, and size is dominated by $n$:

| Element | Li | Na | K | Rb | Cs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metallic radius (pm) | 152 | 186 | 231 | 244 | 262 |

The extra shell wins over the rising nuclear charge, because the intervening inner shells shield the outer electron well. Atomic size therefore increases down every group — including the p-block, where iodine is larger than chlorine, and the noble gases.

**A measurement caveat for noble gases.** Noble-gas radii are van der Waals radii, since no covalent radius can be defined for an atom that does not bond. Neon's $160\ \text{pm}$-ish van der Waals value simply cannot be set beside fluorine's covalent $72\ \text{pm}$: different yardsticks. In exams, noble-gas radii are normally quoted and compared on the van der Waals basis.

## 4. Ionic radii and isoelectronic series

Remove an electron and the ion shrinks; add one and it swells:

- **Cations are smaller than their parent atoms.** Losing the outer electrons empties the outermost shell entirely (e.g. $\mathrm{Na}$, $[\mathrm{Ne}]3s^1$, becomes $\mathrm{Na^+}$, $[\mathrm{Ne}]$), and fewer electrons share the same nuclear pull, tightening the cloud. Sodium metal radius $186\ \text{pm}$, sodium ion $102\ \text{pm}$ — about $45\%$ smaller.
- **Anions are larger than their parent atoms.** The added electron inflates the electron–electron repulsion while the nuclear charge stays fixed. Chlorine $99\ \text{pm}$ covalent, chloride ion $181\ \text{pm}$.

The cleanest laboratory for understanding ionic size is an **isoelectronic series**: different ions carrying the *same* number of electrons. With ten electrons, six familiar species line up:

| Species | $\mathrm{N^{3-}}$ | $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ | $\mathrm{F^-}$ | $\mathrm{Na^+}$ | $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ | $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $Z$ | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 12 | 13 |
| Radius (pm, about) | 146 | 140 | 133 | 102 | 72 | 54 |

All six clouds hold ten electrons, so electron–electron repulsion is essentially the same in each; the only thing that varies is the nuclear charge pulling on them. As $Z$ rises from 7 to 13, the same-sized cloud is drawn tighter, monotonically:

$$
r(\mathrm{N^{3-}}) > r(\mathrm{O^{2-}}) > r(\mathrm{F^-}) > r(\mathrm{Na^+}) > r(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}) > r(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}).
$$

The same logic fixes the anion–cation crossing: for the 18-electron pair $\mathrm{K^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, potassium's extra proton (19 against 17) makes its ion smaller, $138\ \text{pm}$ against $181\ \text{pm}$, even though electron counts are identical.

## Worked example: ranking an 18-electron set

Rank these species by size, largest first: $\mathrm{S^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $\mathrm{Ar}$, $\mathrm{K^+}$, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$.

Every species carries 18 electrons, so the ranking is purely by nuclear charge. The atomic numbers are S = 16, Cl = 17, Ar = 18, K = 19, Ca = 20. Higher $Z$ squeezes the same 18-electron cloud harder:

$$
r(\mathrm{S^{2-}}) > r(\mathrm{Cl^-}) > r(\mathrm{Ar}) > r(\mathrm{K^+}) > r(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}).
$$

Sanity checks: all the anions are larger than the neutral atom radius level of the series, and the dication is the smallest — the order is strictly monotonic in $Z$, exactly as the argument demands.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Radius errors to avoid">

- **Comparing a van der Waals radius with a covalent one** and concluding that a noble gas is "huge". Different definitions; do not mix lists.
- **Assuming the cation is always smaller than any nearby anion "because it is a metal".** Size across an isoelectronic series is decided by nuclear charge alone.
- **Forgetting that a cation may lose an entire shell** ($\mathrm{Na^+}$ has no $3s$ electrons left), which is why first-shell loss shrinks an atom so dramatically.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**1.** Which is larger, $\mathrm{Na^+}$ or $\mathrm{F^-}$? Both have 10 electrons — explain your choice.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Sodium has $Z=11$ and fluorine $Z=9$. With identical 10-electron clouds, the greater nuclear charge of sodium pulls its cloud in harder, so $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ($102\ \text{pm}$) is smaller than $\mathrm{F^-}$ ($133\ \text{pm}$).
</details>

**2.** Down group 17, do the van der Waals radii increase? Which radius would you *not* use for this comparison?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Yes: more shells down the group means larger atoms, whatever radius is used. You would not use a covalent radius, because for the noble gases down the group none is defined — only van der Waals radii exist consistently for that comparison.
</details>

**3.** $\mathrm{Zr}$ and $\mathrm{Hf}$ have nearly identical radii despite hafnium being a full period lower. Why?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Between them lie fourteen lanthanoid elements whose $4f$ electrons shield the nuclear charge very poorly. The nuclear charge therefore keeps growing without much relaxation, and by the time the 5d row starts, hafnium has been "squeezed" down to zirconium's size — the lanthanoid contraction.
</details>

## Remember

- Every atomic radius is half a measured distance; the three definitions (covalent, metallic, van der Waals) are not interchangeable.
- Across a period, $Z_{eff}$ rises within the same shell, so atomic radii shrink; the period 3 fall from 186 to 99 pm is nearly a halving.
- Down a group, each new outer shell dominates, so radii grow; the lanthanoid contraction flattens this in period 6.
- Cations are smaller than parent atoms, anions larger.
- Within an isoelectronic series, size decreases strictly as nuclear charge rises — count electrons first, then compare $Z$.

The same nuclear-pull bookkeeping now explains how tightly an atom holds its own electrons — and that quantity, ionisation enthalpy, is the next module's subject.
