## Why atoms had to be taken apart

By 1900 chemistry had convincing evidence that atoms exist and combine in fixed ratios — but no evidence at all about what an atom is made of. Electricity passing through gases, however, kept hinting that something smaller than the atom could be pulled out of it. This module follows the chain of experiments that cracked the atom open: the electron, the proton, the neutron, and the two atomic models the results produced.

## 1. Cathode rays and the discovery of the electron

In a gas-discharge tube at very low pressure, a high potential difference makes the cathode emit rays that travel towards the anode. J. J. Thomson found in 1897 that these **cathode rays**

- travel in straight lines and cast sharp shadows;
- are deflected by electric and magnetic fields in the direction expected for **negative** charges;
- are produced whatever metal forms the cathode and whatever gas fills the tube.

That last observation is the crucial one. The ray is not a property of the metal or the gas — it is a universal ingredient of all matter. Thomson measured the **charge-to-mass ratio** of the particles by balancing electric and magnetic deflections:

$$
\frac{e}{m_e}=1.76\times10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}.
$$

The value is enormous: the same quantity for the lightest positive ion (a proton) is about $9.6\times10^{7}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$, nearly two thousand times smaller.

## 2. The charge, and therefore the mass

Thomson's ratio gave the mass only if the charge was known separately. Robert Millikan's **oil-drop experiment** (1909) measured the fundamental charge: charged oil droplets suspended between plates by an electric field always carry a charge that is an integer multiple of

$$
e=1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}.
$$

Electrons are indivisible packets of charge, and Millikan's integers showed it directly. Combining the two results gives the electron mass.

### Worked example 1: mass of the electron

$$
m_e=\frac{e}{e/m_e}=\frac{1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}}{1.76\times10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}}=9.1\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}.
$$

Sanity check: this is about $\frac{1}{1837}$ of a hydrogen atom's mass, so the electron is a genuinely tiny fragment of matter — which immediately contradicts Dalton's picture of a hard, indivisible atom.

## 3. Protons and neutrons

When Thomson's tube was run in reverse — puncturing holes in the perforated cathode — Goldstein observed rays travelling in the opposite direction. These **canal rays** (anode rays) are the positive ions of the residual gas, formed when energetic particles knock electrons off gas atoms. The lightest positive ray, from hydrogen, corresponds to a particle with

- charge $+1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, exactly equal and opposite to the electron's charge;
- mass $1.67\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, about 1836 electron masses.

This particle is the **proton**, and it carries essentially all the mass of the lightest atom.

| Particle | Relative charge | Mass (kg) | Mass (u) | Discovered by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| electron, e⁻ | −1 | 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ | 0.000549 | J. J. Thomson (1897) |
| proton, p⁺ | +1 | 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ | 1.00727 | Goldstein's canal rays |
| neutron, n | 0 | 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ | 1.00867 | James Chadwick (1932) |

The neutron took longest to find because it carries no charge: electric and magnetic deflections, the standard tools of particle detection, are blind to it. Chadwick noticed that beryllium, bombarded with alpha particles, emitted a radiation that knocked protons out of paraffin wax. Only a heavy neutral particle could transfer that much momentum in a collision, following the same physics as a cue ball striking other balls:

$$
{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{9}_{4}\mathrm{Be} \rightarrow {}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} + {}^{1}_{0}n.
$$

Note that the mass numbers balance: $4+9=12+1$, and the charges balance: $2+4=6+0$.

## 4. Thomson's model

The first model consistent with these discoveries was Thomson's **plum pudding** (1904, sometimes told as a watermelon): a sphere of positive charge about the size of an atom with electrons embedded in it like seeds, so that the positive and negative charges balance. It predicted a fairly uniform distribution of matter and charge, and it made a clear, testable forecast: fast, heavy, positively charged particles shot through an atom should all pass through with at most small deflections.

## 5. Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment

Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, directed by Ernest Rutherford (1909–1911), fired alpha particles (helium nuclei, positively charged) at an extremely thin gold foil and counted the angles at which they emerged on a movable zinc sulphide screen, which flashes where an alpha particle strikes.

| Observation | What it forces us to conclude |
| --- | --- |
| Most alpha particles pass straight through | The atom is mostly **empty space** |
| A few deflect through small angles | Positive charge is concentrated, not spread out |
| About 1 in 20 000 reverses direction | All positive charge and nearly all mass sit in a tiny, dense **nucleus** |

The rebound observations killed the plum pudding. A diffuse pudding of charge could vaguely jostle an alpha particle, but only a very compact, very strong electric field — approached close and head-on — can throw a fast heavy particle straight back. From the small number of large-angle events Rutherford calculated that the nucleus has a radius of order $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$, against an atomic radius of about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$.

Sanity check with scale: if an atom were magnified until it filled a cricket ground (a hundred metres across), its nucleus would be a centimetre-scale pebble at the centre — yet that pebble contains over 99.9% of all the mass in the stadium.

<learning-block type="concept" title="So what is left unexplained?">

Rutherford's model is a picture, not a physical theory. It says nothing about **how** the electrons are arranged, and it quietly contradicts classical physics: an electron circling the nucleus is an accelerating charged body, and classical electrodynamics insists that any accelerating charge radiates energy continuously. Combined with the attraction to the nucleus, the electron should spiral inwards and collapse the atom in a fraction of a second. Stable atoms exist, and they also emit light in **sharp, discrete lines** rather than a continuous smear — and Rutherford's model cannot begin to explain either fact. The next module looks at that light.

</learning-block>

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Common errors about the early models">

- **Attributing the discovery of the proton to Rutherford alone.** The canal-ray evidence came from Goldstein; Rutherford confirmed and named the particle much later.
- **Thinking "most alpha particles pass through" means the nucleus is small relative to the foil.** It means each atom is mostly empty, so most alphas never approach a nucleus closely.
- **Mixing up mass number and charge in nuclear equations.** Always check that the superscripts (mass) and subscripts (charge) both balance.
- **Believing Millikan discovered the electron.** Thomson identified it; Millikan measured its charge precisely.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** An unknown negative particle has a charge-to-mass ratio of $9.6\times10^{7}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$ — about 1836 times smaller than the electron's. What is it, and why did this comparison matter historically?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

It is the **proton** (or more precisely, a hydrogen ion): the charge is equal and opposite, and the mass is 1836 times larger, so $e/m$ drops by the same factor. Thomson's comparison of the two ratios is what proved electrons and positive ions are different particles of very different mass.
</details>

**B.** An oil droplet sits still between plates when the electric force balances its weight. If the droplet holds 3 excess electrons, express its charge in coulombs.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$Q=3e=3\times1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}=4.80\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. The quantisation of charge — the droplet can hold 2, 3 or 4 electrons but never 2.5 — is exactly what Millikan's integers demonstrated.
</details>

**C.** In the scattering experiment, why was the foil made of gold rather than, say, sodium?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Gold can be hammered to an extremely thin foil (a few hundred atoms thick), so an alpha particle meets very few layers and any reversal must come from a single close encounter with one nucleus. Gold's very high atomic number also strengthens the repulsion, making large-angle deflections more likely and easier to count.
</details>

## Remember

- Electrons, protons and neutrons are the three subatomic particles: charges $-e$, $+e$, $0$, with $e=1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.
- $e/m$ for the electron is $1.76\times10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$; combining it with Millikan's $e$ gives $m_e=9.1\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$.
- Rutherford's experiment shows the atom is mostly empty, with all positive charge and mass in a nucleus of radius about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$.
- The neutron, being neutral, hid from electrical detection until Chadwick's collision argument for it in 1932.
- The Rutherford atom is unstable by classical physics and silent on atomic spectra — the two failures that drive the rest of this chapter.
