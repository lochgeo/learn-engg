## Why some reactions stop halfway

Put nitrogen and hydrogen, mixed in a $1:3$ ratio, into a steel vessel with a little iron and hold the temperature constant. The amount of ammonia in the vessel rises for a while, then levels off — but the analysis shows something strange: ammonia is being destroyed and rebuilt all the time, at equal rates. The mixture is not dead. It is balanced.

This state — no net change, yet continuous unseen change — is **equilibrium**. This chapter builds the quantitative machinery of that idea: how far a reaction goes, which way it is drifting at any moment, and how the same machinery answers every question about acids, bases and solubility.

## Reversible reactions

A reaction is **reversible** when its products can also react among themselves to reform the reactants. In a balanced equation we show this with the double arrow $\rightleftharpoons$:

$$
\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}
\qquad
\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}
$$

Most reactions are reversible to some degree. Practically, we call one "irreversible" (and write a single arrow) when the reverse tendency is immeasurably small — burning magnesium ribbon in air, for instance. The double arrow marks reactions where both directions genuinely compete.

Reversibility is not an accident of the equation. From Chapter 5 you saw that a reaction runs while its Gibbs energy falls, and it must stop when $\Delta G$ reaches zero. A reaction with any significant reverse tendency therefore stops at a **mixture**, not at pure products. The question "how much of each?" is exactly what the next module answers.

## The equilibrium state is dynamic

At equilibrium the **macroscopic** picture is static: concentration, colour, pressure, density all hold steady. The **microscopic** picture is anything but static:

- Product molecules keep converting back into reactants.
- Reactant molecules keep converting into products.
- The two **rates are equal**, so the amounts do not change.

This is why equilibrium is called **dynamic**. The evidence is direct: inject a few radioactive HI molecules into an equilibrated $\mathrm{H_2/I_2/HI}$ mix and the radioactivity is found spread across all three species — molecules are exchanging, even though nothing measurable moves.

## Characteristics of the equilibrium state

1. **Constant composition.** Every observable macroscopic property stops changing with time.
2. **Equal opposing rates.** Rate of the forward change $=$ rate of the reverse change; both are non-zero.
3. **A closed system.** Matter must not be able to escape. Boiling water in an open beaker never equilibrates; in a sealed flask (water $\rightleftharpoons$ vapour) it does.
4. **Approachable from both sides.** Starting from either pure reactants or pure products, the same temperature gives the same final state.
5. **Catalysts only speed the arrival.** A catalyst lowers the activation barrier for **both** directions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner but at the same position.
6. **No completion in general.** Except for cases where the product physically escapes (precipitation, gas leaving an open container), equilibrium mixtures contain all participants.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Equilibrium is not a stopping of motion">

- **"At equilibrium the reaction has stopped."** No: the forward and reverse rates are equal, not zero.
- **"Reactions always go to completion."** Only when the reverse is negligible or product escapes; otherwise a mixture remains.
- **"Adding a catalyst pushes the reaction forward."** A catalyst changes time, never position.
- **"Open systems can reach equilibrium."** Not for matter equilibria — the matter must stay inside. Energy exchange alone (heat) is fine.

</learning-block>

## Physical equilibria

Equilibrium is not owned by chemistry — it governs every balance between two opposing changes. These **physical equilibria** are the best models for the idea, because the two directions are easy to picture.

| Equilibrium | Example | What is constant | Changed by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| solid $\rightleftharpoons$ liquid | ice and water at $0^\circ$C, $1\ \text{atm}$ | temperature (the melting point) | pressure, impurities |
| liquid $\rightleftharpoons$ vapour | water and steam in a closed flask | vapour pressure at a given temperature | temperature |
| solid $\rightleftharpoons$ solution | undissolved salt in a saturated solution | solubility (dissolved amount) | temperature |
| gas $\rightleftharpoons$ solution | carbonated water in a sealed bottle | dissolved gas at a given pressure | pressure, temperature |

The liquid–vapour case rewards attention. In a closed flask, evaporation builds up vapour until molecules return to the liquid as fast as they leave it; the pressure then reads a fixed value, the **vapour pressure**, which depends only on temperature. Raise the temperature and the balance shifts toward vapour. This is Le Chatelier's principle in its clearest form — the quantitative version arrives in module 4.

Gas dissolving in liquid behaves the same way: more pressure forces more gas to dissolve, which is exactly how a soda bottle keeps its fizz, and exactly why an opened bottle goes flat.

## Worked example: the same state from both directions

At a fixed temperature, two sealed $10.0\ \text{L}$ flasks are filled:

| Experiment | Contents at the start | Contents when constant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $2.0\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $2.0\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{I_2}$ | $3.0\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{HI}$, $0.5\ \text{mol}$ each of $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{I_2}$ |
| B | $4.0\ \text{mol}$ of pure $\mathrm{HI}$ | the same mixture: $3.0\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{HI}$, $0.5\ \text{mol}$ each of $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{I_2}$ |

Experiment A starts with no product; experiment B starts with no reactant. Both settle into **identical** compositions. The check: the concentration sets are the same, since moles are compared inside equal volumes. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that the final state is an equilibrium — a fixed, reproducible mixture — rather than exhaustion of any reagent.

From B you can also see the reaction running in the direction opposite to A: $1.0\ \text{mol}$ of $\mathrm{HI}$ decomposed. Equilibrium truly has two faces, and the next module gives you a single constant that describes both of them.

## Try it yourself

**1.** A saturated solution of common salt sits with undissolved crystals at the bottom, at constant temperature. Is anything happening in that flask?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Yes. Ions leave the crystal surface and dissolve while ions in solution join the crystal, at equal rates, forever. The amounts are constant; the process is dynamic. Weigh the undissolved crystals at any moment and the mass is unchanged — yet separate experiments show ions endlessly swapping between crystal and solution.
</details>

**2.** Why can water in an open beaker never be said to reach its liquid–vapour equilibrium?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Equilibrium needs vapour to return to the liquid at the same rate it leaves. In an open beaker the vapour escapes into the room, so the return rate can never match the escape rate; the water simply drains away. The equilibrium exists only in a closed container.
</details>

**3.** An equilibrated mixture is left alone but a catalyst is sprinkled in. What changes — and what does not?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

The rates of **both** directions rise equally, so equilibrium is attained faster than before. Nothing changes in the composition: the same forward and reverse rates were already balanced and a catalyst preserves that balance. Catalysts edit kinetics, not thermodynamics.
</details>

## Remember

- Equilibrium is a **balance of rates**, not a standstill: forward rate $=$ reverse rate, both non-zero.
- The equilibrium state is reproducible: approached from either direction, the same temperature gives the same composition.
- A closed system is essential — open containers cannot hold matter equilibria.
- Catalysts change how fast equilibrium arrives, never where it lies.
- The same logic (balance between two directions) covers boiling, dissolving and gas uptake — the machinery coming next is one tool with many faces.

Quantifying "how far" a reversible reaction goes is next: one number — the equilibrium constant — will summarise the whole equilibrium composition at a given temperature.
