## The simplest hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. They divide into:

- **open-chain (aliphatic)** compounds, with straight or branched carbon chains;
- **cyclic** compounds, including alicyclic rings and aromatic rings such as benzene.

Alkanes are saturated open-chain hydrocarbons with the general formula $C_nH_{2n+2}$. Every carbon is $sp^3$ hybridised, every bond is a σ bond, and the molecules are non-polar and comparatively unreactive. That low reactivity is exactly why alkanes are useful as fuels and solvents — and why their reactions need heat, light or a catalyst.

## 1. Structure and conformations

Methane is tetrahedral. Ethane can rotate about its C–C bond, giving **conformations** that differ in energy:

- **staggered** — the C–H bonds on adjacent carbons are as far apart as possible; this is the lower-energy form.
- **eclipsed** — the bonds line up, giving maximum repulsion; this is about 12 kJ mol⁻¹ higher in energy for ethane.

For butane, the **anti** conformation (methyls opposite) is the most stable; the **gauche** form has the methyls 60° apart and is slightly higher in energy. Conformations interconvert rapidly at room temperature, so they are not separable isomers.

## 2. Physical trends

- Boiling point rises with chain length because London dispersion forces increase with surface area.
- Among isomers, **branching lowers the boiling point** because the molecule is more compact and has less surface contact.
- Alkanes are insoluble in water and soluble in non-polar solvents; liquid alkanes are less dense than water.

<learning-block type="concept" title="Why branching lowers the boiling point">

Two isomers have the same formula and the same number of electrons. The straight-chain isomer has a larger contact area, so induced dipoles interact over a greater distance. The branched isomer is more spherical, so the same attractions act over a smaller area.

</learning-block>

## 3. Preparation of alkanes

**Hydrogenation of alkenes or alkynes**

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni,\ \Delta} CH_3CH_3}
$$

**Wurtz reaction** — two alkyl halides couple in dry ether with sodium:

$$
\mathrm{2R{-}X + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry\ ether} R{-}R + 2NaX}
$$

It works best for symmetrical alkanes. With two different halides, a mixture of products forms, so it is not a general synthesis.

**Frankland reaction** — alkyl halides with zinc:

$$
\mathrm{2R{-}X + Zn \rightarrow R{-}R + ZnX_2}
$$

**Kolbe electrolysis** — electrolysis of a potassium carboxylate solution:

$$
\mathrm{2RCOOK + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{electrolysis} R{-}R + 2CO_2 + H_2 + 2KOH}
$$

**Decarboxylation** — a sodium carboxylate heated with soda lime:

$$
\mathrm{RCOONa + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO,\ \Delta} R{-}H + Na_2CO_3}
$$

**Sabatier–Senderens reaction**

$$
\mathrm{CO + 3H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni,\ \Delta} CH_4 + H_2O}
$$

## 4. Reactions of alkanes

### Halogenation

Alkanes react with halogens under ultraviolet light or at high temperature. The mechanism is **free-radical substitution**:

1. **Initiation:** $\mathrm{Cl_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2Cl^{\bullet}}$
2. **Propagation:** $\mathrm{Cl^{\bullet} + CH_4 \rightarrow CH_3^{\bullet} + HCl}$, then $\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet} + Cl_2 \rightarrow CH_3Cl + Cl^{\bullet}}$
3. **Termination:** radicals combine, for example $\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet} + Cl^{\bullet} \rightarrow CH_3Cl}$.

Reactivity order: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Fluorination is violently exothermic; iodination is reversible and needs an oxidising agent to remove HI.

Chlorination is not very selective. Bromination is much more selective for the more substituted carbon because the propagation step is endothermic and the transition state is product-like.

Approximate relative reactivity at 25 °C:

| Halogen | 3° : 2° : 1° |
| --- | --- |
| Cl₂ | about 5 : 4 : 1 |
| Br₂ | about 1600 : 80 : 1 |

### Combustion

$$
\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2} + \frac{3n+1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow nCO_2 + (n+1)H_2O}
$$

Combustion is highly exothermic, which is why alkanes are fuels. In limited oxygen, carbon monoxide and soot form.

### Cracking, isomerisation and pyrolysis

- **Cracking** breaks larger alkanes into smaller alkanes and alkenes, using heat or a catalyst. It supplies the alkenes needed for polymers.
- **Isomerisation** converts a straight chain into a branched isomer, improving octane rating.
- **Pyrolysis** is thermal decomposition without oxygen.

Alkanes also undergo nitration and sulphonation under drastic conditions (vapour phase, concentrated acids, high temperature), but these are not their characteristic reactions.

## 5. Worked examples

### Example 1: monochloro products

How many monochloro derivatives can 2-methylbutane form?

Structure: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃.

Distinct hydrogen environments:

1. the two equivalent methyls on C2 (six H)
2. the CH hydrogen on C2 (one H)
3. the CH₂ hydrogens on C3 (two H)
4. the terminal CH₃ on C4 (three H)

So **four** monochloro products. Two of them are chiral and would each form a racemic pair, but the question asks for structural monochloro derivatives.

### Example 2: chlorination of propane

Propane has six primary hydrogens and two secondary hydrogens. Per hydrogen, the secondary position is more reactive (roughly 4:1 for chlorine), so the product ratio is about

$$
1\text{-chloro}:2\text{-chloro}=6\times1:2\times4=6:8.
$$

Secondary radicals are more stable, so 2-chloropropane is the major product even though primary hydrogens are more numerous.

### Example 3: choose a preparation

To make ethane from bromomethane, Wurtz coupling is suitable:

$$
\mathrm{2CH_3Br + 2Na \rightarrow CH_3CH_3 + 2NaBr}
$$

To make methane, decarboxylation of sodium ethanoate with soda lime is cleaner than Wurtz, which cannot give methane.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Alkane errors">

- **Using Wurtz for two different alkyl halides.** It gives a mixture and is not selective.
- **Assuming chlorination is selective.** It is not; bromination is the selective one.
- **Forgetting that radicals are the intermediates.** Writing an ionic mechanism for alkane halogenation loses marks.
- **Treating conformations as isomers.** They interconvert and cannot be isolated.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Why does 2-methylpropane have a lower boiling point than butane?
2. Write the propagation steps for the chlorination of ethane.
3. How many monobromo derivatives can propane form?
4. Which preparation would you use for methane: Wurtz coupling or decarboxylation? Why?
5. Why is bromination more selective than chlorination?
6. What is the product of the complete combustion of propane?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Branching makes the molecule more compact, reducing surface contact and dispersion forces.
2. $\mathrm{Cl^{\bullet}+C_2H_6 \rightarrow C_2H_5^{\bullet}+HCl}$ and $\mathrm{C_2H_5^{\bullet}+Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_5Cl+Cl^{\bullet}}$.
3. Two: 1-bromopropane and 2-bromopropane.
4. Decarboxylation of sodium ethanoate, because Wurtz couples two carbon fragments and cannot produce methane.
5. The bromination propagation step is less exothermic and has a later, more product-like transition state, so radical stability matters more.
6. Carbon dioxide and water.

</details>

## Take away

Alkanes are the unreactive baseline of organic chemistry. Their preparation matters for synthesis, and their free-radical halogenation is the model for radical chemistry: initiation, propagation, termination, with selectivity controlled by radical stability.
