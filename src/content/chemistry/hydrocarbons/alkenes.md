## A double bond changes everything

Alkenes contain a C=C bond: one σ and one π. The π bond is weaker and more exposed, so alkenes undergo **addition** reactions readily, while alkanes mostly substitute. The two carbons of the double bond are $sp^2$, the six atoms attached to them are coplanar, and rotation is blocked — which creates geometric isomerism.

General formula: $C_nH_{2n}$ for one double bond in an open chain.

## 1. Structure and stability

| Feature | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| planar $sp^2$ carbons | cis/trans isomerism |
| C=C length about 134 pm | shorter than C–C (154 pm) |
| π bond above and below the plane | nucleophilic π electrons attract electrophiles |
| no rotation about C=C | the two sides are fixed |

Alkene stability increases with substitution: each alkyl group on the double bond adds α-hydrogens and stabilises the π system by hyperconjugation.

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2} < \mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2} < \mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CHCH_3} < \mathrm{(CH_3)_2C{=}CH_2}
$$

## 2. Preparation

**Dehydration of alcohols** — concentrated sulphuric acid or phosphoric acid, about 443 K:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[443\ \mathrm{K}]{\text{conc. }H_2SO_4} CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O}
$$

Tertiary alcohols dehydrate most easily, primary least.

**Dehydrohalogenation** — alcoholic KOH removes HX from an alkyl halide:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br + KOH \xrightarrow{alc.} CH_2{=}CH_2 + KBr + H_2O}
$$

When two alkenes are possible, **Saytzeff's rule** favours the more substituted, more stable alkene.

**Dehalogenation of vicinal dihalides** — zinc dust removes both halogens:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2BrCH_2Br + Zn \rightarrow CH_2{=}CH_2 + ZnBr_2}
$$

**Partial hydrogenation of alkynes** — Lindlar catalyst (palladium on calcium carbonate, poisoned with quinoline) gives the cis alkene.

## 3. Electrophilic addition

The π electrons attack an electrophile, forming a carbocation, which is then captured by a nucleophile.

### Hydrogen halides and Markovnikov's rule

With HX, the hydrogen adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and the halogen adds to the more substituted carbon:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CHBrCH_3}
$$

The rule follows from carbocation stability: the reaction proceeds through the more stable secondary carbocation, not the primary one.

**The peroxide effect** reverses the regiochemistry, but only for HBr. In the presence of peroxides, a radical chain gives the anti-Markovnikov product:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}CH_2 + HBr \xrightarrow{peroxide} CH_3CH_2CH_2Br}
$$

HCl and HI do not show this reversal under ordinary conditions.

### Water, sulphuric acid and hypohalous acids

- Dilute H₂SO₄ then water gives an alcohol, Markovnikov: propene → propan-2-ol.
- HOX gives a halohydrin, with OH on the more substituted carbon.
- Hydroboration–oxidation (B₂H₆, then H₂O₂/OH⁻) gives the **anti-Markovnikov** alcohol without a carbocation intermediate.

### Hydrogen and halogens

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Ni} CH_3CH_3}
$$

$$
\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + Br_2 \rightarrow CH_2BrCH_2Br}
$$

Bromine in carbon tetrachloride is decolourised by alkenes — the standard test for unsaturation.

## 4. Oxidation

**Cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄** adds two OH groups across the double bond, giving a cis-diol. The purple colour fades and a brown precipitate of MnO₂ forms (Baeyer's test).

**Hot acidic KMnO₄** cleaves the double bond completely; the products depend on the substituents and can include ketones, carboxylic acids and CO₂.

**Ozonolysis** — ozone followed by zinc and water cleaves the double bond and gives carbonyl compounds with the carbon skeleton intact:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}C(CH_3)_2 \xrightarrow[Zn/H_2O]{O_3} CH_3CHO + (CH_3)_2C{=}O}
$$

Ozonolysis is the standard way to locate a double bond from the products.

**Epoxidation** with a peroxyacid gives an epoxide, a three-membered oxygen ring.

## 5. Polymerisation

Alkenes join into long chains through addition polymerisation:

$$
n\,\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 \rightarrow (-CH_2-CH_2-)_n}
$$

Ethene gives polythene; propene gives polypropene; styrene gives polystyrene; vinyl chloride gives PVC. The double bond opens and becomes a single bond in the backbone, with no small molecule lost.

## 6. Worked examples

### Example 1: Markovnikov versus peroxide

Propene + HBr gives **2-bromopropane** (Markovnikov). With a peroxide present, the radical pathway gives **1-bromopropane**.

### Example 2: hydration

But-1-ene + H₂O/H⁺ gives **butan-2-ol**. The proton adds to C1 to form the more stable secondary carbocation at C2, which water then attacks.

### Example 3: dehydration

Butan-2-ol dehydrates to **but-2-ene** as the major product, with some but-1-ene. Saytzeff's rule selects the more substituted alkene.

### Example 4: ozonolysis

2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃, cleaves to **propanone** and **ethanal**.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Alkene errors">

- **Applying the peroxide effect to HCl or HI.** Only HBr reverses.
- **Forgetting that cold dilute KMnO₄ gives a cis-diol, not cleavage.** Cleavage needs heat and acid.
- **Assuming the most stable carbocation always gives the major product.** It does for Markovnikov addition, but rearrangements and steric effects can intervene.
- **Calling addition polymerisation a condensation.** No small molecule is eliminated.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Why is but-2-ene more stable than but-1-ene?
2. Predict the product of propene with HCl, and with HCl plus peroxide.
3. What is the major product when butan-2-ol is dehydrated?
4. Give the ozonolysis products of but-1-ene.
5. How would you distinguish hexane from hex-1-ene in the laboratory?
6. Why does Br₂ add to ethene but not to ethane under mild conditions?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. It is more substituted and has more α-hydrogens, so hyperconjugation stabilises it more.
2. With HCl: 2-chloropropane. With peroxide: HCl is unaffected, so it is still 2-chloropropane.
3. But-2-ene (Saytzeff).
4. Methanal and propanal.
5. Add bromine in CCl₄: hex-1-ene decolourises it; hexane does not. Baeyer's reagent gives the same distinction.
6. Ethene has an exposed π bond that can donate electrons to Br₂; ethane has only strong σ bonds.

</details>

## Take away

Alkenes are defined by the C=C bond and its π electrons. Additions follow carbocation stability unless a radical pathway is selected; oxidation either adds two OH groups or cleaves the bond. Learn the reagents and conditions as a small, closed set, and most product questions become predictable.
