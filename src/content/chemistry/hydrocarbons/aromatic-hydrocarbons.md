## Benzene: a ring that refuses to behave like an alkene

Benzene has the formula C₆H₆, so its degrees of unsaturation are four. A simple ring with three double bonds would suggest an alkene, but benzene does not decolourise bromine water, does not add HBr readily, and is far more stable than the hypothetical cyclohexatriene. The reason is **delocalisation**: all six π electrons are shared over the whole ring.

## 1. Structure and bonding

- Six $sp^2$ carbons form a planar regular hexagon.
- Each carbon has one unhybridised p orbital perpendicular to the ring.
- The six p orbitals overlap to give a continuous π system above and below the plane.
- All six C–C bonds are equal, about 139 pm — between a single (154 pm) and a double (134 pm) bond.

Benzene is drawn with alternating double bonds for bookkeeping, but those drawings are resonance structures, not distinct molecules. The resonance energy is roughly 150 kJ mol⁻¹, which is why benzene is unusually stable.

## 2. Aromaticity: Hückel's rule

A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system is **aromatic** when it contains $4n+2$ π electrons, where $n=0,1,2,\dots$

| System | π electrons | Aromatic? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| benzene | 6 | yes |
| naphthalene | 10 | yes |
| anthracene | 14 | yes |
| cyclopentadienyl anion | 6 | yes |
| tropylium cation (C₇H₇⁺) | 6 | yes |
| cyclobutadiene | 4 | no — antiaromatic |
| cyclooctatetraene | 8 | no — non-planar, tub-shaped |

The ring must also be planar and fully conjugated. Cyclooctatetraene has eight π electrons, but it adopts a tub shape to avoid antiaromaticity, so it behaves like an ordinary polyene.

## 3. Electrophilic substitution

Aromatic rings are electron-rich, so they attract electrophiles. Substitution is favoured over addition because addition would destroy the aromatic stabilisation; substitution restores it.

The general mechanism has two steps:

1. The electrophile adds to a ring carbon, giving a delocalised **arenium ion** (σ complex) in which the positive charge is spread over the ring.
2. A proton is lost to a base, restoring the aromatic π system.

### Nitration

Conc. HNO₃ with conc. H₂SO₄ at 323–333 K:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + HNO_3 \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4} C_6H_5NO_2 + H_2O}
$$

The active electrophile is NO₂⁺.

### Sulphonation

Conc. or fuming H₂SO₄ with heat gives benzenesulphonic acid. The reaction is reversible: heating with dilute acid removes the –SO₃H group.

### Halogenation

Chlorine or bromine needs a Lewis acid catalyst such as FeCl₃, FeBr₃ or AlCl₃:

$$
\mathrm{C_6H_6 + Br_2 \xrightarrow{FeBr_3} C_6H_5Br + HBr}
$$

### Friedel–Crafts reactions

**Alkylation** attaches an alkyl group using an alkyl halide and AlCl₃. **Acylation** attaches an acyl group using an acid chloride and AlCl₃, giving a ketone.

Two limitations matter:

- strongly deactivated rings (for example nitrobenzene) do not react;
- alkylation can over-alkylate and can rearrange the carbocation, so acylation is often preferred for clean synthesis.

### Addition under forcing conditions

With hydrogen and a nickel catalyst at high temperature and pressure, benzene is reduced to cyclohexane. With chlorine under ultraviolet light, it adds three molecules of Cl₂ to give benzene hexachloride.

## 4. Directing effects

A substituent already on the ring controls where the next group goes.

| Group | Effect on ring | Directs to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| –OH, –NH₂, –OR, –NHCOR | activating | ortho and para |
| –CH₃, –C₂H₅, –C₆H₅ | weakly activating | ortho and para |
| –Cl, –Br, –I | deactivating | ortho and para |
| –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –COOR, –SO₃H | deactivating | meta |

The pattern follows from the stability of the arenium ion:

- Electron-donating groups (+M or +I, and hyperconjugation for alkyl) stabilise the positive charge at the ortho and para positions, so those products form faster.
- Groups that withdraw by resonance destabilise the ortho and para positions most, leaving the meta pathway relatively favoured.
- Halogens withdraw inductively (deactivating) but donate by resonance (+M), which still directs ortho/para.

<learning-block type="concept" title="Two questions, two answers">

“Is the ring more or less reactive than benzene?” is answered by activation or deactivation. “Where does the next group go?” is answered by the directing effect. Halogens are the standard example where the two answers differ.

</learning-block>

## 5. Side-chain reactions

The benzene ring is stable, but an attached alkyl chain is not.

**Oxidation:** hot alkaline KMnO₄ oxidises an alkyl side chain to –COOH, provided the benzylic carbon has at least one hydrogen. Ethylbenzene gives benzoic acid; tert-butylbenzene does not react under these conditions.

**Halogenation:** chlorine under ultraviolet light substitutes a benzylic hydrogen, giving benzyl chloride. The ring is untouched because the radical is stabilised by the adjacent π system.

## 6. Carcinogenicity and toxicity

Benzene is toxic and causes bone-marrow damage and leukaemia with prolonged exposure. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzopyrene, formed in tobacco smoke and in charred food, are carcinogenic after metabolic activation. Aromatic amines are also associated with cancer. These are reasons aromatic chemistry is handled with care in the laboratory, not just exam facts.

## 7. Worked examples

### Example 1: nitration of toluene

Toluene has a methyl group, which is activating and ortho/para directing. Nitration gives mainly **2-nitrotoluene and 4-nitrotoluene**, with little 3-nitrotoluene.

### Example 2: Friedel–Crafts acylation

Benzene with CH₃COCl and AlCl₃ gives **acetophenone**, C₆H₅COCH₃. Only one acyl group is introduced because the product ketone deactivates the ring.

### Example 3: oxidation of a side chain

Ethylbenzene with hot KMnO₄ gives **benzoic acid**. The benzylic carbon is oxidised; the ring survives.

### Example 4: which ring reacts faster?

Phenol reacts much faster than benzene towards electrophiles because the –OH group activates the ring; nitrobenzene reacts much more slowly because –NO₂ deactivates it.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Arene errors">

- **Drawing benzene as a fixed alternating triene.** It is a resonance hybrid; all C–C bonds are equal.
- **Predicting addition instead of substitution.** Aromatic substitution preserves aromaticity.
- **Forgetting the Lewis acid in halogenation.** Br₂ alone does not brominate benzene.
- **Calling halogens activating.** They are ortho/para directing but deactivating overall.
- **Using Friedel–Crafts on nitrobenzene.** Strongly deactivated rings do not react.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. State Hückel's rule and test cyclopentadienyl anion.
2. Why is benzene unusually stable?
3. Predict the major products of the nitration of phenol.
4. Why is –NO₂ a meta director?
5. What are the products of ethylbenzene with hot KMnO₄?
6. Why does Friedel–Crafts acylation stop after one substitution?
7. Which reacts faster with Br₂/FeBr₃: benzene or anisole (methoxybenzene)?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Cyclic, planar, fully conjugated, $4n+2$ π electrons. The cyclopentadienyl anion has 6 π electrons, so it is aromatic.
2. The six π electrons are delocalised over the ring, giving a resonance energy of about 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol (ortho and para).
4. It withdraws electron density by resonance, destabilising the arenium ion most at ortho and para positions; attack at meta avoids that destabilisation.
5. Benzoic acid.
6. The acyl group is deactivating, so the product ring is less reactive than benzene.
7. Anisole, because the methoxy group activates the ring.

</details>

## Take away

Aromaticity requires a planar, cyclic, conjugated ring with $4n+2$ π electrons. Aromatic reactions are electrophilic substitutions that restore the aromatic system, and substituents control both the rate and the position of the next attack.
