## A triple bond: linear and slightly acidic

Alkynes contain a C≡C bond: one σ and two π bonds. Each carbon is $sp$ hybridised, so the four atoms around the triple bond lie on a straight line with a bond angle of 180°. The general formula for one triple bond in an open chain is $C_nH_{2n-2}$.

The high s-character of the $sp$ orbital holds the bonding electrons closer to carbon, which makes a hydrogen attached to a terminal alkyne weakly acidic — a property alkenes and alkanes do not share.

## 1. Structure and acidity

- C≡C length: about 120 pm, shorter than C=C.
- Geometry: linear at both alkyne carbons.
- Terminal alkyne: $-\mathrm{C \equiv CH}$; internal alkyne: $-\mathrm{C \equiv C}-$.

A terminal alkyne reacts with strong bases to form an acetylide:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH + NaNH_2 \rightarrow R{-}C{\equiv}C^{-}Na^{+} + NH_3}
$$

It also gives precipitates with silver or copper(I) salts, which is the standard test:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}C{\equiv}CH + AgNO_3 + NH_4OH \rightarrow R{-}C{\equiv}CAg\downarrow}
$$

Silver acetylide is white; copper(I) acetylide is red. Internal alkynes do not react, so the test distinguishes terminal from internal triple bonds.

## 2. Preparation

**From calcium carbide**

$$
\mathrm{CaC_2 + 2H_2O \rightarrow HC{\equiv}CH + Ca(OH)_2}
$$

**Dehydrohalogenation of a vicinal dihalide** — two equivalents of alcoholic KOH remove two molecules of HX:

$$
\mathrm{CH_2BrCH_2Br + 2KOH \xrightarrow{alc.} HC{\equiv}CH + 2KBr + 2H_2O}
$$

**Dehalogenation of a tetrahalide** with zinc:

$$
\mathrm{CHBr_2CHBr_2 + 2Zn \rightarrow HC{\equiv}CH + 2ZnBr_2}
$$

**From alkenes** by further dehydrohalogenation, or by isomerisation of a terminal alkyne to an internal alkyne with strong base.

## 3. Addition reactions

### Hydrogen

Complete hydrogenation gives an alkane. Partial hydrogenation can be controlled to give a specific alkene:

- **Lindlar catalyst** (Pd/CaCO₃, poisoned) gives the **cis** alkene.
- **Sodium in liquid ammonia** gives the **trans** alkene.

This is one of the cleanest ways to control alkene geometry.

### Hydrogen halides

One equivalent adds Markovnikov to give a vinyl halide:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CH + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CBr{=}CH_2}
$$

A second equivalent gives the geminal dihalide:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3CBr{=}CH_2 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3CBr_2CH_3}
$$

### Water

Hydration needs mercury(II) sulphate in dilute sulphuric acid. The initial product is an **enol**, which tautomerises to a ketone:

$$
\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CH + H_2O \xrightarrow{HgSO_4/H_2SO_4} CH_3COCH_3}
$$

Ethyne is the exception: its enol is ethenol, which tautomerises to **ethanal**.

### Halogens and hydrogen cyanide

$$
\mathrm{HC{\equiv}CH + 2Br_2 \rightarrow CHBr_2CHBr_2}
$$

$$
\mathrm{HC{\equiv}CH + HCN \rightarrow CH_2{=}CHCN}
$$

The second product is acrylonitrile, the monomer for acrylic fibres.

## 4. Oxidation and polymerisation

Ozonolysis cleaves the triple bond. A terminal alkyne gives a carboxylic acid plus carbon dioxide; an internal alkyne gives two carboxylic acids.

$$
\mathrm{CH_3C{\equiv}CH \xrightarrow[Zn/H_2O]{O_3} CH_3COOH + CO_2}
$$

Ethyne polymerises in different ways depending on conditions:

- at about 873 K through a red-hot tube, three molecules cyclise to **benzene**;
- with a Ziegler–Natta catalyst, it forms linear **polyacetylene**, a conducting polymer;
- with copper(I) chloride, it dimerises to vinylacetylene.

## 5. Worked examples

### Example 1: hydration of propyne

Propyne gives propan-2-one (acetone). The enol intermediate is CH₃C(OH)=CH₂, which tautomerises to the ketone.

### Example 2: controlled hydrogenation

But-2-yne with Lindlar catalyst gives cis-but-2-ene; with sodium in liquid ammonia it gives trans-but-2-ene.

### Example 3: terminal alkyne test

Ethyne gives a white precipitate with ammoniacal silver nitrate. But-2-yne does not, because it has no terminal hydrogen.

### Example 4: ozonolysis

Propyne gives ethanoic acid and carbon dioxide. The terminal carbon becomes CO₂.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Alkyne errors">

- **Writing the alkyne carbon as bent.** It is linear; 180°.
- **Forgetting the enol intermediate.** Hydration gives a carbonyl only after tautomerisation.
- **Expecting HCl to show the peroxide effect.** It does not; only HBr reverses.
- **Using the silver nitrate test on an internal alkyne.** No terminal hydrogen means no precipitate.
- **Hydrating ethyne and writing a ketone.** Ethyne gives ethanal, not a ketone.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Why is a terminal alkyne acidic while an alkene is not?
2. How would you distinguish but-1-yne from but-2-yne?
3. Predict the product of propyne with one equivalent of HBr.
4. What are the products of the hydration of ethyne?
5. How can cis- and trans-but-2-ene be prepared from but-2-yne?
6. Give the ozonolysis products of but-2-yne.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. The $sp$ carbon has more s-character, so the C–H bond is more polarised and the conjugate base (acetylide) is relatively stable.
2. Add ammoniacal silver nitrate: but-1-yne gives a white precipitate; but-2-yne does not.
3. 2-bromopropene, CH₃CBr=CH₂.
4. Ethenol first, which tautomerises to ethanal.
5. Lindlar catalyst gives cis-but-2-ene; sodium in liquid ammonia gives trans-but-2-ene.
6. Two molecules of ethanoic acid.

</details>

## Take away

Alkynes combine a linear geometry with a reactive triple bond and a weakly acidic terminal hydrogen. Controlled hydrogenation sets alkene geometry, hydration gives carbonyls through an enol, and ozonolysis locates the triple bond.
