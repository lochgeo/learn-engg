## How to use this paper

This is a **50-minute, no-notes** paper in the style of a JEE Main chapter test. Sit it in one sitting, calculator aside, and mark an answer even when unsure — you learn more from a reasoned guess than from a blank. Suggested pacing: Section A about 15 minutes, Section B about 20, Section C about 10, Section D about 5. If a question is still open after 90 seconds, flag it and move on.

Every question carries its full working inside the collapsed answer. Score yourself afterward, then read the closing mapping to aim your revision.

## Conditions and formula sheet

| Tool | Statement | Conditions to confirm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electron rule | oxidation = loss of e; reduction = gain of e; releases equal captures | Agents named by job, not by what they suffer |
| Sum rule | sum of oxidation numbers = formula charge | Peroxides −1, superoxides −½, hydrides −1 for H |
| Disproportionation | one element, one starting state, climbs and falls | Needs an intermediate oxidation state |
| Oxidation-number balancing | total increase = total decrease | Balance O and H afterwards; check charge |
| $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ (acid) | $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O}$ | $n = 5$; ratio with $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ is $1:5$; with oxalate $2:5$ |
| $\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$ (mild base) | $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + e^- \rightarrow MnO_4^{2-}}$ | $n = 1$ |
| $\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}$ (acid) | $\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-} + 14H^+ + 6e^- \rightarrow 2Cr^{3+} + 7H_2O}$ | $n = 6$; ratio with $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ is $1:6$ |
| Dichromate indicator | diphenylamine; $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ too pale unaided | Endpoint flagged externally |
| Permanganate indicator | self-indicator; one excess drop = permanent pink | Standardise before use |

## Section A — 10 rapid MCQs

### 1. Hypochlorite arithmetic

The oxidation number of chlorine in $\mathrm{Ca(ClO)_2}$ is:

- A. $+1$
- B. $+2$
- C. $+3$
- D. $-1$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Calcium is $+2$; each hypochlorite group carries $-1$, and with oxygen at $-2$, chlorine must be $+1$: $x + (-2) = -1$ gives $x = +1$.

</details>

### 2. The peroxide exception doing real work

The oxidation number of chromium in the peroxide compound $\mathrm{CrO_5}$ is:

- A. $+4$
- B. $+5$
- C. $+6$
- D. $+7$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** $\mathrm{CrO_5}$ contains two peroxide linkages: four oxygens at $-1$ and the remaining oxygen at $-2$, total $-6$, so chromium is $+6$. Missing the peroxide bonds and treating every oxygen as $-2$ would make the formula sum to $-10$ and assign chromium $+10$, an impossible-looking result; spot the O–O bonds first.

</details>

### 3. Spot the intruder

Which reaction is **not** a redox reaction?

- A. $\mathrm{Zn + CuSO_4 \rightarrow ZnSO_4 + Cu}$
- B. $\mathrm{2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2}$
- C. $\mathrm{NaCl + AgNO_3 \rightarrow AgCl + NaNO_3}$
- D. $\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2KOH \rightarrow KCl + KClO + H_2O}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Oxidation numbers of Na, Cl, Ag, N and O are identical on both sides — this is a precipitation exchange. In A, C and D, electron transfer happens: zinc rises, oxygen splits $-1$ into $-2$ and $0$, and chlorine splits $0$ into $-1$ and $+1$.

</details>

### 4. Peroxide as reductant

In acidic medium the reaction is $\mathrm{2MnO_4^- + 5H_2O_2 + 6H^+ \rightarrow 2Mn^{2+} + 5O_2 + 8H_2O}$. The $n$-factor of hydrogen peroxide in this reaction is:

- A. $1$
- B. $2$
- C. $5$
- D. $3$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Each peroxide molecule releases two electrons as its oxygen goes $-1 \rightarrow 0$ (the pair of oxygen atoms in one molecule move together to $\mathrm{O_2}$), so the five-electron permanganate pairs with peroxide in the ratio $2:5$.

</details>

### 5. Who can disproportionate?

Which species is capable of disproportionation?

- A. $\mathrm{Cl^-}$
- B. $\mathrm{Cl_2}$
- C. $\mathrm{Na^+}$
- D. $\mathrm{F^-}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Disproportionation needs an intermediate oxidation state with a lower and a higher neighbour. Chlorine at 0 has $-1$ and $+1$ (or the deeper $+5$) available; chloride and fluoride already sit at their lowest states and sodium at its common $+1$.

</details>

### 6. Redox on heating

Which of the following is a redox reaction?

- A. $\mathrm{NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O}$
- B. $\mathrm{2KClO_3 \rightarrow 2KCl + 3O_2}$
- C. $\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$
- D. $\mathrm{AgNO_3 + NaCl \rightarrow AgCl + NaNO_3}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Chlorine falls $+5 \rightarrow -1$ while oxygen rises $-2 \rightarrow 0$: intramolecular redox. The others change no oxidation numbers (neutralisation, calcination, precipitation).

</details>

### 7. A hydride compound

The oxidation number of nitrogen changes in $\mathrm{N_2H_4 \rightarrow NH_3}$ as follows:

- A. $-2 \rightarrow -3$
- B. $-3 \rightarrow -2$
- C. $0 \rightarrow -3$
- D. $-2 \rightarrow 0$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** In hydrazine, hydrogen is $+1$ (nitrogen is more electronegative), so nitrogen averages $-2$; in ammonia it is $-3$. Nitrogen gains one electron per atom: a reduction.

</details>

### 8. Equivalent mass of dichromate

With molar mass $294\ \text{g/mol}$, the equivalent mass of $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$ in acidic medium is:

- A. $49$
- B. $98$
- C. $294$
- D. $147$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** One dichromate ion accepts 6 electrons, so equivalent mass $= 294/6 = 49\ \text{g}$ per equivalent.

</details>

### 9. Electrons per ion

The number of electrons accepted by one dichromate ion in acidic reduction is:

- A. $2$
- B. $3$
- C. $6$
- D. $12$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Each chromium drops $+6 \rightarrow +3$, a gain of 3 per atom, and there are two chromium atoms per ion: $6$ electrons in total.

</details>

### 10. The oxalate ratio

In acidic solution the mole ratio $\mathrm{MnO_4^- : C_2O_4^{2-}}$ at the equivalence point is:

- A. $1 : 5$
- B. $2 : 5$
- C. $5 : 2$
- D. $1 : 2$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Permanganate captures 5 electrons per ion; oxalate releases 2 electrons per ion, one from each carbon climbing $+3 \rightarrow +4$. LCM of 5 and 2 is 10: two permanganates against five oxalates.

</details>

## Section B — 5 numerical-value questions

### 11. Tetrathionate average

The average oxidation number of sulphur in $\mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}$ is:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

$+2.5$. Oxygen contributes $-12$; the ion charge is $-2$, so the four sulphurs total $+10$, an average of $+2.5$. (The actual atoms sit at $0$ and $+5$, two of each.)

</details>

### 12. Manganate middle state

The oxidation number of manganese in $\mathrm{K_2MnO_4}$ is:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

$+6$. Two potassiums give $+2$, four oxygens give $-8$, so manganese must supply $+6$. This is the stable product of permanganate reduction in strongly alkaline solution.

</details>

### 13. Permanganate volume

What volume of $0.1\ \text{M}$ $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ is required to oxidise $100\ \text{mL}$ of $0.2\ \text{M}$ $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ in acidic medium, in mL?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Iron(II) moles: $0.2 \times 0.100 = 0.020\ \text{mol}$. The $1:5$ ratio demands $0.004\ \text{mol}$ permanganate:

$$
V = \frac{0.004\ \text{mol}}{0.1\ \text{mol/L}} = 0.040\ \text{L} = \mathbf{40}\ \text{mL}
$$

</details>

### 14. The spectator fraction

In $\mathrm{MnO_2 + 4HCl \rightarrow MnCl_2 + Cl_2 + 2H_2O}$, the percentage of the hydrochloric acid acting as reducing agent is:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

$50$. Four HCl are consumed, but only two chlorides climb $-1 \rightarrow 0$; the other two end as unchanged chloride in $\mathrm{MnCl_2}$. Fraction $= 2/4 = 0.50$.

</details>

### 15. Neutral-medium equivalent mass

To the nearest integer, the equivalent mass of $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ ($M = 158\ \text{g/mol}$) when it is reduced to $\mathrm{MnO_2}$ in neutral or faintly alkaline medium is:

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

$53$ (exactly $158/3 = 52.67$). In this medium each permanganate accepts 3 electrons, $+7 \rightarrow +4$:

$$
\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 2H_2O + 3e^- \rightarrow MnO_2 + 4OH^-}
$$

</details>

## Section C — 3 assertion–reason questions

Each question offers an assertion (A) and a reason (R). Choose:

- (a) both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
- (b) both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A
- (c) A is true, but R is false
- (d) A is false, but R is true

### 16. The peroxide dual role

**A:** Hydrogen peroxide can act both as an oxidising agent and as a reducing agent.
**R:** The oxygen in $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ sits in the intermediate oxidation state $-1$, which lies between the lower $-2$ and the upper $0$.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a).** An intermediate oxidation state is precisely the requirement for a species to move in either direction: oxygen can fall to $-2$ by accepting electrons (oxidant behaviour, e.g. against $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$) or rise to $0$ by releasing them (reductant behaviour, e.g. against permanganate, as in Section B above).

</details>

### 17. Naming the agent

**A:** In acidic permanganate oxidising iron(II), the $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ ion acts as the oxidising agent.
**R:** $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ loses electrons on the way to $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d).** The assertion fails: a species losing electrons is **oxidised** and therefore behaves as the **reducing** agent. The reason is true — the rise $+2 \rightarrow +3$ is genuine — which is exactly what contradicts the assertion.

</details>

### 18. Equivalent mass of permanganate

**A:** The equivalent mass of $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ in strongly acidic medium is $158/5\ \text{g}$ per equivalent.
**R:** In acid each permanganate ion accepts five electrons, falling to $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a).** Equivalent mass equals molar mass divided by the electrons exchanged per formula unit; in acid the half-reaction $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O}$ fixes $n = 5$, so $158/5 = 31.6\ \text{g}$ per equivalent.

</details>

## Section D — 2 matching and multi-statement questions

### 19. Matching oxidation numbers

Match each species in Column I with the average oxidation number of the marked element in Column II. Some values in Column II may go unused.

| Column I | Column II |
| --- | --- |
| (P) Mn in $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$ | (1) $+7$ |
| (Q) S in $\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}$ | (2) $+6$ |
| (R) Fe in $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$ | (3) $+2$ |
| (S) Cr in $\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}$ | (4) $+8/3$ |
| | (5) $+5$ |

Which set is correct?

- A. P–1, Q–3, R–4, S–2
- B. P–1, Q–4, R–3, S–2
- C. P–1, Q–5, R–4, S–2
- D. P–2, Q–3, R–4, S–5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Potassium is $+1$ and oxygen $-2$, so Mn is $+7$ (P–1). In thiosulphate, two sodiums contribute $+2$ and three oxygens $-6$, leaving $+4$ across the sulphur pair — an average of $+2$ (Q–3). Magnetite shares $+8$ across three iron atoms, an average of $+8/3$ (R–4). In chromate, three oxygens give $-6$ and the charge is $-2$, so Cr is $+6$ (S–2). The unused $+5$ tempts anyone who miscounts the oxygens in thiosulphate.

</details>

### 20. Chlorine in alkali, cold and hot

Study these statements about chlorine in alkaline solution.

- **I.** With cold, dilute $\mathrm{NaOH}$ the products include chloride $(-1)$ and hypochlorite $(+1)$; the change is disproportionation.
- **II.** With hot, concentrated $\mathrm{NaOH}$ the chlorine-containing anion is chlorate, with chlorine at $+5$.
- **III.** The disproportionation of chlorine into chlorate is favoured at low temperature.
- **IV.** In every one of these alkali reactions, some chlorine is oxidised and some is reduced: no chlorine-containing product keeps chlorine at 0.

Which of the following is correct?

- A. I, II and III only
- B. I, II and IV only
- C. II, III and IV only
- D. I, III and IV only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** I is true: $\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2OH^- \rightarrow Cl^- + ClO^- + H_2O}$. II is true: the hot, concentrated medium gives $\mathrm{3Cl_2 + 6OH^- \rightarrow 5Cl^- + ClO_3^- + 3H_2O}$ with chlorine at $+5$. III is false — the $+5$ product (chlorate) is the *hot* condition's outcome; cold alkali stops at $+1$. IV is true: in both stoichiometries every entering $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ molecule is split between climbing and falling partners (five chloride ions versus one chlorate in the hot case).

</details>
