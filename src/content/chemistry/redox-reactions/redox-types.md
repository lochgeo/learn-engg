## Same physics, different shapes

Electron transfer shows up in pictures that look nothing alike: two elements joining to make an oxide, a metal strip dissolving while another metal settles out of solution, one molecule falling apart into two different products. JEE questions group these as **types of redox reactions** because each type carries its own tip-off. Learn the five patterns below and the classification usually takes one glance at the reactants.

## 1. Combination

Two (or more) reactants join to make a single product. It is a redox combination when at least one partner changes oxidation number:

$$
\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO} \qquad \mathrm{C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2}
$$

Magnesium climbs 0 to $+2$; carbon does too. Not every combination is redox: $\mathrm{CaO + CO_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3}$ changes nobody's electron count.

## 2. Decomposition

The reverse: a compound splits, and it is redox when the fragments change oxidation number. Two sub-cases matter:

**Intramolecular redox** — one element rises, a *different* element falls inside the same compound. Heating potassium chlorate:

$$
\mathrm{2KClO_3 \rightarrow 2KCl + 3O_2}
$$

Chlorine drops from $+5$ to $-1$; oxygen rises from $-2$ to $0$. Two elements in one compound trade electrons.

**Non-redox decomposition** — nothing changes, as in $\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$, where Ca stays $+2$, C stays $+4$ and O stays $-2$.

## 3. Displacement

One element pushes another out of its compound, and the pusher is always the more electron-hungry (or electron-giving) of the two.

**Metal displacement** follows the activity series: a more elective metal reduces the ions of a less elective one.

$$
\mathrm{Cu + 2AgNO_3 \rightarrow Cu(NO_3)_2 + 2Ag}
$$

**Halogen displacement** descends group 17; each halogen displaces a lower one from its salt:

$$
\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2KBr \rightarrow 2KCl + Br_2} \qquad \mathrm{Br_2 + 2KI \rightarrow 2KBr + I_2}
$$

Displacement by hydrogen follows the same logic: metals above hydrogen in the activity series dissolve in non-oxidising acids ($\mathrm{Zn + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow ZnSO_4 + H_2}$), while copper and silver do not.

## 4. Disproportionation

One single species renders both couples needed: the *same element* in one starting oxidation state appears in a lower state (reduction) and a higher state (oxidation) simultaneously. Chlorine in hot alkaline solution:

$$
\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2OH^- \rightarrow Cl^- + ClO^- + H_2O}
$$

Chlorine enters at 0 and leaves partly as $-1$ and partly as $+1$. Hydrogen peroxide decomposing:

$$
\mathrm{2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2}
$$

oxygen enters at $-1$ and leaves as $-2$ (in water) and 0 (as $\mathrm{O_2}$).

The logic of the same numbers from the oxidation-number module: disproportionation is only possible for an element sitting in an **intermediate** oxidation state, one that has both a lower and a higher state to fall to and rise to. Chlorine at 0 lies between $-1$ and $+1$; oxygen at $-1$ lies between $-2$ and $0$.

## 5. Comproportionation

The mirror image: two different starting states of the same element converge on one common state. Ammonium salts with nitrite salts:

$$
\mathrm{NH_4^+ + NO_2^- \rightarrow N_2 + 2H_2O}
$$

nitrogen at $-3$ climbs to 0 while nitrogen at $+3$ descends to 0, meeting in molecular nitrogen. In the laboratory this pair is kept apart by adding a base; storing them together invites a violent self-neutralisation of oxidation numbers.

<learning-block type="example" title="Worked example: heating potassium permanganate">

$$
\mathrm{2KMnO_4 \rightarrow K_2MnO_4 + MnO_2 + O_2}
$$

Assign numbers: $\mathrm{K}$ stays $+1$ and $\mathrm{O}$ rises from $-2$ to 0 (gas). Manganese enters at $+7$ and leaves as $+6$ (in $\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}$, manganate) and as $+4$ (in $\mathrm{MnO_2}$). One reactant, one starting compound, two destinations — this is disproportionation of manganese inside the same species, with the oxygen heading for 0 as a by-product. Sanity check: one Mn atom gains one electron ($+7 \rightarrow +6$) and the other gains three ($+7 \rightarrow +4$), while exactly two oxygen atoms each release two electrons ($-2 \rightarrow 0$) to form one $\mathrm{O_2}$ molecule. Four electrons captured match four released.

</learning-block>

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Mixing up the self-reactions">

- **Disproportionation is not the same as intramolecular redox.** In $\mathrm{KClO_3 \rightarrow KCl + O_2}$ oxygen and chlorine are *different elements* trading electrons, so it is intramolecular redox, not disproportionation, even though the reactant is a single compound.
- **Disproportionation needs a middle state.** Ask whether the starting oxidation number has a lower and a higher state available. $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ at 0 qualifies; $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ at $-1$ (the lowest chlorine state) cannot disproportionate further.
- **Comproportionation is rare in questions but beloved in answer keys.** When two reactants of the same element at different oxidation numbers merge into one product, name it rather than force it into "combination".

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Classify $\mathrm{2H_2S + SO_2 \rightarrow 3S + 2H_2O}$, giving every oxidation number that changes.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Sulphur enters at $-2$ in $\mathrm{H_2S}$ twice and at $+4$ in $\mathrm{SO_2}$; both leave as 0 in elemental sulphur. The $-2$ end climbs to 0 (oxidation) and the $+4$ end falls to 0 (reduction): two different starting states of the same element converge — comproportionation.

</details>

**B.** Which type is $\mathrm{Ag_2O \rightarrow 2Ag + \frac12 O_2}$ on heating?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Oxidation numbers: $\mathrm{Ag}$ falls from $+1$ to 0, $\mathrm{O}$ rises from $-2$ to 0. Two *different* elements in one decomposing compound trade electrons: intramolecular redox decomposition, not disproportionation.

</details>

**C.** In alkaline solution, $\mathrm{3Br_2 + 6OH^- \rightarrow 5Br^- + BrO_3^- + 3H_2O}$. Confirm the disproportionation by counting electrons gained and lost in each direction.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Bromine leaves as $\mathrm{Br^-}$ at $-1$ — a fall of 1 per atom, so 5 atoms capture 5 electrons. Bromine also leaves as $\mathrm{BrO_3^-}$ with $\mathrm{Br}$ at $+5$ — a rise of 5 per atom, so 1 atom releases 5 electrons. Five electrons captured $=$ five released, closing the ledger and confirming that one molecule's worth of bromine atoms pays for the other five moving down.

</details>

## Remember

- Combination, decomposition, displacement, disproportionation, comproportionation: five families, one shared physics — balanced electron transfer.
- Displacement runs down the activity series (metals) and down group 17 (halogens).
- Disproportionation needs an intermediate oxidation state and produces two destinations for the same element.
- Comproportionation is its mirror: two states of one element converge.
- Intramolecular redox (one compound, two elements) must not be confused with disproportionation (one element, two directions).
