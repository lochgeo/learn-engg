## A spark, a rust stain and a blue solution

Drop a clean zinc strip into copper sulphate solution and the strip slowly turns copper-coloured while the blue colour of the solution fades. Zinc atoms have thrown electrons into the solution, copper ions have caught them. Nothing was destroyed or created — electrons simply moved house. That movement is the whole business of this chapter: **oxidation is the loss of electrons, reduction is the gain of electrons**, and a reaction where both happen together is a redox reaction.

The names are older than the idea. When Lavoisier studied burning in the eighteenth century, every reaction he examined added oxygen, so the process was called oxidation. Later chemists hydrogenated oils and called the reverse change reduction. The electron picture came last and made both terms precise.

## 1. Electrons, not elements, change hands

Write the zinc–copper change as two lone steps, called **half-reactions**:

$$
\mathrm{Zn \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + 2e^-} \qquad \text{(oxidation: electrons released)}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu} \qquad \text{(reduction: electrons taken up)}
$$

Electrons never appear in the final mixture; they are transfers only. An oxidation half-reaction always has electrons on the right (products), a reduction half-reaction always has them on the left (reactants). Free electrons do not accumulate in a beaker, so every oxidation must pair with a reduction and the total electrons released must equal the total captured:

$$
\mathrm{Zn + Cu^{2+} \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + Cu}
$$

The same bookkeeping works for reactions between neutral molecules, not just ions. Burning magnesium, $\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$, is electron transfer even though no ions are written: each magnesium atom gives two electrons to an oxygen atom.

## 2. Oxidising and reducing agents act in opposite ways

The vocabulary confuses every beginner once, so say it carefully:

| Term | Meaning | What happens to it during the reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxidised species | loses electrons | gains oxidation number |
| Reduced species | gains electrons | loses oxidation number |
| **Oxidising agent** | the species that **removes** electrons from something else | is itself **reduced** |
| **Reducing agent** | the species that **supplies** electrons | is itself **oxidised** |

An agent is identified by the job it does, not by what it suffers. In the zinc–copper reaction, $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ is the oxidising agent (it takes electrons and is reduced), and zinc metal is the reducing agent (it gives electrons and is oxidised).

Common laboratory oxidising agents: $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$, $\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}$, $\mathrm{HNO_3}$, $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$. Common reducing agents: active metals (Zn, Al, Mg), $\mathrm{H_2}$, CO, $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{H_2S}$, and the $(\mathrm{COO})_2^{2-}$ oxalate ion.

## 3. Redox couples

Any two species that differ from each other by an integral number of electrons form a **redox couple**, written Ox/Red:

$$
\mathrm{Zn^{2+}/Zn}, \qquad \mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}, \qquad \mathrm{MnO_4^-/Mn^{2+}}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl_2/Cl^-}
$$

A couple is like the conjugate acid–base pair you met in the Equilibrium chapter: the two forms can be interconverted, and the conversion is one half-reaction. A complete redox reaction always pairs two couples together — the electrons released by one couple are the exact electrons gained by the other. Extracting the couples from a given equation is the first step in balancing by the half-reaction method (covered in the fourth module of this chapter).

## 4. Finding who did what to whom

Reading a redox equation is a skill in itself. Work through it in three moves:

1. Split the equation into ions including charges.
2. Locate the electron transfer: count how many electrons move from the couple that is oxidised to the couple that is reduced.
3. Name the oxidant, the reductant and the spectator (the species whose atoms change not at all).

<learning-block type="example" title="Worked example: zinc and silver nitrate">

$$
\mathrm{Zn + 2AgNO_3 \rightarrow Zn(NO_3)_2 + 2Ag}
$$

Split into everything that actually reacts:

$$
\mathrm{Zn \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + 2e^-} \qquad \mathrm{2(Ag^+ + e^- \rightarrow Ag)}
$$

Two silver ions must be reduced per zinc atom because one zinc atom releases two electrons.

- Oxidised: Zn, from $\mathrm{Zn}$ to $\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}$.
- Reduced: $\mathrm{Ag^+}$.
- Reducing agent: Zn. Oxidising agent: $\mathrm{Ag^+}$.
- Spectator: $\mathrm{NO_3^-}$ — it appears unchanged on both sides, two on each.

Sanity check: count the released electrons, $2$, and the captured electrons, $2 \times 1 = 2$. They match, so no charge is left stranded on either side.

</learning-block>

## 5. Not every reaction is a redox reaction

Electron transfer changes atoms only when the number of electrons attached to an atom changes. In $\mathrm{CaO + CO_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3}$ every atom keeps its electrons; the reaction merely shuffles bonds. In $\mathrm{NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O}$ the proton moves from hydroxide to chloride, but no atom gains or loses electrons. These are not redox reactions at all. Oxygen involvement is neither necessary (see zinc and silver) nor sufficient (see the carbonate above) for redox behaviour.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="The agent–sufferer reversal">

The single most common error in this topic is to call the oxidised species the oxidising agent. Remember that an *agent* does the deed to someone else, and doing the deed costs the agent: the oxidising agent grabs electrons and is therefore reduced. Test yourself each time with one sentence: "This species took electrons away from ___ , so it itself must have gained them."

A second error is assuming the metal taking part in the cheaper coin always gets oxidised. The direction of electron flow is decided by the relative tendencies of the two couples, not by appearance or price.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** In the reaction $\mathrm{Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu}$, state which species is oxidised, which is reduced, and name the oxidising agent.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Write the working ions: iron ends as $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and copper ends as $\mathrm{Cu}$, the sulphate ion $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$ accompanying both but unchanged. The electron transfer is $\mathrm{Fe \rightarrow Fe^{2+} + 2e^-}$ and $\mathrm{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu}$. Iron metal loses electrons, so Fe is oxidised; $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ gains them, so it is reduced and is the oxidising agent. Fe is the reducing agent and $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$ is a spectator.

</details>

**B.** Aluminium burns in chlorine to give $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$. Write the two half-reactions and say how many chlorine molecules are reduced per aluminium atom.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{Al \rightarrow Al^{3+} + 3e^-}$ takes three electrons from one aluminium atom. Each chlorine molecule needs two electrons: $\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2e^- \rightarrow 2Cl^-}$. The smallest common electron count is 6, giving $\mathrm{2Al + 3Cl_2 \rightarrow 2AlCl_3}$: two aluminium atoms and three chlorine molecules. So three chlorine molecules are reduced per two aluminium atoms, i.e. $\tfrac32$ per aluminium atom.

</details>

**C.** Hydrogen burns in oxygen to give water. Identify the two redox couples in $\mathrm{2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Hydrogen is 0 as the element $\mathrm{H_2}$ and ends as the hydrogen of $\mathrm{H_2O}$, having lost one electron per atom. Oxygen is 0 as $\mathrm{O_2}$ and ends as the oxygen of $\mathrm{H_2O}$, having gained two electrons per atom. The couples are $\mathrm{H_2O/H_2}$ (the reduced form is written second) and $\mathrm{O_2/H_2O}$. Combustion is therefore an oxidation of the fuel by oxygen as oxidising agent.

</details>

## Remember

- Oxidation is electron loss, reduction is electron gain; electrons released must always equal electrons captured.
- An oxidising agent is itself reduced; a reducing agent is itself oxidised.
- Every redox reaction is two half-reactions (two redox couples) joined at the electrons.
- Split the equation into ions and check spectator ions before naming the agents.
- Presence of oxygen neither proves nor disproves a redox change.
