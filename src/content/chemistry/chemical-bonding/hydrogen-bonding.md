## Start with thermometer evidence

Compare the boiling points of the group-16 hydrides: $\mathrm{H_2O}$ boils at 373 K while $\mathrm{H_2S}$ boils at 212 K. On every intramolecular measure — bond lengths, bond enthalpies — $\mathrm{H_2S}$ is the heavier molecule and should need more heat to separate. Yet water's boiling point towers above every trend line. The culprit is not the covalent bond at all, but the forces **between** molecules: hydrogen bonds. This module covers the intermolecular family in order of strength and what each one does to matter.

## 1. Dipole moment: measuring bond polarity

When two bonded atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared electron density shifts toward the more electronegative atom. The bond is **polar**. A whole molecule may then have a permanent dipole, measured as the **dipole moment**:

$$
\mu = q \times d,
$$

with charge $q$ in esu or coulomb and separation $d$ in metres. Chemists quote $\mu$ in **debye** (D), defined so that a charge separation of $4.8 \times 10^{-10}$ esu across $1\ \text{Å}$ gives exactly 4.8 D. Some benchmark values: HF $1.91\ \text{D}$, HCl $1.03$, HBr $0.79$, HI $0.38$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$ $1.85$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$ $1.47$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ $0$.

The zero on $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is the teaching moment. Each C=O bond is strongly polar, but the molecule is **linear**, so the two bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel vectorially. Polarity is a **vector sum**, and molecular shape decides it:

| Molecule | Shape | Bond dipoles | Resulting $\mu$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CO_2}$ | linear | two, opposite, equal | 0 |
| $\mathrm{H_2O}$ | bent ($104.5^\circ$) | two, at an angle, unequal separation | $1.85\ \text{D}$ |
| $\mathrm{BF_3}$ | trigonal planar | three, symmetric triangle | 0 |
| $\mathrm{NH_3}$ | trigonal pyramidal | three, at angles on a cone | $1.47\ \text{D}$ |
| $\mathrm{CCl_4}$ | tetrahedral | four, symmetric tetrahedron | 0 |
| $\mathrm{CHCl_3}$ | tetrahedral but unsymmetrical | three Cl dipoles, one H | $1.04\ \text{D}$ |

Symmetric molecules with identical outer atoms and no lone pair on the central atom have $\mu = 0$; any asymmetry — one lone pair, one substituted atom, a bent shape — breaks the cancellation. Dipole moments are therefore an experimental probe of shape, not just of polarity.

## 2. The intermolecular family, weakest to strongest

| Force | Acts between | Typical strength | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| London dispersion | all molecules | $0.5$–$40\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, rising with size | $\mathrm{I_2}$ solid is held together this way |
| dipole–dipole (Keesom) | permanent dipoles | comparable with dispersion | acetone, HCl |
| hydrogen bond | H donor X–H to lone-pair acceptor | $10$–$40\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, strongest cases higher | water, alcohols, amides |

The phrase "van der Waals forces" is used loosely in many books for dispersion plus dipole forces together; interpret whichever term an exam question uses against the definitions above.

**London dispersion** arises because electron clouds fluctuate: an instantaneous dipole induces alignment in its neighbour, and the correlated induced dipoles attract. It exists in *every* molecule — including noble gases — and it is the only intermolecular force between non-polar species. It grows with the number of electrons and with molecular surface area: heptane boils at 371 K while butane boils at 273 K, purely on dispersion, because heptane's chain has more electrons and more area to polarise.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Force-strength confusions">

- Treating dispersion as weak because a lone helium pair is. Summed across big molecules it dominates: $\mathrm{I_2}$, with zero dipole, sits solid on the bench at room temperature — stronger intermolecular pull than many polar gases can manage.
- Assigning the boil-point rise down the halogens to polarity: $\mathrm{F_2 < Cl_2 < Br_2 < I_2}$ is a pure dispersion effect, since all are non-polar.
- Calling a hydrogen bond a "strong dipole–dipole force". It has covalent-adjacent character, a preferred direction, and outcompetes dipole–dipole by a large factor.

</learning-block>

## 3. The hydrogen bond

A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen **covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom** (N, O or F) points its partially bare proton at a lone pair on another such atom:

$$
\mathrm{X{-}H \cdots {:}Y}
$$

The donor X–H must be a strong dipole; only N, O and F are electronegative enough. Intermolecular examples: water's tetrahedral network (each molecule donates two H and accepts two), alcohol chains, HF's zigzag chains in liquid and gas. A hydrogen bond gives direction as well as strength, because the donor H points straight at the acceptor lone pair.

**Worked example: the group-16 hydride boiling points.**

| Hydride | bp (K) |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{H_2O}$ | 373 |
| $\mathrm{H_2S}$ | 212 |
| $\mathrm{H_2Se}$ | 232 |
| $\mathrm{H_2Te}$ | 271 |

Down the group, dispersion grows as the molecules grow, so H₂Te (271 K) boils above H₂Se (232 K) and H₂S (212 K). Extrapolate that smooth trend and water "should" boil near 185 K. Its actual 373 K comes entirely from each molecule's two donated and two accepted hydrogen bonds. The gaps to the trend represent the hydrogen-bond energy in thermal terms.

## 4. Hydrogen bonds *inside* molecules

Intramolecular H-bonds form when donor and acceptor sit on the same molecule with geometry to spare. The classic comparison is ortho- versus para-nitrophenol:

| Isomer | H-bond type | Consequence in boiling/sublimation behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $o$-nitrophenol | intramolecular $\mathrm{O{-}H \cdots O}$ within one molecule | the H-bond is "spent" internally; molecule–molecule contacts are weaker → more volatile, steam-distillable |
| $p$-nitrophenol | intermolecular $\mathrm{O{-}H \cdots O}$ between molecules | molecules link into extended networks → high melting point, not steam-distillable |

Salicylaldehyde shows the same trick as $o$-nitrophenol. Carboxylic acids do the opposite: formic and acetic acid pair up as **cyclic dimers** in the gas phase through two intermolecular H-bonds each — which is why even acetic acid's dimeric molar mass looks doubled in vapour-density measurements.

## 5. Effects on solubility and the structure of ice

Hydrogen bonding decides who dissolves: alcohols dissolve freely in water as long as the hydrocarbon tail is short (ethanol, mixable; pentanol, partly; octanol, barely), because each –OH both donates and accepts H-bonds with water. Ammonia dissolves freely for the same reason; conversely, hexane (no donors, no acceptors) refuses water.

Ice is the architectural proof. Each water molecule locks into four neighbours in a tetrahedral, open lattice, and that emptiness is why **ice is less dense than liquid water** and floats on it. As ice melts the lattice partially collapses, so the density of the melt rises with temperature until $4^\circ\text{C}$ (277 K); beyond that ordinary thermal expansion takes over and water thins out again. Real lakes therefore freeze from the surface down, and the temperature-stratified water beneath the ice stays liquid at $4^\circ\text{C}$.

<learning-block type="concept" title="Why the anomaly matters">

Water's density anomaly regulates climate and biology: floating ice insulates lakes in winter; ocean and body temperatures are buffered by water's huge heat capacity and hydrogen-bonded network. Biology adds further H-bonds: DNA base pairs pair adenine–thymine and guanine–cytosine through two and three H-bonds respectively, and protein α-helices are stitched by N–H···O=C links along the backbone.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Which has the higher boiling point, ethanol (bp 351 K) or dimethyl ether (bp 248 K), and why? Both are $\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Ethanol. Its hydrogen is bonded directly to oxygen, so two ethanol molecules form O–H···O hydrogen bonds between them. In dimethyl ether every hydrogen sits on carbon, so no O–H donor exists and no intermolecular H-bond can form: only dispersion plus dipole–dipole hold it together. Same formula, different donor — different boiling point. This pair is classic evidence that hydrogen bonding needs the X–H donor, not merely the presence of hetero atoms.

</details>

**B.** Rank in order of increasing boiling point: $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{CH_3Cl}$, $\mathrm{CH_3OH}$, and justify.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{CH_4\ (112\ K) < CH_3Cl\ (249\ K) < CH_3OH\ (338\ K)}$. Methane relies on dispersion only (few electrons, small cloud). Chloromethane adds a permanent dipole: dipole–dipole attraction plus somewhat larger dispersion. Methanol has both of those plus intermolecular hydrogen bonding through its O–H group — the strongest set of the three.

</details>

**C.** Acetic acid is dimeric in the vapour phase and in benzene solution, but monomeric in water. Explain both observations.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

In the vapour and in benzene there is nothing better to hydrogen-bond with, so two acid molecules close the loop on each other through **two mutual O–H···O hydrogen bonds**, as a six-membered cyclic dimer — vapour-density measurements then report an apparent molar mass near 120. Water is a strong H-bond partner: each –COOH hydrogen-bonds with water instead, breaking the dimer apart, so in aqueous solution the acid behaves as monomers solvated by water.

</details>

## Remember

- Dipole moment is a vector sum: shape can cancel polar bonds entirely ($\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{BF_3}$, $\mathrm{CCl_4}$) or reveal asymmetry.
- Intermolecular forces rank roughly: dispersion (everywhere) < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding (needs an N–H, O–H or F–H donor).
- Dispersion grows with electron count and surface area — it alone sets the boiling-point trend down the halogens.
- Intramolecular H-bonds (ortho-nitrophenol) reduce molecule–molecule contact; intermolecular ones (para-nitrophenol) build networks.
- Boiling-point anomalies (water, ammonia, HF), ice floating, and small-alcohol miscibility are all the same physics seen from different sides.
