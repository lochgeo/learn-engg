## Start with the geometry problem

Draw methane four ways and all are the same; draw water with its two lone pairs ignored and you would predict a linear molecule with no polarity — yet water is bent, $104.5^\circ$, and that single fact underlies nearly all its chemistry. Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory answers the shape question with one assumption: electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to be **as far apart as possible**.

## 1. Counting regions of electron density

Work only with the Lewis structure and treat everything attached to the central atom — single bond, double bond, triple bond, lone pair — as one **electron domain** (also called a bond pair/lone pair or a "region of electron density"). A double bond counts as one domain because its two electron pairs must stay on the same side of the atom anyway, packed between the same two nuclei.

Then name the arrangement of the domains:

| Number of domains | Electron-pair geometry | Angles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | linear | $180^\circ$ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | $90^\circ$, $120^\circ$ |
| 6 | octahedral | $90^\circ$ |

## 2. Lone pairs squeeze

Repulsions are not equal. Repulsion strength runs:

$$
\text{lone pair–lone pair} > \text{lone pair–bond pair} > \text{bond pair–bond pair},
$$

because a lone pair is held by only one nucleus and therefore spreads out wider. Lone pairs press bond pairs together and compress the angles between them:

| Species | Domains | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{CH_4}$ | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ |
| $\mathrm{NH_3}$ | 4 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | $107^\circ$ |
| $\mathrm{H_2O}$ | 4 | 2 | bent (V-shaped) | $104.5^\circ$ |
| $\mathrm{BF_3}$ | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ |

The sequence $\mathrm{CH_4} \rightarrow \mathrm{NH_3} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}$ is the cleanest demonstration: same four domains, but each additional lone pair pushes the remaining bond pairs inward a little more.

## 3. The full shape catalogue

Write species as $\mathrm{AX_nE_m}$, with $n$ bonded atoms and $m$ lone pairs on the central atom A. The *molecular shape* names only the atoms; lone pairs shape it silently from behind.

| Type | Domains | Electron-pair geometry | Molecular shape | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{AX_2}$ | 2 | linear | linear | $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_3}$ | 3 | trigonal planar | trigonal planar | $\mathrm{BF_3}$, $\mathrm{SO_3}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_2E}$ | 3 | trigonal planar | bent | $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{O_3}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_4}$ | 4 | tetrahedral | tetrahedral | $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_3E}$ | 4 | tetrahedral | trigonal pyramidal | $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_2E_2}$ | 4 | tetrahedral | bent | $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{SCl_2}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_5}$ | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | trigonal bipyramidal | $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_4E}$ | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | seesaw | $\mathrm{SF_4}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_3E_2}$ | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | T-shaped | $\mathrm{ClF_3}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_2E_3}$ | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | linear | $\mathrm{XeF_2}$, $\mathrm{I_3^-}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_6}$ | 6 | octahedral | octahedral | $\mathrm{SF_6}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_5E}$ | 6 | octahedral | square pyramidal | $\mathrm{BrF_5}$ |
| $\mathrm{AX_4E_2}$ | 6 | octahedral | square planar | $\mathrm{XeF_4}$ |

## 4. Where lone pairs sit in a trigonal bipyramid

In a trigonal bipyramid the five positions are not equivalent: three **equatorial** (separated by $120^\circ$) and two **axial** (perpendicular, $90^\circ$ from each equatorial). A lone pair occupies whatever position minimises its $90^\circ$ contacts, because those are the tightest angles in the structure. An equatorial site touches only two axial positions at $90^\circ$; an axial site touches three. So lone pairs go **equatorial** first, whichever species we are drawing:

- $\mathrm{AX_4E}$ ($\mathrm{SF_4}$): one lone pair equatorial → seesaw, with angles bent to about $102^\circ$ (equatorial) and $173^\circ$ (axial).
- $\mathrm{AX_3E_2}$ ($\mathrm{ClF_3}$): two lone pairs equatorial → T-shaped.
- $\mathrm{AX_2E_3}$ ($\mathrm{XeF_2}$, $\mathrm{I_3^-}$): all three lone pairs equatorial → linear.

In the octahedron all six positions are equivalent, so $\mathrm{XeF_4}$ simply places its two lone pairs **trans** to each other, maximising their separation and giving a square planar molecule.

## 5. Examples across the periods

Second-period central atoms obey the octet strictly: four domains is the maximum ($\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{BF_3}$, $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$). From period 3 onward, valence $d$ orbitals allow expanded octets, so five and six domains appear: $\mathrm{PCl_5}$, $\mathrm{SF_6}$, $\mathrm{XeF_4}$, $\mathrm{I_3^-}$. The shapes themselves still obey identical repulsion logic.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Shape errors that cost marks">

- Naming the **electron-pair geometry** when the question asks for the **molecular shape**: $\mathrm{H_2O}$ is tetrahedral-by-domains but *bent* as a molecule.
- Forgetting to write the correct Lewis structure first; VSEPR applied to a wrong structure gives a wrong shape.
- Placing lone pairs **axial** in an $\mathrm{AX_4E}$ or $\mathrm{AX_3E_2}$ species — equatorial is the rule.
- Ignoring multiple-bond oxygen in $\mathrm{CO_2}$ because "double bond" sounds like two domains.
- Applying VSEPR as if lone pairs were visible in the shape name: $\mathrm{I_3^-}$ is *linear*, not trigonal bipyramidal.

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Give the shape and approximate bond angle of $\mathrm{SO_2}$ and $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{SO_2}$: $3$ domains on S (two S–O bonds, one lone pair; each S–O bond partial double in reality but one domain each) → trigonal-planar electron geometry, **bent** molecular shape, angle a little below $120^\circ$ ($\approx 119^\circ$). $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$: 4 domains (three bonds, one lone pair) → trigonal pyramidal, angle near $107^\circ$.

</details>

**B.** Predict the shapes of $\mathrm{SF_4}$ and $\mathrm{XeF_4}$, and account for the difference.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{SF_4}$: 5 domains, one lone pair takes the equatorial slot → **seesaw**. $\mathrm{XeF_4}$: 6 domains, two lone pairs take trans positions → **square planar**. Placing a lone pair in the equatorial position of a trigonal bipyramid avoids $120^\circ$ contacts and legitimises the observed minimum-energy structure; in the octahedron every position is equivalent, so the lone pairs simply maximise their separation by going trans.

</details>

**C.** Which species is not linear: $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{I_3^-}$, $\mathrm{H_2S}$, $\mathrm{XeF_2}$?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{H_2S}$. Four domains around S (two bonds, two lone pairs) make it **bent** at about $92^\circ$. The other three are linear: $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (2 domains), $\mathrm{I_3^-}$ and $\mathrm{XeF_2}$ (5 domains, all three lone pairs equatorial).

</details>

## Remember

- Draw the correct Lewis structure, then count **domains** — each single, double or triple bond is one, and so is each lone pair.
- A lone pair is invisible in the name of the shape but bends angles by $2$–$5^\circ$ per compressive influence.
- Lone pairs prefer the least-crowded positions: equatorial in a trigonal bipyramid, trans in an octahedron.
- $\mathrm{AX_2E_3}$ and $\mathrm{AX_4E_2}$ species can look strange yet follow the same rule as everything else.
- Shape feeds directly into polarity, which drives both solubility and boiling point — the last module of this chapter closes that loop.

Molecular shape is one influence on how orbitals meet; the next module asks how atomic orbitals mix into new ones to make those shapes achievable.
