## Your study plan for this chapter

Chemical bonding is learned as three layers, and each layer answers a different exam question. A focused pass takes about **2 hours 40 minutes**:

1. **Lewis structures and formal charge** — 25 min: get counting automatic.
2. **Ionic bonding and lattice enthalpy** — 22 min: energy accounts and Fajans' rules.
3. **Covalent bonds and bond parameters** — 22 min: length, enthalpy, bond order.
4. **VSEPR** — 25 min: shapes and angles, on top of correct structures.
5. **Hybridisation** — 25 min: overlap, steric numbers, where VB theory fails.
6. **Molecular orbital theory** — 25 min: filling orders, bond order, magnetism.
7. **Hydrogen bonding** — 22 min: dipoles and intermolecular forces.
8. **This mastery check** — 25 min.

After each concept lesson, redraw its tables from memory: the shape table, the hybridisation table, the second-period MO filling order. These three tables carry most of the chapter's marks. Then use the optional **JEE Main timed practice** module for a 50-minute mixed set.

## Mastery check

Try each question without looking at the reveal first. The set runs from routine to demanding; the last two questions ask for reasoning, not arithmetic.

### 1. Lewis structure of N2O

Draw the Lewis structure of $\mathrm{N_2O}$ with N central and assign every formal charge. Which of the two reasonable octet structures puts the negative charge on oxygen?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

16 valence electrons, skeleton N–N–O. The two octet structures are $\mathrm{N{\equiv}N^+{-}O^-}$ (terminal N $0$, central N $+1$, O $-1$) and $\mathrm{^-N{=}N^+{=}O}$ (terminal N $-1$, central N $+1$, O $0$). Both put $+1$ on the central nitrogen; the difference is which terminal atom carries the $-1$. The form with the negative charge **on oxygen**, the more electronegative atom, is the more realistic contributor.
</details>

### 2. Born–Haber for KCl

Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation of KCl from: $\Delta_f H = -437$; K sublimation $+89$; $IE_1(\mathrm{K}) = +419$; $\tfrac12 D(\mathrm{Cl_2}) = +122$; $\Delta_{eg}H(\mathrm{Cl}) = -349\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Rearranging the cycle: $\Delta_{lattice}H = \Delta_f H - (\Delta_{sub}H + IE + \tfrac12 D + \Delta_{eg}H) = -437 - (89 + 419 + 122 - 349) = -437 - 281 = -718\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The value is negative because the lattice *forms* from gaseous ions; a question asking for the lattice *dissociation* enthalpy wants $+718\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
</details>

### 3. Reaction enthalpy from bond enthalpies

Estimate the enthalpy of $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)}$ using bond enthalpies (N–H $= 389$), and reconcile the estimate with the accepted value of $-92\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Broken: N≡N ($941$) and three H–H ($3 \times 436 = 1308$), totalling $2249$. Formed: six N–H $= 6 \times 389 = 2334$. $\Delta H = 2249 - 2334 = -85\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The estimate is reasonably close to $-92$; the gap comes from using *average* bond enthalpies for gas-phase bonds rather than molecule-specific values, and from ignoring phase and non-ideality corrections in the real reaction.
</details>

### 4. Three tricky shapes

Predict the shape and bond angle of $\mathrm{ClF_3}$, $\mathrm{I_3^-}$ and $\mathrm{SF_4}$, naming where each lone pair sits.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{ClF_3}$: five domains, two lone pairs equatorial → **T-shaped**. $\mathrm{I_3^-}$: five domains, three lone pairs all equatorial → **linear**. $\mathrm{SF_4}$: five domains, one lone pair equatorial → **seesaw** ($\approx 173^\circ$ axial–S–axial, $\approx 102^\circ$ equatorial–S–equatorial). Lone pairs always take the equatorial slot in a trigonal bipyramid because it has only two $90^\circ$ neighbours instead of three.
</details>

### 5. Hybridisation round-robin

Assign a hybridisation to the central atom of $\mathrm{XeF_4}$, $\mathrm{SO_2}$, $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$ and $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Count sigma bonds plus lone pairs on the centre (a multiple bond still contributes only one domain). $\mathrm{XeF_4}$: steric number 6 → $sp^3d^2$ (square planar, two trans lone pairs). $\mathrm{SO_2}$: steric number 3 → $sp^2$. $\mathrm{BeCl_2}$: steric number 2 → $sp$ (linear). $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$: steric number 4 → $sp^3$ (tetrahedral).
</details>

### 6. Which one is paramagnetic?

Which species is paramagnetic: $\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$, $\mathrm{B_2}$, $\mathrm{Li_2}$?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{B_2}$ only. Fill its six valence electrons as $\sigma 2s^2\,\sigma^{*}2s^2$ then one electron in *each* $\pi 2p$ orbital (Hund's rule) → two unpaired electrons. $\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{Li_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}$ (peroxide, like $\mathrm{F_2}$'s electron count) have all electrons paired and are diamagnetic. The check in one line: paramagnetism needs an odd total or a doubly degenerate frontier level filled singly.
</details>

### 7. Bond order of NO

Give the bond order and magnetic character of $\mathrm{NO}$ (15 electrons in total).

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

NO contributes 11 valence electrons (5 from N, 6 from O). Using the $\mathrm{O_2}$-style ordering: bonding $\sigma 2s^2 + \sigma 2p^2 + \pi 2p^4 = 8$; antibonding $\sigma^{*}2s^2 + \pi^{*}2p^1 = 3$. Bond order $= \tfrac12(8 - 3) = 2.5$, and the single $\pi^{*}$ electron makes it **paramagnetic**. That half-order explains why NO's bond ($115\ \text{pm}$) sits between N≡N and N=O in strength and length.
</details>

### 8. The nitrophenol split

Why does $o$-nitrophenol steam-distil while $p$-nitrophenol cannot?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Ortho-nitrophenol's phenolic –OH forms an **intramolecular** hydrogen bond with the oxygen of its own nitro group; nothing links neighbouring molecules, so thermal jostling pulls molecules into the vapour easily and the compound co-distils with steam. Para-nitrophenol's two groups are too far apart to meet internally, so it hydrogen-bonds **between** molecules; breaking that network before vaporising demands far more heat.
</details>

### 9. Lattice enthalpy ranking

Arrange in order of increasing lattice enthalpy (as a magnitude): NaCl, MgO, NaF, KCl. Justify each step in one line.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$\mathrm{KCl < NaCl < NaF < MgO}$. KCl: the largest $\pm1$ ion pair of the three chlorides, so the smallest Coulomb attraction. NaCl: the smaller $\mathrm{Na^+}$ shrinks $r_+ + r_-$ and raises $|U|$ ($-718$ to $-788$). NaF: swapping $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ for the smaller $\mathrm{F^-}$ gains again ($\approx -918$). MgO: the $\pm2$ charges multiply the $|z_+z_-|$ product fourfold and the small ions compound it, so its magnitude ($\approx 3890$) dwarfs all three.
</details>

### 10. The NF3 paradox

Why is the bond angle of $\mathrm{NF_3}$ ($102^\circ$) smaller than that of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ ($107^\circ$) even though fluorine is far more electronegative than hydrogen?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

In $\mathrm{NH_3}$ hydrogen is electropositive, so each N–H bonding pair sits close to nitrogen and crowds the other bond pairs — the pairs repel strongly and open the angle toward tetrahedral. In $\mathrm{NF_3}$ the fluorines drag each shared pair outward along the longer N–F bonds, away from the nitrogen, where they repel one another less; the lone pair at the top then closes the angle down to $102^\circ$. VSEPR repulsions are set by where the electron density actually sits, not by which atom owns the bond.
</details>

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Bonding errors that cost marks">

- Drawing resonance structures that move **atoms** instead of electrons.
- Putting the factor $\tfrac12$ on the wrong side of a Born–Haber term, or forgetting it entirely on $D(\mathrm{Cl_2})$.
- Quoting the electron-pair geometry when the question asks for the molecular shape — check whether "shape of the molecule" or "arrangement of electron pairs" is asked.
- Using the $\mathrm{N_2}$-type filling order for $\mathrm{O_2}$ (or the reverse): swap at the N/O boundary.
- Calling dipole–dipole the reason for the halogens' boiling-point rise; there is no dipole there.

</learning-block>

## Self-check

You are ready for the timed set when you can:

- draw the Lewis structure of any simple main-group species and defend a formal-charge choice without references;
- complete a Born–Haber cycle for a $\pm1$ ion pair in under two minutes;
- name all $13$ $\mathrm{AX_nE_m}$ shapes from 2 to 6 domains and place lone pairs in trigonal-bipyramidal cases unaided;
- assign hybridisation from a steric number, including ions with lone pairs on the centre;
- fill a second-period MO diagram and read out bond order, stability and magnetism in one pass;
- explain any boiling-point anomaly in the hydrides through the correct intermolecular force.
