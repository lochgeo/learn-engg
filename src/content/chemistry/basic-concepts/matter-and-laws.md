## Chemistry begins with careful weighing

Nobody has ever watched an atom rearrange into a molecule. What chemists *can* do is weigh everything before and after the change — and from the late eighteenth century that habit of disciplined weighing produced three quantitative laws and one explanatory theory. Together they settle what matter is made of, and they justify every balance-sheet calculation in this course.

## 1. Sorting matter

Every sample you will ever handle fits somewhere on this map.

- **Pure substances** have a fixed, unvarying composition.
  - An **element** contains only one kind of atom and cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means: iron, oxygen, copper.
  - A **compound** contains two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed proportion: water, carbon dioxide, calcium carbonate.
- **Mixtures** are physical combinations whose composition *can* vary.
  - A **homogeneous mixture** is uniform throughout: a salt solution, brass, clean air.
  - A **heterogeneous mixture** shows separable or visible parts: sand stirred into water, oil shaken with water.

| Category | Fixed composition? | How it can be separated | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Element | one kind of atom | only by nuclear change | mercury |
| Compound | fixed by the combining ratio | by chemical reaction | calcium carbonate |
| Homogeneous mixture | variable | physical methods: distillation, crystallisation | a salt solution |
| Heterogeneous mixture | variable | physical methods: filtering, settling, hand-picking | muddy water |

The distinction matters practically, because the laws below hold for **pure substances only**. Sugar and salt can be mixed in any proportion you like; iron and oxygen cannot combine in any proportion you like.

## 2. Mass is conserved

Weighing tin as it calcined in sealed vessels led Antoine Lavoisier to a law that still underwrites every calculation in this chapter:

*In any chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.*

The essential trick was the **closed system**. In an open vessel, oxygen may enter from the air or a gaseous product may escape, so a local mass balance seems to fail even though mass is conserved overall.

<learning-block type="example" title="Burning magnesium, accounted honestly">

A covered crucible holding $2.4\ \text{g}$ of magnesium ribbon is heated until the metal converts entirely to white magnesium oxide, which weighs $4.0\ \text{g}$. Mass appears to have been created — until you include the oxygen: $4.0-2.4=1.6\ \text{g}$ of oxygen came in from the air. The full ledger $2.4+1.6=4.0\ \text{g}$ balances exactly. The law does not forbid gaining weight; it forbids treating the air as 'outside' when the air is reacting.

</learning-block>

Conservation of mass is *why* every balanced equation shows equal numbers of atoms of each kind on both sides — and that fact becomes a calculating tool in the stoichiometry module.

## 3. Definite proportions: a compound is a fixed recipe

Analysing natural samples gathered from many places, Joseph Proust found that composition refuses to vary:

*In a pure compound, the elements are always present in the same mass proportion.*

Every pure sample of water analyses to hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio $1:8$. Every pure sample of calcium carbonate gives calcium, carbon and oxygen in the ratio $40:12:48$ by mass, whether it was crystallised in a laboratory, mined from a chalk cliff or ground from sea shell.

<learning-block type="check" title="One ratio, reused forever">

Because every molecule of CO2 contains one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms, the carbon in *any* pure sample of CO2 is a fixed fraction, $12/44$. So if $10\ \text{g}$ of CO2 released by a fizzy drink yields $m$ grams of carbon on complete analysis, $m=10\times\frac{12}{44}=2.73\ \text{g}$ — exactly the same figure you would get for CO2 condensed from a volcanic vent. The substance, not the source, fixes the recipe.

</learning-block>

## 4. Multiple proportions: fixed amounts pair in simple ratios

Elements that form several compounds obey a second rule, stated by Dalton:

*When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio.*

Carbon forms two oxides. Take exactly $12\ \text{g}$ of carbon in each: in carbon monoxide it takes up $16\ \text{g}$ of oxygen; in carbon dioxide it takes up $32\ \text{g}$. The ratio $16:32$ simplifies to $1:2$.

Nitrogen does it five ways. Combining with a fixed $14\ \text{g}$ of nitrogen, oxygen masses of $8$, $16$, $24$, $32$ and $40\ \text{g}$ give the oxides N2O, NO, N2O3, NO2 and N2O5 — the oxygen masses fall in the ratio $1:2:3:4:5$. Small whole numbers keep appearing because **atoms combine as discrete, indivisible units**, never as fractions of an atom.

## 5. Dalton's atomic theory — and where it cracks

Dalton proposed that the laws of combination follow if matter is particulate:

- All matter is built from **atoms**, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical change.
- All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and in properties.
- Atoms of different elements differ in mass.
- Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in **small whole-number ratios**.
- In a chemical reaction, atoms are only separated, joined or rearranged, never changed into other elements.

The theory explains the laws mechanically: conservation of mass holds because atoms are neither created nor destroyed; definite proportions holds because a compound has a fixed atom-per-atom recipe; multiple proportions holds because one carbon can pair with either one oxygen (CO) or two (CO2).

Known cracks, discovered later:

- Atoms are **divisible**: electrons, protons and neutrons exist inside them.
- Atoms of one element are not always identical: **isotopes** of chlorine weigh $35$ or $37$.
- The same element may behave differently in different forms, such as diamond and graphite.

Nothing in these cracks devalues the bookkeeping; the mole concept in the next module keeps Dalton's *counting* logic and simply attaches reliable, average masses to each element.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Compound or mixture?">

A common framework error: a brass rod is usually listed as a compound because it "contains two things". It is a **homogeneous mixture** — copper and zinc can be present in any proportion, and no fixed ratio or chemical bond defines it. Test with the two questions that matter: is the composition fixed by the substance (compound) or negotiable (mixture), and would you need a chemical reaction (compound) or just a physical operation (mixture) to take it apart?

</learning-block>

## Try it yourself

**A.** Classify each as element, compound, homogeneous mixture or heterogeneous mixture: muddy water, carbon dioxide, a salt solution, ozone, brass, glucose.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

Muddy water — heterogeneous mixture. Carbon dioxide — compound. Salt solution — homogeneous mixture. Ozone — element (one kind of atom, in molecules). Brass — homogeneous mixture. Glucose — compound.
</details>

**B.** When $25\ \text{g}$ of pure calcium carbonate is heated strongly, it decomposes to calcium oxide and carbon dioxide, and $11\ \text{g}$ of CO2 escapes. What mass of calcium oxide remains, and what does the sum of the products tell you?

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

By conservation of mass, the calcium oxide must weigh $25-11=14\ \text{g}$. That is not guesswork: the fixed recipe of CaCO3 would also predict it, since $25\ \text{g}$ corresponds to $0.25\ \text{mol}$, giving $0.25\times44=11\ \text{g}$ of CO2 and $0.25\times56=14\ \text{g}$ of CaO. Data and law agree — as they must.
</details>

**C.** Sulphur forms two oxides. In one, $32\ \text{g}$ of sulphur combines with $32\ \text{g}$ of oxygen; in the other, $32\ \text{g}$ of sulphur combines with $48\ \text{g}$ of oxygen. State the law this illustrates and the formulae you would guess.

<details><summary>Show the reasoning</summary>

With a fixed $32\ \text{g}$ of sulphur the oxygen masses are $32\ \text{g}$ and $48\ \text{g}$, in the ratio $2:3$ — the law of multiple proportions. Atoms must join in whole numbers, so the formulae are SO2 and SO3, in which the oxygen atom counts per sulphur are indeed $2:3$.
</details>

## Remember

- Ask two questions to sort matter: is the composition fixed, and is a chemical reaction needed to change it?
- Conservation of mass is only checkable in practice when the system is closed or the entering/escaping species are counted.
- Every pure compound has one fixed mass ratio; use it to predict the mass of any element in any sample size.
- Whole-number ratios of combining masses reflect whole atoms combining — the root idea of all counting in chemistry.
- The next module turns these counting instincts into the chemist's working currency: masses per $6.022\times10^{23}$ particles.
