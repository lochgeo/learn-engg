## First choose the tool

| Clue in the question | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| "Same recipe in every sample", fixed composition | definite proportions: $m \% = \dfrac{\text{of element}}{\text{total}}\times100$ |
| Two oxides, one metal, whole-number ratio | multiple proportions: fix one element's mass |
| Grams, litres (STP) or particles given | moles first: $n=m/M$, $V=22.4n$, $N=n\times6.022\times10^{23}$ |
| Isotope percentages | weighted average: $\sum$ (fraction × mass) |
| Percentage analysis of an unknown | empirical formula: assume $100$ g, divide by atomic masses |
| Molecular formula | empirical formula $\times\ \dfrac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical mass}}$ |
| Balanced equation and masses | mole-ratio bridge: mass → mol → mol → mass |
| Two or more reactant amounts | limiting reagent: smallest $\dfrac{n}{\text{coefficient}}$ |
| Actual vs expected product | percentage yield $=\dfrac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}}\times100$ |
| Solution strength by volume | molarity $=\dfrac{n}{V(\text{L})}$, dilution $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$ |
| Solution strength independent of T | molality $=\dfrac{n}{\text{kg of solvent}}$; mole fraction for theory |
| Multi-step and mixed data | the chain map: everything runs through moles |

## Mastery check

Try each question without looking at the answer. Worked-example atomic masses apply: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, K = 39, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 63.5, B = 10.8.

### 1. Whole-number ratios from analysis

Two oxides of nitrogen analyse as follows: oxide P is $46.7\%$ N, oxide Q is $30.4\%$ N. Taking a fixed $14\ \text{g}$ of nitrogen in each, find the oxygen masses and the ratio between them. Which law does this display, and which two formulae fit?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Per gram of nitrogen, oxide P carries $\frac{0.533}{0.467}=1.14\ \text{g}$ oxygen, so $14\ \text{g}$ N pairs with $16\ \text{g}$ O. Oxide Q carries $\frac{0.696}{0.304}=2.29\ \text{g}$ oxygen per gram of N, so $14\ \text{g}$ N pairs with about $32\ \text{g}$ O. Ratio $16:32=1:2$ — the law of multiple proportions. The formulae are NO (14N : 16O) and NO2 (14N : 32O).
</details>

### 2. Average atomic mass

An element is $20\%$ isotope of mass $10\ \text{u}$ and $80\%$ isotope of mass $11\ \text{u}$. What is the atomic mass you would quote?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$$
\text{mass}=\frac{(20)(10)+(80)(11)}{100}=10.8\ \text{u},
$$

matching the tabulated value for boron. No individual atom weighs $10.8$ — that is the *bulk* average.
</details>

### 3. Counting molecules

How many molecules are in $36\ \text{g}$ of water, and how many hydrogen atoms?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$n=36/18=2$ mol. Molecules: $2\times6.022\times10^{23}=1.204\times10^{24}$. Each molecule has 2 hydrogen atoms, so hydrogen atoms $=2.41\times10^{24}$. Cross-check: total atoms $=2.41\times10^{24}+1.204\times10^{24}=3.61\times10^{24}=3\times$ molecule count, exactly the H2O recipe.
</details>

### 4. Empirical and molecular formula

A hydrocarbon analyses to $85.7\%$ C and $14.3\%$ H. Its molar mass is $84\ \text{g/mol}$. Find both formulae.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Per $100\ \text{g}$: C $=85.7/12=7.14$ mol; H $=14.3/1=14.3$ mol. Dividing by $7.14$ gives $1:2$, so the empirical formula is CH2 (empirical mass 14). Multiplier $=84/14=6$, so the molecule is **C6H12**.
</details>

### 5. Who limits?

$5.6\ \text{g}$ of nitrogen reacts with $2.0\ \text{g}$ of hydrogen: $\mathrm{N_2 + 3\,H_2 \rightarrow 2\,NH_3}$. Identify the limiting reagent and the maximum mass of ammonia.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Moles: N2 $=5.6/28=0.2$; H2 $=2.0/2=1.0$. Per coefficient: N2 $=0.2/1=0.2$; H2 $=1.0/3=0.33$. Nitrogen is smaller, so **N2 limits**. Ammonia: $0.2\times2=0.4$ mol $=0.4\times17=6.8\ \text{g}$. (Hydrogen consumed: $0.6$ mol; leftover $0.4$ mol.)
</details>

### 6. Percentage yield

In the reaction of question 5 the actual ammonia obtained was $5.1\ \text{g}$. Find the percentage yield.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$$
\%\ \text{yield}=\frac{5.1}{6.8}\times100=75\%.
$$

The other $25\%$ never became ammonia in the vessel — but conservation of mass still holds; the nitrogen sits elsewhere in the system.
</details>

### 7. Molarity

$7.45\ \text{g}$ of KCl ($M=74.5$) is dissolved and the solution is made up to $250\ \text{mL}$. Find the molarity.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$n=7.45/74.5=0.1$ mol; $V=0.250\ \text{L}$.

$$
M=0.1/0.250=0.4\ \text{mol/L}.
$$
</details>

### 8. Molality

The same $0.1$ mol of KCl is dissolved in $500\ \text{g}$ of water instead. Find the molality.

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$$
m=\frac{0.1}{0.500\ \text{kg}}=0.2\ \text{mol/kg}.
$$

Denominator: solvent only. If you had divided by the total mass ($0.6\ \text{kg}$), you would have reported $0.17$ — the sign that the denominator was misread.
</details>

### 9. Dilution

$100\ \text{mL}$ of $0.5\ \text{M}$ sodium carbonate is diluted to $500\ \text{mL}$. New molarity? Moles moved?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

Dilution conserves moles: $n=0.5\times0.100=0.05$ mol. New molarity $=0.05/0.500=0.1\ \text{M}$ — or directly $M_2=\frac{0.5\times100}{500}=0.1\ \text{M}$.
</details>

### 10. Full chain with gas volume

What mass of nitrogen occupies $0.28\ \text{L}$ at STP, and to how many significant figures should the answer be reported if the volume is quoted to two s.f.?

<details><summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

$n=0.28/22.4=0.0125$ mol; $m=0.0125\times28=0.35\ \text{g}$. Report $0.35\ \text{g}$ — two significant figures, matching the volume data. The chain is volume → moles → mass, with rounding only at the end.
</details>

## Self-check

You are ready for the timed set when you can:

- classify any unfamiliar sample as element, compound or mixture, and say which physical or chemical separation would take it apart;
- convert between mass, moles, particles and STP gas volume without hesitating over which bridge applies;
- turn a percentage table into an empirical formula, and a molar mass into the molecular formula;
- balance an equation and read its coefficients as a mole recipe, never as masses;
- find the limiting reagent with $n/\text{coefficient}$ and defend why the comparison is not made in grams;
- compute theoretical and percentage yield and explain where the "lost" mass goes;
- state molarity, molality, mole fraction, mass % and ppm for one solution from a single data set, and say which vary with temperature;
- run a four-link chain (mass → moles → reaction → solution or gas) and round the final answer honestly.

Anything still shaky: return to that module's worked example and redo it from the given data alone.
