## Finding out what is in an organic compound

Qualitative analysis answers “which elements are present?”. Quantitative analysis answers “how much of each?”. Together they turn an unknown substance into an empirical formula, and with a molar mass, into a molecular formula.

## 1. Qualitative analysis

### Carbon and hydrogen

Heat the compound with dry copper(II) oxide. Carbon is oxidised to CO₂, which turns limewater milky, and hydrogen becomes water, which turns anhydrous copper(II) sulphate from white to blue.

$$
\mathrm{C + 2CuO \rightarrow CO_2 + 2Cu}
$$

$$
\mathrm{CO_2 + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow CaCO_3 + H_2O}
$$

### Nitrogen, sulphur and halogens: Lassaigne's test

Sodium fusion converts covalently bonded elements into soluble sodium salts. A small piece of sodium is heated with the compound, the residue is extracted with water, and the filtrate is the **sodium fusion extract (SFE)**.

$$
\mathrm{Na + C + N \rightarrow NaCN}
$$

$$
\mathrm{2Na + S \rightarrow Na_2S}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Na + X \rightarrow NaX}
$$

| Element | Reagents added to the SFE | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nitrogen | FeSO₄, then acidified FeCl₃ | Prussian blue colour |
| sulphur | sodium nitroprusside | violet colour |
| sulphur | lead acetate | black PbS precipitate |
| chlorine | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃ | white precipitate |
| bromine | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃ | pale yellow precipitate |
| iodine | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃ | yellow precipitate |

If nitrogen and sulphur are both present, sodium thiocyanate forms and iron(III) gives a blood-red colour. When testing for halogens, the extract is acidified with dilute nitric acid and boiled to remove HCN and H₂S, which would otherwise interfere.

A copper wire dipped in the compound and held in a flame gives a green colour if a halogen is present (Beilstein test). It is a quick screening test, not a proof.

## 2. Quantitative analysis

### Carbon and hydrogen: combustion

The compound is burnt in a stream of oxygen over copper(II) oxide. Water is absorbed in a weighed tube of anhydrous calcium chloride or magnesium perchlorate; carbon dioxide is absorbed in a weighed tube of potassium hydroxide.

$$
\%\mathrm{C}=\frac{12}{44}\times\frac{\text{mass of CO}_2}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

$$
\%\mathrm{H}=\frac{2}{18}\times\frac{\text{mass of H}_2\mathrm{O}}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

### Nitrogen: Dumas and Kjeldahl

**Dumas method** — the compound is heated with copper(II) oxide in an atmosphere of CO₂; nitrogen is collected as N₂ gas over potassium hydroxide.

$$
\%\mathrm{N}=\frac{28}{22400}\times\frac{V}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

where $V$ is the volume of nitrogen in mL at STP.

**Kjeldahl method** — the compound is digested with concentrated H₂SO₄, converting nitrogen to ammonium sulphate. The ammonia released on adding alkali is absorbed in a measured excess of standard acid, and the unused acid is titrated.

$$
\%\mathrm{N}=\frac{1.4\times N\times V}{\text{mass of compound}}
$$

where $N$ is the normality of the acid and $V$ the volume of acid consumed, in mL. Kjeldahl's method fails for compounds containing nitro, azo or ring nitrogen that is not converted to ammonia.

### Halogens, sulphur and phosphorus: Carius method

The compound is heated with fuming nitric acid and silver nitrate in a sealed tube. Halogens precipitate as silver halide.

$$
\%\mathrm{X}=\frac{\text{atomic mass of X}}{108+\text{atomic mass of X}}\times\frac{\text{mass of AgX}}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

Sulphur is oxidised to sulphate and precipitated as barium sulphate:

$$
\%\mathrm{S}=\frac{32}{233}\times\frac{\text{mass of BaSO}_4}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

Phosphorus is precipitated as ammonium phosphomolybdate and weighed as magnesium pyrophosphate:

$$
\%\mathrm{P}=\frac{62}{222}\times\frac{\text{mass of Mg}_2\mathrm{P}_2\mathrm{O}_7}{\text{mass of compound}}\times100
$$

**Oxygen** is usually found by difference: subtract the percentages of all other elements from 100.

## 3. Empirical and molecular formulae

1. Divide each percentage by the atomic mass to get moles.
2. Divide every value by the smallest.
3. If the ratios are not whole numbers, multiply through to obtain the simplest whole-number ratio.
4. The result is the **empirical formula**.
5. Divide the molar mass by the empirical formula mass to get $n$; the molecular formula is the empirical formula multiplied by $n$.

<learning-block type="example" title="A complete formula calculation">

A compound contains 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O by mass, and has molar mass 180 g mol⁻¹.

Moles: C $=40.0/12=3.33$; H $=6.7/1=6.7$; O $=53.3/16=3.33$.

Divide by 3.33: C 1, H 2, O 1. Empirical formula CH₂O, mass 30.

$n=180/30=6$, so the molecular formula is **C₆H₁₂O₆**.

</learning-block>

## 4. Worked calculations

### Example 1: percentage carbon and hydrogen

0.20 g of a compound gives 0.44 g CO₂ and 0.18 g H₂O.

$$
\%\mathrm{C}=\frac{12}{44}\times\frac{0.44}{0.20}\times100=60\%
$$

$$
\%\mathrm{H}=\frac{2}{18}\times\frac{0.18}{0.20}\times100=10\%
$$

### Example 2: nitrogen by Dumas

0.25 g of a compound gives 40 mL of N₂ at STP.

$$
\%\mathrm{N}=\frac{28}{22400}\times\frac{40}{0.25}\times100=20\%
$$

### Example 3: nitrogen by Kjeldahl

0.30 g of a compound consumes 15 mL of 0.1 N acid.

$$
\%\mathrm{N}=\frac{1.4\times0.1\times15}{0.30}=7.0\%
$$

### Example 4: bromine by Carius

0.20 g gives 0.287 g AgBr. Using $M(\mathrm{AgBr})=188$:

$$
\%\mathrm{Br}=\frac{80}{188}\times\frac{0.287}{0.20}\times100=61.1\%
$$

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Unit and method traps">

- **Using the mass of the compound instead of the mass of CO₂ or H₂O** in the numerator.
- **Forgetting the factor of 2 for hydrogen.** Each mole of H₂O contains two moles of H.
- **Mixing normality and molarity** in the Kjeldahl formula.
- **Using Kjeldahl for nitro compounds.** The nitrogen is not fully converted to ammonia; use Dumas.
- **Treating the empirical formula as the molecular formula** when a molar mass is available.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Why does anhydrous copper(II) sulphate turn blue in the carbon–hydrogen test?
2. What is the purpose of sodium fusion in Lassaigne's test?
3. 0.15 g of an organic compound gives 0.22 g CO₂ and 0.09 g H₂O. Find %C and %H.
4. 0.20 g of a compound gives 28 mL N₂ at STP by Dumas. Calculate %N.
5. A compound contains 24.3% C, 4.1% H and 71.6% Cl, with molar mass 99 g mol⁻¹. Find its empirical and molecular formulae.
6. Why is oxygen usually estimated by difference?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Water produced by combustion hydrates the white anhydrous salt to blue CuSO₄·5H₂O.
2. It converts covalently bonded N, S and halogens into water-soluble sodium salts (NaCN, Na₂S, NaX) that can be tested by ionic reactions.
3. %C $=\frac{12}{44}\times\frac{0.22}{0.15}\times100=40\%$; %H $=\frac{2}{18}\times\frac{0.09}{0.15}\times100=6.67\%$.
4. %N $=\frac{28}{22400}\times\frac{28}{0.20}\times100=17.5\%$.
5. Moles: C $24.3/12=2.02$; H $4.1/1=4.1$; Cl $71.6/35.5=2.02$. Ratio CH₂Cl, mass 49.5. $n=99/49.5=2$, so C₂H₄Cl₂.
6. Direct estimation of oxygen is difficult; once all other elements are measured, the remainder is taken as oxygen.

</details>

## Take away

Qualitative tests identify the elements; quantitative methods convert those elements into masses and percentages; the percentages become an empirical formula, and the molar mass turns it into the molecular formula. Learn the constants (12/44, 2/18, 28/22400, 1.4, 32/233) and the method each belongs to.
