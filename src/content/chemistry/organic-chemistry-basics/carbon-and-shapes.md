## Why carbon gets its own branch of chemistry

Organic chemistry was once defined as the chemistry of compounds produced by living things. That definition collapsed in 1828, when Friedrich Wöhler heated ammonium cyanate and obtained urea — a compound previously known only from urine. The lesson stuck: carbon compounds obey ordinary chemical laws, and they can be made in a laboratory.

Today, **organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds**, with a small set of exceptions treated as inorganic: carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides and carbides.

Carbon produces millions of compounds because it combines four useful abilities:

1. **Tetravalency** — it forms four covalent bonds.
2. **Catenation** — it bonds to other carbon atoms in chains, branches and rings.
3. **Multiple bonding** — it forms stable double and triple bonds.
4. **Heteroatom bonding** — it bonds to H, O, N, S and halogens, creating functional groups.

<learning-block type="concept" title="The one-line summary">

A carbon skeleton supplies the shape; functional groups supply the chemistry. Most of this chapter is about describing the skeleton precisely and predicting what the functional groups do.

</learning-block>

## 1. Tetravalency, hybridisation and shape

A carbon atom has the ground-state configuration $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2$. Promotion of one $2s$ electron gives four half-filled orbitals, so carbon can form four covalent bonds. The observed shapes come from mixing (hybridising) the orbitals:

| Hybridisation | Shape | Bond angle | Example | Bonds made |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $sp^3$ | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ | CH₄ | four σ |
| $sp^2$ | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ | CH₂=CH₂ | three σ + one π |
| $sp$ | linear | $180^\circ$ | HC≡CH | two σ + two π |

A **σ bond** is formed by end-on overlap and allows rotation. A **π bond** is formed by side-on overlap of p orbitals; it is weaker and locks the atoms into a plane, which is why alkenes show cis–trans isomerism.

| Bond | Typical length | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C–C | 154 pm | single, free rotation |
| C=C | 134 pm | shorter; one σ, one π |
| C≡C | 120 pm | shortest; one σ, two π |

Do not assume that a triple bond is three times as strong as a single bond. The σ framework stays much the same; the extra π bonds are individually weaker, which is why alkenes and alkynes are more reactive than alkanes.

## 2. Three ways to write the same molecule

**Full Lewis structure** shows every atom and every valence electron. It is useful for checking octets but slow to draw.

**Condensed formula** writes the atoms in order and uses brackets to show branches:

- butane: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃
- 2-methylpropane: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃
- propan-2-ol: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃

**Bond-line (skeletal) formula** is the working shorthand of organic chemistry:

- every line end and every vertex is a carbon atom;
- hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are **not** drawn — they are inferred from the four-bond rule;
- heteroatoms (O, N, S, halogens) and the hydrogens bonded to them **are** drawn.

<learning-block type="example" title="Reading a skeleton">

A skeletal drawing shows a five-carbon zig-zag chain with a methyl branch on carbon 3 and an OH group on carbon 2. Count the skeleton: five chain carbons plus one branch = C₆. It is a saturated acyclic alcohol, so the formula is C₆H₁₄O (six carbons, 13 hydrogens on carbon plus the one on oxygen).

Name it: the longest chain containing the –OH is five carbons long, so the parent is pentan-2-ol with a methyl at C3 — **3-methylpentan-2-ol**.

</learning-block>

## 3. Classification and functional groups

Organic compounds are first divided by skeleton:

- **Open chain (acyclic)** — carbon atoms in chains, with or without branches.
- **Cyclic** — at least one ring. A ring made only of carbon is **homocyclic**; a ring containing another element is **heterocyclic**. Homocyclic rings are **alicyclic** if they behave like alkanes/alkenes and **aromatic** if they satisfy the aromaticity rules.

They are then divided by bonding: **saturated** compounds contain only single bonds; **unsaturated** compounds contain double or triple bonds.

The functional group is the part that reacts. Learn this table as vocabulary, not as a list to memorise blindly:

| Class | Group | Suffix or prefix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| alkane | — | -ane |
| alkene | C=C | -ene |
| alkyne | C≡C | -yne |
| haloalkane | –X | prefix: chloro-, bromo- |
| alcohol | –OH | -ol |
| ether | –O– | prefix: alkoxy- |
| aldehyde | –CHO | -al |
| ketone | >C=O | -one |
| carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid |
| ester | –COOR | alkyl -oate |
| amine | –NH₂ | -amine |
| amide | –CONH₂ | -amide |
| nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile |
| nitro | –NO₂ | prefix: nitro- |

A **homologous series** is a family with the same functional group and the same general formula, in which successive members differ by one CH₂ unit (14 u). Members show a gradual change in physical properties and closely similar chemical behaviour.

- alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
- alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ
- alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
- alcohols: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH

## 4. Degrees of unsaturation

A molecular formula alone tells you how many rings and π bonds are present. For a formula containing C, H, N and halogens,

$$
\text{DoU}=\frac{2C+2+N-H-X}{2}.
$$

Oxygen and sulphur are ignored. Each degree of unsaturation is one ring **or** one π bond. This is the fastest way to check whether a proposed structure is even possible.

<learning-block type="example" title="Two quick checks">

**C₄H₈:** $\text{DoU}=(8+2-8)/2=1$. One double bond or one ring — and indeed but-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene, cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane all fit.

**C₆H₆:** $\text{DoU}=(12+2-6)/2=4$. Benzene uses one ring and three π bonds, which is exactly 4.

</learning-block>

## 5. Worked examples

### Example 1: formula from a skeleton

A bond-line drawing shows a four-carbon chain with an OH group on carbon 2 and a methyl group on carbon 3. Count the skeleton: four chain carbons plus one branch = C₅. It is a saturated acyclic alcohol, so H = $2(5)+2=12$ before replacing one H by OH — the oxygen does not change the hydrogen count. Formula: **C₅H₁₂O**, name: 3-methylbutan-2-ol.

### Example 2: which group is principal?

In HOCH₂CH₂COOH, two functional groups are present: alcohol and carboxylic acid. The acid has higher priority, so the molecule is named as an acid: **3-hydroxypropanoic acid**. The –OH appears as a prefix, not as the suffix -ol.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Common slip">

Writing C₄H₁₀O and assuming it must be an alcohol. It can also be an ether: CH₃OCH₂CH₂CH₃ has the same formula. Always use DoU and the functional-group count together, not the formula alone.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Give the molecular formula of a saturated acyclic hydrocarbon with eight carbons.
2. How many degrees of unsaturation does C₇H₈ have, and what does toluene's structure use them for?
3. Identify the functional groups in CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO.
4. Write the next two members of the homologous series CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
5. Convert CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ into a bond-line description and name the longest chain length.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. C₈H₁₈.
2. $(14+2-8)/2=4$: one ring plus three π bonds, as in toluene.
3. One alcohol (–OH) and one aldehyde (–CHO); the aldehyde is principal for naming.
4. C₅H₈ and C₆H₁₀ (for example penta-1,3-diyne and hexa-1,3-diyne, though many isomers exist).
5. A five-carbon chain with a methyl branch on carbon 3; the parent chain has five carbons, so the skeleton is 3-methylpentane.

</details>

## Take away

Carbon's four bonds, self-linking and multiple bonding generate the whole library of organic compounds. Before naming or reacting anything, establish the skeleton, count the degrees of unsaturation, and identify the functional groups. Everything later in this chapter builds on those three steps.
