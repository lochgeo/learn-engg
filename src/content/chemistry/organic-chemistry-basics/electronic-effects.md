## Why organic reactions happen where they do

Most organic reactions begin with electron movement. A bond breaks in one of two ways, giving a small set of reactive intermediates; the surrounding groups then push or pull electron density through the molecule. If you can rank those effects, you can predict the major product instead of memorising each reaction separately.

## 1. How bonds break

**Homolytic cleavage** — each atom takes one electron:

$$
\mathrm{A{-}B \rightarrow A^{\bullet}+B^{\bullet}}
$$

This produces **free radicals** and is favoured by heat or ultraviolet light in non-polar media.

**Heterolytic cleavage** — one atom takes both electrons:

$$
\mathrm{A{-}B \rightarrow A^{+}+B^{-}}
$$

This produces **carbocations** and **carbanions** and is favoured in polar solvents.

## 2. The three key intermediates

**Carbocation** — carbon with three bonds and a positive charge. It is $sp^2$, planar, and has an empty p orbital, so it is electron-poor (a Lewis acid). Stability:

$$
3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{CH_3^+}
$$

Allylic and benzylic carbocations are extra-stable because the charge is delocalised by resonance. A carbocation can **rearrange** by a hydride or methyl shift if that produces a more stable cation.

**Carbanion** — carbon with three bonds, a lone pair and a negative charge. It is roughly $sp^3$ and pyramidal. Stability runs the other way:

$$
1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ
$$

because alkyl groups donate electron density and intensify the negative charge.

**Free radical** — an atom with an unpaired electron. It is $sp^2$-like and planar; stability follows $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ$.

## 3. Inductive effect

The inductive effect is **permanent** polarisation of a σ bond caused by a difference in electronegativity. It fades rapidly with distance — usually negligible beyond three bonds.

- **−I groups** withdraw electrons: –NO₂, –CN, –COOH, –CHO, –CO–, –SO₃H, –X, –OH, –OR, –NH₂.
- **+I groups** release electrons: alkyl groups, especially tert-butyl.

Consequences you must be able to predict:

- **Acidity of carboxylic acids:** ClCH₂COOH is stronger than CH₃COOH because the −I chlorine stabilises the carboxylate ion. Cl₃CCOOH is stronger still.
- **Basicity of amines:** alkyl groups increase electron density on nitrogen, so alkylamines are more basic than ammonia in water; aniline is much less basic because its lone pair is delocalised into the ring.

## 4. Electromeric effect

The electromeric effect is **temporary**: π electrons shift completely toward one atom when a reagent attacks, and the original arrangement returns when the reagent leaves.

- **+E** — electrons move toward the attacking group.
- **−E** — electrons move away from it.

For example, when H⁺ attacks ethene, the π electrons move to form the new C–H bond and leave a carbocation at the other carbon.

## 5. Resonance (mesomeric) effect

When π electrons can be delocalised over three or more atoms, the molecule is better described by several resonance structures. The real molecule is a hybrid, lower in energy than any single structure.

- **+M groups** donate electrons into the conjugated system: –OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂, –NHCOCH₃. Halogens are +M but also strongly −I, so their net effect on a benzene ring is deactivating.
- **−M groups** withdraw electrons: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –CO–, –COOH, –COOR, –SO₃H.

Resonance explains why benzene is unusually stable, why phenol is acidic, and why aniline's lone pair is not available for protonation.

## 6. Hyperconjugation

Hyperconjugation is the delocalisation of σ electrons — usually from a C–H bond next to an empty or partly empty p orbital — into that orbital. It is sometimes drawn as “no-bond resonance”.

The more **α-hydrogens** (hydrogens on the carbon next to the unsaturated centre), the greater the stabilising effect.

- **Alkene stability:** more substituted alkenes are more stable. 2-methylbut-2-ene has nine α-hydrogens; but-1-ene has two, so the substituted alkene is much more stable.
- **Carbocation stability:** a tertiary carbocation has more adjacent C–H bonds to donate.
- **Saytzeff rule:** in elimination, the more substituted alkene usually predominates because it is more stable.

## 7. Electrophiles, nucleophiles and reaction types

An **electrophile** is electron-poor and accepts an electron pair: H⁺, NO₂⁺, Br⁺, carbocations, carbonyl carbons.

A **nucleophile** is electron-rich and donates an electron pair: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O, alkoxide ions, and the π electrons of alkenes and arenes.

| Reaction type | What happens | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substitution | one group replaces another | Cl₂ + CH₄ → CH₃Cl + HCl |
| Addition | two groups add across a π bond | CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| Elimination | a small molecule is removed, forming a π bond | CH₃CH₂Br → CH₂=CH₂ + HBr |
| Rearrangement | atoms move to give a more stable intermediate | hydride shift in a carbocation |

Nucleophilic substitution at a saturated carbon can proceed through a two-step $S_N1$ route (carbocation intermediate) or a one-step $S_N2$ route (backside attack). The $S_N1$ rate follows carbocation stability; $S_N2$ is sensitive to crowding.

<learning-block type="concept" title="Push versus pull">

Inductive and resonance effects are permanent. Electromeric and hyperconjugative effects act through a reaction pathway but still help decide the product. When effects conflict, resonance usually dominates for conjugated systems; induction matters most when no conjugation is possible.

</learning-block>

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1: rank the carbocations

(CH₃)₃C⁺ > (CH₃)₂CH⁺ > CH₃CH₂⁺ > CH₃⁺

More alkyl groups and more α C–H bonds stabilise the empty p orbital.

### Example 2: which alkene is more stable?

2-methylbut-2-ene has nine α-hydrogens; but-1-ene has two. Hyperconjugation makes the more substituted alkene more stable.

### Example 3: which acid is stronger?

ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH. Chlorine's −I effect disperses the negative charge on the carboxylate, lowering the energy of the conjugate base.

### Example 4: why is aniline a weak base?

The nitrogen lone pair participates in the aromatic π system by resonance, so it is less available to accept a proton. Protonating aniline would destroy that delocalisation.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Three common confusions">

- **Inductive effect is not resonance.** Induction works through σ bonds and dies with distance; resonance works through parallel p orbitals and can act across many atoms.
- **More stable carbocation does not mean more stable carbanion.** Their stability orders are opposite.
- **Alkyl groups are +I, not +M.** For benzene substituents, alkyl groups are weakly activating and ortho/para directing through hyperconjugation; they do not donate by resonance.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. Rank by stability: CH₃CH₂⁺, (CH₃)₃C⁺, (CH₃)₂CH⁺.
2. Which is more acidic: CH₃COOH or CF₃COOH? Why?
3. Identify the electrophile and nucleophile in CH₃Br + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Br⁻.
4. Why is phenol more acidic than ethanol?
5. Draw the resonance structures of the benzyl cation and explain its stability.
6. How many α-hydrogens does propene have? Compare it with 2-methylpropene and state which is more stable.
7. Classify each group as +I, −I, +M or −M: –OH, –NO₂, –CH₃, –CN.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. (CH₃)₃C⁺ > (CH₃)₂CH⁺ > CH₃CH₂⁺.
2. CF₃COOH. The three fluorine atoms exert a strong −I effect that stabilises the carboxylate ion.
3. Electrophile: CH₃Br (the carbon bearing the leaving group); nucleophile: OH⁻.
4. The phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance with the ring; ethoxide has no such delocalisation.
5. The positive charge is delocalised into the ring at the ortho and para positions, giving three contributing structures; this makes the benzyl cation more stable than a simple primary carbocation.
6. Propene has three α-hydrogens; 2-methylpropene has six. 2-methylpropene is more stable.
7. –OH: −I and +M; –NO₂: −I and −M; –CH₃: +I and hyperconjugation; –CN: −I and −M.

</details>

## Take away

Bond cleavage creates radicals or ions. Inductive and resonance effects decide where electron density sits; hyperconjugation and the electromeric shift decide how intermediates stabilise. Rank the intermediates correctly and most product questions answer themselves.
