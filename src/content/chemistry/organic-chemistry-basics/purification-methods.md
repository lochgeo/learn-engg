## Why purification is a real skill

A reaction rarely gives a clean product. Unreacted starting material, solvents, isomers and inorganic salts all travel with it. Before you can identify a compound or measure its properties, you have to isolate it. Purification also gives you a practical test of identity: a pure solid melts sharply, and a pure liquid boils at a constant temperature.

## 1. Choosing a method

| Mixture | Best method | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| solid + non-volatile impurity | crystallisation | the pure solid reforms slowly from a hot solution |
| solid that vaporises on heating | sublimation | the solid sublimes while the impurity does not |
| liquids with boiling points far apart | simple distillation | the more volatile liquid distils first |
| liquids with close boiling points | fractional distillation | repeated vaporisation–condensation separates them |
| steam-volatile, water-insoluble organic liquid | steam distillation | the mixture boils below 100 °C and the oil distils with steam |
| liquid that decomposes below its boiling point | vacuum distillation | lowering the pressure lowers the boiling point |
| solute distributed between two immiscible solvents | differential extraction | the solute concentrates in the better solvent |
| mixture of coloured or UV-visible compounds | chromatography | components move at different rates on a stationary phase |

## 2. Crystallisation

The impure solid is dissolved in the **minimum** volume of a hot solvent, filtered while hot to remove insoluble material, then cooled slowly. The compound crystallises while most impurities stay in solution. The crystals are filtered and dried.

The solvent is chosen so that the compound dissolves easily when hot but only sparingly when cold, and so that impurities are either insoluble when hot or very soluble when cold. Typical examples: sugar, benzoic acid, urea.

Slow cooling gives larger, purer crystals; rapid cooling traps impurities and gives a fine powder.

## 3. Sublimation

Some solids pass directly to vapour on heating and re-form as solid on a cold surface. This separates them from non-sublimable impurities in one step.

Common sublimes: camphor, naphthalene, anthracene, iodine, ammonium chloride. A mixture such as camphor and sodium chloride is separated by gentle heating: camphor sublimes and collects on the cool surface, leaving the salt behind.

## 4. Distillation

**Simple distillation** is used when the components differ in boiling point by roughly 25 K or more, or when the impurity is non-volatile. The vapour of the lower-boiling component is condensed and collected. It cannot cleanly separate liquids with similar boiling points.

**Fractional distillation** uses a fractionating column. Vapour rises, condenses, re-vaporises repeatedly, and each cycle enriches the vapour in the more volatile component. This is how petroleum is separated into fractions and how ethanol–water mixtures are concentrated.

**Steam distillation** is used for compounds that are steam-volatile and immiscible with water. Because the mixture boils when the sum of the two vapour pressures reaches atmospheric pressure, the organic compound distils below 100 °C. Aniline and many essential oils are isolated this way.

**Vacuum (reduced-pressure) distillation** is used for liquids that would decompose at their normal boiling point. Reducing the external pressure lowers the boiling temperature, so glycerol and high-boiling petroleum fractions can be distilled safely.

## 5. Differential extraction

A solute distributes itself between two immiscible solvents. If it is much more soluble in the second solvent, repeated small extractions with the second solvent remove it efficiently from the first.

For example, benzoic acid can be extracted from an aqueous solution into ether. Adding an acid or base can also change a compound's solubility: benzoic acid becomes water-soluble as sodium benzoate in base, and returns to the ether layer on acidification.

## 6. Chromatography

Chromatography separates components by their different rates of movement between a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**.

- **Adsorption chromatography** — the stationary phase is a solid such as silica gel or alumina; components adsorb to different extents. Column chromatography and thin-layer chromatography (TLC) work this way.
- **Partition chromatography** — the stationary phase is a liquid held on a solid support; components partition differently between the two liquid phases. Paper chromatography is the classic example.

In TLC, spots are seen under ultraviolet light or after spraying with a locating agent. The **retardation factor** is

$$
R_f=\frac{\text{distance travelled by the spot}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}.
$$

$R_f$ is a fraction between 0 and 1 and is characteristic of a compound under fixed conditions. It is used to identify compounds and to check whether a reaction has gone to completion.

<learning-block type="example" title="Calculating Rf">

A spot moves 4.5 cm while the solvent front moves 9.0 cm.

$$R_f=4.5/9.0=0.50.$$

A second spot moves 2.0 cm, giving $R_f=0.22$. The larger $R_f$ belongs to the less strongly adsorbed compound.

</learning-block>

## 7. Drying and decolourising

After extraction or distillation, traces of water are removed with a drying agent such as anhydrous calcium chloride or magnesium sulphate. Coloured impurities can be removed by boiling with activated charcoal and filtering. Neither step purifies by itself; both support the main method.

<learning-block type="mistake" title="Choosing the wrong method">

- **Crystallising a compound that decomposes before melting.** Use sublimation or a different solvent.
- **Simple-distilling two liquids with similar boiling points.** The distillate will be a mixture; use a fractionating column.
- **Steam-distilling a water-soluble compound.** It will not separate cleanly into an oil layer.
- **Reporting an $R_f$ without the solvent system.** $R_f$ depends on the stationary phase, mobile phase and temperature.

</learning-block>

## Practice

1. How would you separate a mixture of camphor and common salt?
2. Why is vacuum distillation used for glycerol?
3. A mixture of two liquids boils at 350 K and 352 K. Which distillation method is appropriate?
4. In a TLC experiment, a spot moves 3.2 cm and the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Calculate $R_f$.
5. Why is steam distillation suitable for aniline but not for ethanol?
6. Which technique would you use to separate a mixture of two dyes that are both soluble in ethanol?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Sublimation: camphor sublimes and is collected; salt remains.
2. Glycerol has a high boiling point and decomposes near it, so the pressure is reduced to lower the boiling temperature.
3. Fractional distillation, because the boiling points are only 2 K apart.
4. $R_f=3.2/8.0=0.40$.
5. Aniline is steam-volatile and immiscible with water; ethanol is completely miscible with water, so it would not separate as an oil.
6. Column chromatography or TLC on a suitable stationary phase.

</details>

## Take away

Match the physical difference between components to the method: volatility for distillation, solubility for crystallisation and extraction, adsorption or partition for chromatography. Purity is then confirmed by a sharp melting point or a constant boiling point.
