## The big idea

Temperature describes thermal state; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. A thermometer reads temperature, while a calorimetry calculation tracks energy exchanged.

## Start here

When a substance warms without changing phase, the energy change is $Q=mc\Delta T$, where $c$ is specific heat capacity. During a phase change, temperature can stay constant while energy changes the arrangement of particles: $Q=mL$. Thermal expansion describes size changes with temperature; for small changes in a solid, $\Delta L=\alpha L\Delta T$.

Heat travels by conduction through material interactions, convection through bulk fluid motion, and radiation by electromagnetic waves. A vacuum blocks conduction and convection but not radiation. Energy conservation in an insulated mixture gives heat lost by warmer parts equal to heat gained by cooler parts.

## Worked example

How much energy warms $0.5\ \text{kg}$ of water by $10^\circ\text{C}$? Using $c=4200\ \text{J/(kg·K)}$:

$$
Q=mc\Delta T=(0.5)(4200)(10)=21{,}000\ \text{J}.
$$

A temperature interval of $10^\circ\text{C}$ equals $10\ \text{K}$.

## Try it yourself

**How much energy melts $0.20\ \text{kg}$ of ice at its melting point if its latent heat of fusion is $3.34\times10^5\ \text{J/kg}$?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Use $Q=mL=(0.20)(3.34\times10^5)=6.68\times10^4\ \text{J}$. During melting, the temperature remains at the melting point.
</details>

## Remember

- Heat is transferred energy, not a substance stored inside an object.
- Use specific heat for temperature change and latent heat for phase change.
- Conduction, convection, and radiation transfer energy in different ways.
