## The big idea

Fluids flow and transmit pressure. Their motion can be understood by tracking how pressure, speed, and height trade off along a streamline.

## Start here

Pressure is force per area, $p=F/A$. In a fluid at rest, pressure increases with depth: $p=p_0+\rho gh$. Buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid; an object floats when the fluid’s upward force can balance its weight.

For steady incompressible flow through a tube, volume flow rate is conserved: $A_1v_1=A_2v_2$. Bernoulli’s equation along a streamline for an ideal, non-viscous, incompressible fluid is $p+\frac12\rho v^2+\rho gh=\text{constant}$. Greater flow speed corresponds to lower static pressure at the same height in this model. Real fluids also have viscosity, which creates internal resistance and dissipates energy.

Surface tension arises from cohesive forces at a liquid surface. It helps explain droplets and capillary rise, while viscosity describes resistance to relative motion between fluid layers.

## Worked example

At $2\ \text{m}$ below the surface of water, the gauge pressure is $\rho gh$. With $\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and $g=9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, this is $19{,}600\ \text{Pa}$, or about $19.6\ \text{kPa}$ above atmospheric pressure.

## Try it yourself

**A pipe narrows to half its original cross-sectional area. For steady incompressible flow, how does the speed change?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

From $Av=\text{constant}$, halving area doubles speed.
</details>

## Remember

- Fluid pressure at rest depends on depth and density.
- Continuity expresses conservation of flow.
- Bernoulli’s relation has specific ideal-flow assumptions.
