## The big idea

Solids deform when forces act on them. Elasticity describes how a material returns toward its original shape when the load is removed, provided the deformation stayed within its elastic limit.

## Start here

Stress measures applied force per area: $\sigma=F/A$. Longitudinal strain is fractional extension, $\epsilon=\Delta L/L$. Within the proportional region, Young’s modulus is $Y=\sigma/\epsilon$. A large modulus means a material is stiff; it does not by itself mean the material is strong or tough.

For a wire of length $L$ and area $A$, a small extension under force $F$ is $\Delta L=FL/(AY)$. This model assumes uniform material and small elastic strain. A stress–strain curve can show a linear region, an elastic limit, yielding, and eventual fracture. The area under the curve represents energy stored or dissipated per unit volume, depending on the region and loading history.

## Worked example

A $1\ \text{m}$ wire extends $1\ \text{mm}$ under tension. Its strain is $0.001/1=1.0\times10^{-3}$ (dimensionless). Strain has no unit because it is a ratio of lengths.

## Try it yourself

**A rod of area $2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$ carries a $100\ \text{N}$ tensile force. What is the tensile stress?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Stress is $F/A=100/(2\times10^{-4})=5.0\times10^5\ \text{Pa}$.
</details>

## Remember

- Stress has units of pressure; strain is dimensionless.
- Elastic equations apply only within the material’s elastic range.
- Stiffness, strength, and toughness are related but distinct properties.
