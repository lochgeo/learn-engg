## The big idea

Plane motion combines two perpendicular one-dimensional motions. A useful trick is to resolve a vector into components, solve each direction separately, then combine the results.

## Start here

For a vector of magnitude $A$ at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, $A_x=A\cos\theta$ and $A_y=A\sin\theta$. Components can be added like signed numbers; the final magnitude is $A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}$.

In ideal projectile motion, gravity acts vertically while horizontal acceleration is zero. If launch and landing heights are equal, the flight time is $T=2u_y/g$, horizontal range is $R=u_xT$, and the path is a parabola. Air resistance is neglected in this model.

Uniform circular motion has changing velocity even when speed is constant, because direction changes. Its inward acceleration is $a_c=v^2/r=\omega^2r$.

## Worked example

A ball is launched at $20\ \text{m/s}$ at $30^\circ$ above level ground. Taking $g=10\ \text{m/s}^2$, its launch components are $u_x=20\cos30^\circ\approx17.3\ \text{m/s}$ and $u_y=10\ \text{m/s}$. The flight lasts $T=2u_y/g=2\ \text{s}$, so the range is about $34.6\ \text{m}$.

## Try it yourself

**A runner moves around a circular track of radius $5\ \text{m}$ at $4\ \text{m/s}$. What is the centripetal acceleration?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Use $a_c=v^2/r=16/5=3.2\ \text{m/s}^2$, directed toward the centre.
</details>

## Remember

- Keep horizontal and vertical motion separate until the final step.
- Acceleration toward the centre changes direction, not necessarily speed.
- A vector needs both magnitude and direction.
