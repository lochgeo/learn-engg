## The big idea

Every mass attracts every other mass. The force is weak for ordinary objects but dominates at astronomical scales because it acts over long distances and is always attractive.

## Start here

For two point masses, Newton’s law gives $F=Gm_1m_2/r^2$, directed along the line joining them. Outside a spherical body, its gravitational field is the same as if all its mass were concentrated at the centre: $g=GM/r^2$. The field strength decreases with distance squared.

Near a planet’s surface, weight is $mg$. Gravitational potential energy is conventionally zero at infinity, giving $U=-GMm/r$. The negative sign indicates a bound state. For a circular orbit, gravity supplies centripetal force, so $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$ and the orbital period obeys $T^2\propto r^3$. Escape speed from radius $R$ is $v_e=\sqrt{2GM/R}$ when air resistance and rotation are ignored.

## Worked example

At twice the distance from a planet’s centre, field strength becomes $g'=GM/(2r)^2=g/4$. It falls to one quarter, not one half.

## Try it yourself

**If a satellite’s orbital radius becomes four times larger, by what factor does its circular speed change?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Since $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$, multiplying $r$ by four divides $v$ by $\sqrt4=2$. The speed becomes half as large.
</details>

## Remember

- Gravitational force follows an inverse-square law.
- Use distance from the centre in field and orbit equations.
- Circular orbit speed and escape speed are different situations.
