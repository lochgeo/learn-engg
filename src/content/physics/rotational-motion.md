## The big idea

A multi-particle object can translate and rotate at the same time. Its centre of mass tracks the overall translation; torque and angular momentum describe rotation about an axis.

## Start here

For point masses, the centre of mass is $\vec r_{cm}=\sum m_i\vec r_i/\sum m_i$. External forces change the motion of the centre of mass just as if the total mass were concentrated there. Internal forces cannot change the system’s total momentum.

Torque is $\vec\tau=\vec r\times\vec F$; its magnitude is $rF\sin\theta$. For a rigid body about a fixed axis, $\tau=I\alpha$, where $I=\sum m_ir_i^2$ is rotational inertia. Rotational kinetic energy is $K_{rot}=\frac12I\omega^2$. Angular momentum is $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ for a particle and $L=I\omega$ for a rigid body rotating about a principal axis. With negligible external torque, total angular momentum is conserved.

## Worked example

A perpendicular $10\ \text{N}$ force acts at the end of a $0.40\ \text{m}$ lever. The torque magnitude is

$$\tau=rF=(0.40)(10)=4.0\ \text{N·m}.$$

If the same force points along the lever, its torque is zero because its perpendicular moment arm is zero.

## Try it yourself

**A $2\ \text{kg}$ mass lies $0.5\ \text{m}$ from an axis. What is its contribution to rotational inertia?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Use $I=mr^2=2(0.5)^2=0.50\ \text{kg·m}^2$.
</details>

## Remember

- Rotational inertia depends on how mass is distributed around the axis.
- Torque depends on the perpendicular lever arm.
- Angular momentum is conserved when net external torque is zero.
