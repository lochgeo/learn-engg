## The big idea

A wave carries a disturbance and energy from place to place. In a travelling wave, particles of the medium oscillate around equilibrium; they do not travel along with the wave over long distances.

## Start here

In a transverse wave, oscillation is perpendicular to travel; in a longitudinal wave, it is parallel. Wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance between points in the same phase, frequency $f$ is cycles per second, and period $T=1/f$. Wave speed is

$$
v=f\lambda.
$$

When waves overlap, their displacements add (superposition). Interference can reinforce or reduce the resulting displacement. A standing wave forms from two similar waves travelling in opposite directions; nodes remain still and antinodes oscillate with maximum amplitude. For a string fixed at both ends, allowed wavelengths satisfy $L=n\lambda/2$ for positive integer $n$.

Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave in air. Its speed depends on the medium and conditions, not on the loudness. Frequency is associated with pitch; amplitude relates to intensity. A moving source or observer can change the measured frequency through the Doppler effect.

## Worked example

A wave has frequency $250\ \text{Hz}$ and wavelength $1.2\ \text{m}$. Its speed is $v=f\lambda=(250)(1.2)=300\ \text{m/s}$.

## Try it yourself

**A string of length $0.80\ \text{m}$ is fixed at both ends. What is its fundamental wavelength?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

For the fundamental, $n=1$ and $L=\lambda/2$, so $\lambda=2L=1.6\ \text{m}$.
</details>

## Remember

- A travelling wave transfers energy; medium particles oscillate locally.
- Use consistent units in $v=f\lambda$.
- Boundary conditions determine the allowed standing-wave patterns.
