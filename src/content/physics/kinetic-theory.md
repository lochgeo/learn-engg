## The big idea

Kinetic theory links a gas’s large-scale pressure and temperature to the motion of enormous numbers of tiny particles. It explains gas behaviour statistically rather than tracking each molecule.

## Start here

In the ideal-gas model, particles are far apart, have negligible volume, move randomly, and collide elastically. The equation of state is $pV=nRT=Nk_BT$. Pressure results from momentum transferred to container walls. For a monatomic ideal gas, average translational kinetic energy per molecule is

$$\langle K\rangle=\frac32k_BT.$$

Temperature in kelvin is therefore proportional to average random translational kinetic energy. At the same temperature, different ideal gases have the same average translational kinetic energy, though their typical speeds differ because their molecular masses differ. The root-mean-square speed is $v_{rms}=\sqrt{3RT/M}$ for molar mass $M$.

Real molecules interact and occupy space, especially at high pressure or low temperature; under those conditions the ideal model becomes less accurate.

## Worked example

At fixed volume, an ideal gas warms from $300\ \text{K}$ to $450\ \text{K}$. Since $pV=nRT$ with $V$ and $n$ fixed, $p\propto T$, so the pressure becomes $450/300=1.5$ times its original value.

## Try it yourself

**At fixed temperature, the volume of an ideal gas is halved. What happens to its pressure?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

With $nRT$ constant, $pV$ is constant. Halving $V$ doubles $p$ (Boyle’s law).
</details>

## Remember

- Use kelvin in gas-law ratios.
- Temperature tracks average molecular kinetic energy.
- State which variables are held constant before using a gas law.
