## The big idea

Thermodynamics tracks energy moving as heat and work. The first law is energy conservation applied to a system, while the second law limits the direction and usefulness of those transfers.

## Start here

Choose a system boundary and a sign convention. Here, $Q>0$ means heat enters the system and $W>0$ means work is done by the system. The first law is

$$
\Delta U=Q-W.
$$

Internal energy $U$ is a state property. Heat and work describe energy crossing the boundary, so neither belongs to the system as a stored state. In an isochoric process, volume is fixed and boundary work is zero. In an isothermal ideal-gas process, temperature and internal energy remain constant. In an adiabatic process, no heat crosses the boundary ($Q=0$).

A heat engine absorbs heat from a hot source, produces work, and releases some heat to a cooler sink. Its efficiency is $e=W_{out}/Q_{in}$ and cannot reach 100% in a cyclic engine operating between finite-temperature reservoirs. A refrigerator uses work to move heat from cold to warm.

## Worked example

A gas receives $500\ \text{J}$ of heat and does $180\ \text{J}$ of work. Then $\Delta U=Q-W=500-180=320\ \text{J}$. Internal energy rises by $320\ \text{J}$.

## Try it yourself

**In an adiabatic compression, $Q=0$ and the surroundings do $75\ \text{J}$ of work on the gas. What is $\Delta U$?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Work done by the gas is $W=-75\ \text{J}$. Therefore $\Delta U=Q-W=0-(-75)=+75\ \text{J}$.
</details>

## Remember

- State your sign convention before using the first law.
- Heat and work are transfer mechanisms; internal energy is a state property.
- Real cyclic engines must reject some heat to a cooler sink.
