## The big idea

An oscillator repeatedly moves around a stable equilibrium. In simple harmonic motion (SHM), the restoring acceleration points toward equilibrium and is proportional to displacement: $a=-\omega^2x$.

## Start here

One way to describe SHM is $x=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, where amplitude $A$ is the maximum displacement, angular frequency $\omega=2\pi/T$, period $T$, and phase $\phi$ sets the starting point. Differentiating gives velocity and acceleration; when displacement is largest, speed is zero, and at equilibrium speed is greatest.

For an ideal mass–spring system, $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. For a simple pendulum at small angular displacement, $T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. In either ideal case, the period does not depend on amplitude (within the stated approximation). Total mechanical energy swaps between kinetic and potential forms.

## Worked example

An oscillator has period $2\ \text{s}$. Its angular frequency is $\omega=2\pi/T=\pi\ \text{rad/s}$. Its acceleration at displacement $x=0.10\ \text{m}$ is $a=-\omega^2x\approx-0.99\ \text{m/s}^2$, toward equilibrium.

## Try it yourself

**A spring–mass system’s mass is multiplied by four. How does its period change?**

<details><summary>Reveal the explanation</summary>

Since $T\propto\sqrt m$, the period doubles.
</details>

## Remember

- In SHM, acceleration is opposite to displacement.
- Period and frequency are reciprocals; $\omega=2\pi f$.
- Ideal period formulas depend on their modelling assumptions.
